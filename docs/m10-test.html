Section A — examinable content

1. Ana works at a furniture firm and spends her wages at a supermarket. In the simple circular-flow diagram, which description of the flows is correct?

  • (A) Labor flows from Ana's household to the firm and wages from the firm to her household in factor markets; groceries flow from the supermarket to her household and her spending flows to the supermarket in product markets.
  • (B) Labor and wages both flow from Ana's household to the firm, through factor markets.
  • (C) Her wages flow through product markets, because a wage is a price.
  • (D) Her spending flows to the supermarket through factor markets.
  • (E) Money and goods flow in the same direction round the circle, from firms to households.

2. In which of the following transactions is a household the seller?

  • (A) A family buys groceries at a supermarket.
  • (B) A firm buys a delivery van from a van maker.
  • (C) The government buys school supplies.
  • (D) A landowner leases a field to a farming company for $5,000 a year.
  • (E) A restaurant buys fish from a wholesaler.

3. Which pair of transactions is classified correctly?

  • (A) A firm hiring a programmer: product market. A household buying a laptop: factor market.
  • (B) A firm hiring a programmer: product market. A household buying a laptop: product market.
  • (C) A firm hiring a programmer: factor market. A household buying a laptop: product market.
  • (D) Both in product markets, because money changes hands in each.
  • (E) Both in factor markets, because a firm is involved in each.

4. A household earns $60,000 in wages, interest and rent, receives $4,000 in government transfers and pays $12,000 in taxes. It spends $45,000 on goods and services.

What are its disposable income and its private savings?

  • (A) Disposable income $64,000; savings $19,000.
  • (B) Disposable income $48,000; savings $3,000.
  • (C) Disposable income $76,000; savings $31,000.
  • (D) Disposable income $52,000; savings $45,000.
  • (E) Disposable income $52,000; savings $7,000.

5. In a simple economy, money flowing into households is $900 of wages, profit, interest and rent plus $100 of government transfers. Money flowing out is $150 of taxes and $700 of consumer spending, and there is one other outflow.

What must that outflow be, and where does it go?

  • (A) $50 of savings, because transfers are not income.
  • (B) $150 of private savings, which flow into financial markets and on to firms, the government or borrowers abroad.
  • (C) $300 of savings, because taxes come back to households as transfers.
  • (D) $150 of private savings, which leave the circular flow permanently.
  • (E) Nothing — what flows into households must equal consumer spending.

6. A dairy sells milk to a family and to a café that uses it in the lattes it sells.

Which statement is correct?

  • (A) Both purchases are final, because the milk is not physically transformed.
  • (B) The café's purchase is final, because the café is a business customer.
  • (C) Both purchases are intermediate, because milk is always an input.
  • (D) The family's purchase is final; the café's is intermediate, because its value is counted in the price of the lattes.
  • (E) Neither purchase is in GDP, because milk is a raw material.

7. In one year, Toyota's plant in Kentucky produces $3 billion of cars and Ford's plant in Mexico produces $2 billion of cars.

What do these two plants add to U.S. GDP?

  • (A) $3 billion — only the Kentucky production.
  • (B) $2 billion — only Ford's production, because Ford is an American company.
  • (C) $5 billion — both, because both involve major U.S. car markets.
  • (D) $1 billion — the Kentucky output net of the Mexican output.
  • (E) Nothing, because car production is counted as exports.

8. This year, a house built in 1995 is resold for $300,000 and the estate agent earns a $15,000 commission on the sale. A newly built house is also completed and sold for $400,000.

By how much do these transactions raise this year's GDP?

  • (A) $715,000
  • (B) $700,000
  • (C) $400,000
  • (D) $15,000
  • (E) $415,000

9. A cotton farmer sells cotton to a mill for $50. The mill sells the cloth to a tailor for $120. The tailor sells shirts to consumers for $300.

Using the value-added approach, what do these transactions contribute to GDP, and what is the mill's value added?

  • (A) GDP $470; the mill's value added $120.
  • (B) GDP $300; the mill's value added $70.
  • (C) GDP $300; the mill's value added $120.
  • (D) GDP $470; the mill's value added $70.
  • (E) GDP $180; the mill's value added $70.

10. Using the textbook's three-firm economy, a student adds the sale price of the car ($21,500) and the price of a car's worth of steel ($9,000) and reports total value added of $30,500.

What is wrong with the calculation?

  • (A) Nothing; both the car and the steel were produced this year.
  • (B) The student should also add the $4,200 of iron ore, giving $34,700.
  • (C) The steel is counted twice — once when it is sold and again inside the car. The car maker's value added is $12,500, and the economy's total is $21,500.
  • (D) Value added should be calculated after subtracting wages, which the student forgot.
  • (E) Value added cannot be summed; only the income approach gives GDP.

11. Which of the following is counted as consumer spending in GDP?

How does the purchase appear in the U.S. expenditures calculation?

  • (C) Consumer spending and imports both rise by $1,200, so GDP is unchanged.

17. A car maker buys $2 million of steel for this year's cars and a $2 million stamping machine that will last ten years.

Why is the machine counted as investment spending in GDP and the steel is not?

  • (A) The steel is imported and the machine is domestic.
  • (B) The machine is more expensive per unit than the steel.
  • (C) The steel is counted as investment spending and the machine as consumer spending.
  • (D) The steel is an intermediate good used up in the cars, so its value is already in the price of the cars; the machine is a final good that will last for years.
  • (E) Neither is counted, because both are business purchases.

18. An economy's factor payments for a year are wages $700 billion, interest $80 billion, rent $50 billion and profit $170 billion. Government transfers are $90 billion.

19. A statistician finds that an economy's firms produced $2,000 billion of final goods, measured by value added, but that sales to final buyers were only $1,800 billion.

What reconciles the two figures?

  • (A) The $200 billion of unsold goods are additions to inventories, which count as investment spending, so spending is $2,000 billion too.
  • (B) Value added overstates GDP; the true figure is $1,800 billion.
  • (C) The difference is imports, which must be subtracted from value added.
  • (D) The approaches measure different things and need not agree.
  • (E) Factor income must equal sales, so the value-added figure is wrong.

Which statement is correct?

  • (C) Nominal GDP doubled from $200 to $400, but the economy's output did not change.
  • (D) Nominal GDP rose from $200 to $300.
  • (E) Nominal GDP cannot be calculated without choosing a base year.

Solutions

Question 1 — Circular-flow diagram · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Real things and money move in opposite directions in both markets. In factor markets labor goes household → firm and wages go firm → household; in product markets goods go firm → household and spending goes household → firm. That opposition is what makes the diagram show GDP as "a total flow of income and expenditure" (MEA-1.A.2).

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Labor and its payment cannot travel the same way; one is sold for the other.
  • (C) ✗ A wage is the price of a factor, so it is paid in a factor market.
  • (D) ✗ Spending on groceries is paid in a product market.
  • (E) ✗ The defining feature of the diagram is that the two flows run opposite ways.

Question 2 — Households and firms · Application

Answer: (D)

Households own the factors of production and sell their use to firms. Leasing land for rent is a household selling a factor in a factor market.

  • (A) ✗ The family is the buyer, in a product market.
  • (B) ✗ Firm to firm; no household involved.
  • (C) ✗ The government is the buyer.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Firm to firm — and an intermediate purchase.

Question 3 — Product and factor markets · Analysis

Answer: (C)

Hiring labor is buying a factor of production, so it happens in a factor market. A household buying a laptop is buying a good, in a product market.

  • (A) ✗ Exactly reversed.
  • (B) ✗ Right about the laptop, wrong about the programmer: labor is a factor.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Money changes hands in both kinds of market; what is bought decides which one it is.
  • (E) ✗ Firms appear in both markets — as sellers in one, buyers in the other.

Question 4 — Expanded circular flow · Application

Answer: (E)

Disposable income = income + transfers − taxes = 60,000 + 4,000 − 12,000 = $52,000. Private savings = disposable income − consumer spending = 52,000 − 45,000 = $7,000.

  • (A) ✗ Forgets to subtract taxes: 64,000, and 64,000 − 45,000 = 19,000.
  • (B) ✗ Forgets to add transfers: 48,000, and 48,000 − 45,000 = 3,000.
  • (C) ✗ Adds taxes instead of subtracting them.
  • (D) ✗ The right disposable income, but savings is what is not spent, not what is spent.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 5 — Expanded circular flow · Synthesis

Answer: (B)

What flows into a box must flow out. In: 900 + 100 = 1,000. Out so far: 150 + 700 = 850. So the remaining outflow is $150, and in the expanded diagram that is private savings, which go into financial markets and on to firms, the government and borrowers abroad.

  • (A) ✗ Transfers are an inflow to households, so they must be counted: 1,000 − 850, not 900 − 850.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Taxes are an outflow whatever happens to them afterwards; 1,000 − 700 ignores them.
  • (D) ✗ Right number, wrong mechanism. Savings return to the flow through financial markets — as investment spending or government borrowing.
  • (E) ✗ Taxes and savings are outflows too; the rule is total in equals total out.

Question 6 — Final and intermediate goods · Analysis

Answer: (D)

Whether a good is final depends on who buys it and why. The family is the end user. The café buys milk as an input, and its value will be counted inside the price of the lattes.

  • (A) ✗ Transformation is not the test; use is.
  • (B) ✗ Being a business makes the café's purchase intermediate, not final.
  • (C) ✗ The family's milk is final — nobody else buys it after them.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ The family's milk is a final good produced this year and is in GDP.

Question 7 — GDP · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Gross domestic product counts production within the country's borders, whoever owns the firm. Kentucky is in the United States; Mexico is not.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Ownership is the wrong test. Ford's Mexican output is Mexican GDP.
  • (C) ✗ Adds production that happened abroad.
  • (D) ✗ There is no netting of one plant against another.
  • (E) ✗ Neither is described as sold abroad, and exports are part of GDP anyway.

Question 8 — GDP · Synthesis

Answer: (E)

Two rules together. The 1995 house was counted when it was built, so its resale adds nothing — but the agent's commission is a service produced this year and adds $15,000. The new house is new residential construction, counted as investment spending: $400,000. Total $415,000.

  • (A) ✗ Adds the used house and the commission and the new house.
  • (B) ✗ Counts the used house; misses that the commission is new production.
  • (C) ✗ Right about both houses, but misses the agent's service.
  • (D) ✗ Right about the resale, but leaves out the new house, which is investment spending.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 9 — Value added · Application

Answer: (B)

The value-added approach sums each producer's sales minus its purchases of inputs: farmer 50, mill 120 − 50 = 70, tailor 300 − 120 = 180. Total 50 + 70 + 180 = 300, the value of the final shirts.

  • (A) ✗ Sums all sales (50 + 120 + 300 = 470) and gives the mill's sales, not its value added.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Right GDP, but $120 is the mill's sales; its value added subtracts the $50 of cotton.
  • (D) ✗ Right value added for the mill, but GDP by summed sales double-counts.
  • (E) ✗ $180 is the tailor's value added alone, not GDP.

Question 10 — Value added · Analysis

Answer: (C)

This is Krugman's Check Your Understanding 3. Adding the car and the steel counts the steel twice. The car maker's value added is 21,500 − 9,000 = 12,500, and the three firms' value added sums to 4,200 + 4,800 + 12,500 = 21,500.

  • (A) ✗ Both were produced this year, but the steel's value is inside the car's price.
  • (B) ✗ Makes the error worse: $34,700 is total sales, which counts the ore three times.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Wages are part of value added, not a deduction from it.
  • (E) ✗ Value added sums to GDP; that is the value-added approach.

Question 11 — Consumer spending · Analysis

Answer: (E)

Rent is household spending on a service — housing — and is consumer spending. The other four are investment spending, a transfer or a financial transaction.

  • (A) ✗ A newly built house is investment spending, not consumer spending.
  • (B) ✗ A transfer: nothing is produced in exchange.
  • (C) ✗ Shares are a financial asset, not in GDP.
  • (D) ✗ A landlord is a business buying physical capital: investment spending.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 12 — Investment and inventories · Application · the College Board's own sample question

Answer: (B)

The published answer key (CED Exam Information p. 140) gives B, Skill 1.A, Learning Objective MEA-1.A, Unit 2. Changes in inventories are part of investment spending, so they are in GDP.

  • (A) ✗ Financial assets are claims, not production — Module 10's own "what's out" list.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ A limitation of GDP (Topic 2.2): quality improvements are not fully captured.
  • (D) ✗ Unreported transactions are not recorded — another Topic 2.2 limitation.
  • (E) ✗ Nonmarket activities are never sold, so never recorded — the limitation MEA-1.B.1 names.

Question 13 — Investment and inventories · Synthesis

Answer: (D)

GDP measures production. Year 1: 1,000 bicycles × $200 = $200,000 — $160,000 sold and $40,000 added to inventories as investment spending. Year 2: the $40,000 sale is offset by a $40,000 fall in inventories, so the bicycles add $0.

  • (A) ✗ Counts sales, not production, in both years.
  • (B) ✗ Counts the 200 bicycles twice — produced in year 1 and sold in year 2.
  • (C) ✗ Reverses the years.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Misses the addition to inventories in year 1.

Question 14 — Government purchases and transfers · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Roads (80) and teachers' services (40) are goods and services the government buys: G = $120 million. Unemployment benefits are transfers.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Includes the transfers.
  • (C) ✗ Counts only goods. Teachers' services are services the government buys.
  • (D) ✗ The transfers are the one item that is not G.
  • (E) ✗ Roads plus transfers — the wrong pair.

Question 15 — Exports, imports and net exports · Analysis

Answer: (C)

The purchase is consumer spending, so C rises by $1,200. But the scooter was produced in Italy, so imports rise by $1,200 and are subtracted. GDP is unchanged — imports are subtracted to remove spending on foreign production, not to penalise it.

  • (A) ✗ Forgets the subtraction for imports.
  • (B) ✗ Forgets that the purchase was first counted in C.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Exports are goods produced here and sold abroad.
  • (E) ✗ It does appear, twice, and the two entries cancel.

Question 16 — Expenditures approach · Application

Answer: (E)

GDP = C + I + G + (X − IM) = 1,200 + 300 + 400 + (150 − 250) = $1,800 billion. Transfers are not part of G and are left out.

  • (A) ✗ Adds imports instead of subtracting them: 1,200 + 300 + 400 + 150 + 250 = 2,300.
  • (B) ✗ Adds the $200 billion of transfers.
  • (C) ✗ C + I + G only, ignoring net exports.
  • (D) ✗ Subtracts imports but forgets to add exports.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 17 — Expenditures approach · Analysis

Answer: (D)

Only spending on final goods enters the expenditures approach. Steel is used up in this year's cars, so it is intermediate and its value is already in the price of the cars. A machine that lasts for years is physical capital, a final good, and is investment spending.

  • (A) ✗ Nothing in the stem says where either was made.
  • (B) ✗ Price is irrelevant to the classification.
  • (C) ✗ Neither is consumer spending, and the steel is not counted at all.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Business purchases of physical capital are exactly what investment spending is.

Question 18 — Income approach · Application

Answer: (B)

GDP = wages + interest + rent + profit = 700 + 80 + 50 + 170 = $1,000 billion. Transfers are not earned by supplying a factor, so they are excluded.

  • (A) ✗ Adds the $90 billion of transfers.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Leaves out profit.
  • (D) ✗ Leaves out interest and rent: 700 + 170.
  • (E) ✗ Leaves out rent: 700 + 80 + 170 = 950.

Question 19 — Why the approaches agree · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

Krugman's Check Your Understanding 1: the approaches agree because "every final good and service produced in the economy is either purchased by someone or added to inventories, and additions to inventories are counted as spending by firms". Spending is 1,800 + 200 = $2,000 billion.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ GDP measures production, and $2,000 billion was produced.
  • (C) ✗ Nothing in the stem involves foreign trade.
  • (D) ✗ Done correctly, the three approaches always give the same total.
  • (E) ✗ Firms' revenue includes the value of goods added to inventory; factor income matches production, not sales.

Question 20 — What is in GDP · Analysis

Answer: (D)

A new warehouse is a new structure built in the United States: investment spending, in GDP. Each of the others is out for one of the reasons on the "what's out" list.

  • (A) ✗ A financial asset.
  • (B) ✗ A used good.
  • (C) ✗ A transfer.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ An import — produced abroad.

Question 21 — Nominal GDP · Synthesis

Answer: (C)

Nominal GDP values output at current prices. Year 1: 100 × $1 + 50 × $2 = $200. Year 2: 100 × $2 + 50 × $4 = $400. It doubled, but the quantities — the output — are exactly the same. A rise in nominal GDP alone cannot tell you output rose; that needs real GDP (Module 11).

  • (A) ✗ Right calculation, wrong inference. Prices, not quantities, doubled.
  • (B) ✗ Nominal GDP is price × quantity; the prices changed.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ An arithmetic slip — only one good's price doubled in this total.
  • (E) ✗ A base year is needed for real GDP. Nominal GDP uses each year's own prices.

Question 22 — National income and product accounts · Analysis · background

Answer: (B)

Krugman's definition (p. 102): the national accounts "keep track of the flows of money between different sectors of the economy". GDP is their headline figure.

  • (A) ✗ The accounts record flows over a period, not a stock of wealth.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Taxes and transfers are only two of the flows they record.
  • (D) ✗ They record money flows, not satisfaction.
  • (E) ✗ Trade is one part; consumer spending, investment and government purchases are recorded too.

Notes for the teacher

Content validity. All 17 concept entries appear across the 22 items. The weight goes where the framework puts it: the "final output" statement, MEA-1.A.1, carries seven items because every in-or-out decision is an application of it, and the three approaches of MEA-1.A.3 carry four, each using the framework's name for the approach rather than Krugman's description.

The calculation is tested nine times. Questions 4, 5, 8, 9, 13, 14, 16, 18 and 21 need arithmetic, which is proportionate to a learning objective whose second verb is calculate. Every numeric distractor is a specific wrong operation, named in its solution.

Bloom levels. Application 6 · Analysis 11 · Synthesis 5, a ratio of 1 : 1.83 : 0.83 against the standard's 1 : 3 : 1.75. The suggested skill for Topic 2.1 is 1.A, describe, and the CED's own sample item is a one-step classification; this paper pushes past that, but analysis is still under-weighted because the topic's hardest thinking is classification rather than chains of reasoning. The five synthesis items are the only places where two rules must be combined — a used house with a new commission (8), an inventory across two years (13), the approaches reconciled (19), the household box balanced (5), nominal GDP against output (21).

Question 12 is not mine. It is reproduced verbatim from the CED's sample multiple-choice questions (Exam Information p. 135), and its solution cites the published answer key (p. 140). Three of its four distractors — product quality, the underground economy, nonmarket activities — are Topic 2.2 limitations that Module 10 does not teach. A class that has done only Module 10 can still answer it, because the key is Module 10's own, but expect them to be unsure why the distractors are wrong.

Right answer, wrong reasoning. Two options reach a defensible conclusion by a route that would fail on a written answer, and each is called out in its solution: 5D (the right figure, but savings do not leave the flow) and 21A (the right calculation, the wrong inference).

Distractor design. Every wrong option encodes a documented error. The recurring ones: counting total sales or intermediate goods (9A, 10B, 13B); counting transfers as spending or income (14B, 16B, 18A); adding imports or forgetting exports (16A, 16D); confusing ownership with location (7B); counting used goods or financial assets (8A, 8B, 20A, 20B); forgetting inventories (13A, 13E); and reading a price change as an output change (21A).

Answer distribution. A 4 · B 5 · C 4 · D 5 · E 4. Question 12 keeps its published order, with its key at B. No two consecutive items share a key: the longest run is one.

Timing. 45 minutes for 22 items; the nine arithmetic items take longest, but none needs more than four operations.
