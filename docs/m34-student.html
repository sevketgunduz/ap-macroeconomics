The Phillips Curve

Fifteen terms from Module 34 of Krugman’s Economics for AP: the short-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment, why it disappears in the long run, and what shifts each curve — with the words your exam uses and your textbook does not: demand shocks, long-run equilibrium, and the inflationary and recessionary gaps.

One question decides most Phillips-curve answers: along, or shift? Changes in spending move the economy along the short-run curve. Supply shocks and expected inflation shift the short-run curve. Only a change in the natural rate shifts the long-run curve.

The 15 terms

01

Short-run Phillips curve

★ Exam
Definition

The negative short-run relationship between the unemployment rate and the inflation rate: a downward-sloping curve, the SRPC.

Textbook page 331, margin, and Figure 34.2.

Low unemployment comes with high inflation, and high unemployment with low inflation. When spending is strong, firms hire more and raise prices faster; when it is weak, they hire less and raise prices more slowly.

Draw it with the inflation rate on the vertical axis and the unemployment rate on the horizontal. Both axes are rates, not levels.

Watch out. The axes are not the price level and real GDP — those belong to the aggregate demand and supply graph. A Phillips curve labeled with the price level earns nothing.

Textbook

Figure 34.1. U.S. data, 1955–1968: each dot is a year, and the low-unemployment years sit high on the inflation axis while the high-unemployment years sit low.

Numeric

Your textbook’s curve. With 0% expected inflation, 6% unemployment gives 0% inflation and 4% gives 2%.

Illustrative

A hiring boom. Unemployment falls from 6% to 4%; firms short of workers raise wages and prices faster.

How it connects. You read the economy’s position off it (entry 2). Demand moves the economy along it (entry 3); supply shocks shift it (entry 4). Its long-run partner is the LRPC (entry 7).

Check yourself

1.1 Which axis carries the inflation rate?

Show answer

The vertical axis. The unemployment rate is on the horizontal.

1.2 Unemployment falls along an SRPC. What happens to inflation?

Show answer

It rises.

02

Reading a point

★ Exam
Definition

An economy is always somewhere on a short-run Phillips curve. Its unemployment rate and inflation rate today are one point on it.

Shown in every figure of the module; not said in words.

To read the economy’s position, use the SRPC. Given the inflation rate, go across to the SRPC and down to the unemployment rate; given unemployment, go up to the SRPC and across.

The long-run curve tells you where the economy will end up, not where it is now.

Watch out. Read the point off the SRPC, not the vertical LRPC. The LRPC gives the natural rate, not the current rate.

Textbook

Figure 34.5. Points A, B and C are each on a short-run curve: A is on SRPC0, at 4% unemployment and 2% inflation.

Numeric

A straight-line SRPC. It runs from 1% unemployment and 8% inflation to 5% and 0%; the LRPC is at 4%. At 6% inflation, unemployment is 2%.

Illustrative

The diagram above. Whatever button you press, the dot sits on the solid SRPC.

How it connects. It reads the SRPC (entry 1). Where the point sits against the LRPC tells you long-run equilibrium (entry 9) or a gap (entry 10).

Check yourself

2.1 On that straight-line SRPC, what is unemployment when inflation is 4%?

Show answer

3%: inflation falls 2 points for each point of unemployment, from 8% at 1%.

2.2 Why not read the current rate off the LRPC?

Show answer

The LRPC is vertical at the natural rate. It shows the long run, not the economy’s current point.

03

Demand shocks: movement along the SRPC

★ Exam

⚑ Partly not in your textbook — learn it from here. Your textbook moves the economy along the curve with “monetary policy, fiscal policy, or both” but never calls it a demand shock, and links it to the aggregate demand and supply graph only in the solutions at the back of the book.

Definition

A change in aggregate demand moves the economy along the short-run Phillips curve. It does not shift the curve.

Textbook page 335, and the solution to Check Your Understanding 1.

When aggregate demand rises, real GDP rises, firms hire and unemployment falls; the price level rises faster, so inflation rises. On the Phillips curve that is a move up and to the left.

When aggregate demand falls, unemployment rises and inflation falls — a move down and to the right. Consumer and business spending, government purchases, taxes and the money supply all work this way.

Watch out. A demand shock never shifts the SRPC. If your answer shifts the curve after a change in spending, it is wrong.

Textbook

Point A. Policy pushes unemployment from 6% to 4%: the economy moves from E0 to point A on SRPC0, and inflation rises from 0% to 2%.

Numeric

The diagram above. Press Aggregate demand rises. Unemployment falls from 6% to 4% and inflation rises from 2% to 4%, along the same curve.

Illustrative

A loss of confidence. Households cut spending: unemployment rises and inflation falls, down the SRPC.

How it connects. It moves the economy along the SRPC (entry 1), unlike a supply shock (entry 4), and can open either gap (entry 10).

Check yourself

3.1 The central bank increases the money supply. Along the SRPC, or a shift?

Show answer

Along it: up and to the left.

3.2 Government spending falls. Which way does the economy move on the SRPC?

Show answer

Down and to the right: higher unemployment, lower inflation.

04

Supply shocks: the SRPC shifts

★ Exam
Definition

A negative supply shock shifts the SRPC up; a positive supply shock shifts it down.

Textbook page 332 and Figure 34.3.

A supply shock shifts the short-run aggregate supply curve. A surge in oil prices raises costs, so inflation is higher at every unemployment rate: the whole SRPC moves up.

That is how inflation and unemployment can rise together — stagflation, the 1970s. Falling raw-material prices or faster productivity growth do the reverse, as in the 1990s.

Watch out. After a negative supply shock the economy is to the right of long-run equilibrium — a recessionary gap — even though inflation is higher.

Textbook

The 1970s. Oil prices soared and productivity growth slowed; the SRPC shifted up, and high unemployment came with high inflation.

Numeric

The diagram above. Negative supply shock raises the SRPC by 3 points. Unemployment goes from 6% to 7% and inflation from 2% to 4%.

Illustrative

A drought. Food prices jump across the economy; costs rise, and inflation and unemployment rise together.

How it connects. It shifts the SRPC (entry 1), where a demand shock (entry 3) moves along it. Expected inflation (entry 6) shifts it the same way.

Check yourself

4.1 Oil prices fall sharply. Which way does the SRPC shift?

Show answer

Down — a positive supply shock.

4.2 What is stagflation?

Show answer

Higher inflation and higher unemployment at the same time, from a negative supply shock.

05

Expected inflation

★ Exam
Definition

The rate of inflation that employers and workers expect in the near future.

Textbook page 333, margin.

People base it on experience: if inflation has been about 2% for a few years, they expect about 2%; if it has averaged 5%, they expect 5%.

It matters because it is built into wage contracts and prices. A worker who expects 5% inflation asks for a raise that keeps up; an employer who expects to sell at higher prices can agree to it.

Watch out. Expected inflation is not actual inflation. In the short run they can differ; in the long run they are equal.

Textbook

The 1960s. Inflation had been low for decades, so people expected little; only after 1965 did persistent inflation become a fact of life and enter expectations.

Numeric

Recent experience. Inflation of 4%, 5% and 6% in the last three years: people expect about 5%.

Illustrative

A wage contract. A union that expects 3% inflation asks for 3% just to stand still, before any real raise.

How it connects. It shifts the SRPC (entry 6), and it is what catches up in the long run (entry 11).

Check yourself

5.1 How do people form expected inflation, in your textbook?

Show answer

From experience: recent inflation.

5.2 Actual inflation is 6% and expected inflation is 4%. Can that last?

Show answer

Not in the long run: expectations catch up.

06

Expected inflation shifts the SRPC

★ Exam
Definition

A rise in expected inflation shifts the SRPC up by the same amount: each extra point of expected inflation adds one point to inflation at every unemployment rate.

Textbook pages 333–335 and Figure 34.4.

With 0% expected inflation, your textbook’s curve gives 0% inflation at 6% unemployment and 2% at 4%. With 2% expected, every point is 2 higher: 2% at 6%, 4% at 4%.

It works through costs: higher expected inflation raises wages, which shifts the short-run aggregate supply curve left and the SRPC up. A fall in expected inflation shifts both back.

Watch out. A change in expected inflation shifts the curve. It is not a movement along it.

Textbook

Figure 34.4. SRPC0 and SRPC2 are 2 points apart at every unemployment rate.

Numeric

The diagram above. Expectations catch up: expected inflation rises from 2% to 4%, the SRPC rises 2 points, and at 6% unemployment inflation is 4%.

Illustrative

A believed announcement. The central bank convinces everyone that inflation will fall to 2%; expected inflation drops and the SRPC shifts down.

How it connects. It is how expected inflation (entry 5) acts, in the same direction as a supply shock (entry 4), and it drives the long-run adjustment (entry 11).

Check yourself

6.1 Expected inflation rises from 3% to 5%. How far does the SRPC move?

Show answer

Up 2 percentage points at every unemployment rate.

6.2 On SRPC0 inflation is 2% at 4% unemployment. What is it at 4% on the curve for 4% expected inflation?

Show answer

6% — four points higher.

07

Long-run Phillips curve

★ Exam
Definition

The relationship between unemployment and inflation after expectations have had time to adjust. It is vertical at the natural rate of unemployment: the LRPC.

Textbook page 336, margin, and Figure 34.5.

In the long run any steady rate of inflation is expected, and once it is expected it no longer lowers unemployment. Unemployment returns to the natural rate whatever the inflation rate.

So there is no long-run trade-off. The LRPC is the Phillips-curve twin of the vertical long-run aggregate supply curve.

Watch out. Vertical does not mean inflation cannot change. Inflation can settle at any rate; unemployment cannot stay off the natural rate.

Textbook

Figure 34.5. E0, E2 and E4 — 0%, 2% and 4% inflation — are all at 6% unemployment; the LRPC passes through all three.

Numeric

Your textbook’s natural rate. 6%: the LRPC is drawn there at every inflation rate.

Illustrative

Two decades. A country averages 2% inflation one decade and 8% the next; its unemployment averages the same natural rate in both.

How it connects. It is the long-run partner of the SRPC (entry 1), standing at the natural rate (entry 8). Where the two cross is long-run equilibrium (entry 9); it moves only when the natural rate does (entry 12).

Check yourself

7.1 What shape is the LRPC, and where does it stand?

Show answer

Vertical, at the natural rate of unemployment.

7.2 Why is there no long-run trade-off?

Show answer

Once inflation is expected, unemployment returns to the natural rate at any inflation rate.

08

The natural rate and the NAIRU

★ Exam
Definition

The natural rate of unemployment — frictional plus structural — is where the LRPC stands. Your textbook also calls it the NAIRU, the nonaccelerating inflation rate of unemployment.

Textbook pages 336–337.

The name NAIRU describes what happens at that rate: inflation stays constant. Below it, inflation keeps speeding up; above it, inflation keeps slowing down.

Your textbook says plainly that the NAIRU is another name for the natural rate. Your exam uses only natural rate — use that name on every graph.

Watch out. The natural rate is not zero, and it is not the lowest unemployment the economy can reach in the short run. It is the rate the economy returns to.

Textbook

Europe. When European unemployment fell from above 9% towards 8% in the late 1980s, inflation began to speed up: Europe’s natural rate was 9% or more.

Numeric

Figure 34.5. At 6% unemployment inflation is constant, at 0%, 2% or 4%: whatever was expected.

Illustrative

From Module 13. Frictional unemployment of 3% plus structural unemployment of 2% is a natural rate of 5%; the LRPC stands there.

How it connects. It places the LRPC (entry 7), which moves when it changes (entry 12).

Check yourself

8.1 What other name does your textbook use for the natural rate?

Show answer

The NAIRU.

8.2 Frictional unemployment is 4% and structural unemployment 1.5%. Where is the LRPC?

Show answer

At 5.5%.

09

Long-run equilibrium

★ Exam

⚑ The name is not in your textbook — learn it from here. Your textbook marks these points E0, E2 and E4 but never calls them long-run equilibrium. Your exam does.

Definition

Long-run equilibrium is where the SRPC crosses the LRPC: unemployment is at the natural rate and actual inflation equals expected inflation.

Textbook pages 335–336, Figure 34.5, without the name.

At that point nobody is surprised: inflation is what people expected, so wages and prices have no reason to change course, and unemployment stays at the natural rate.

Every SRPC crosses the LRPC once, at an inflation rate equal to its expected inflation. That crossing is the long-run equilibrium for that curve.

Watch out. Long-run equilibrium can come with any inflation rate — 0%, 2% or 4%. What makes it long-run equilibrium is unemployment at the natural rate.

Textbook

E2. Expected inflation 2%, unemployment 6%, inflation 2%.

Numeric

The diagram above. Long-run equilibrium: 6% unemployment, 2% inflation, 2% expected.

Illustrative

A steady economy. Inflation has run at 3% for years and everyone expects 3%; unemployment sits at the natural rate.

How it connects. It is the point where the LRPC (entry 7) crosses the curve you read (entry 2). Points off it are gaps (entry 10), and the economy returns to it (entry 11).

Check yourself

9.1 An SRPC was drawn for 5% expected inflation. At what inflation rate does it cross the LRPC?

Show answer

5%.

9.2 The natural rate is 6% and unemployment is 4%. Is the economy in long-run equilibrium?

Show answer

No. It is off the LRPC, to its left.

10

Inflationary and recessionary gaps

★ Exam

⚑ Not in your textbook — learn it from here. Your textbook uses these two words only on the aggregate demand and supply graph, never on the Phillips curve. Your exam asks for them on both.

Definition

A point to the left of long-run equilibrium — unemployment below the natural rate — is an inflationary gap. A point to the right — unemployment above it — is a recessionary gap.

Not in the textbook on this graph. Its aggregate demand and supply version is in Unit 3.

Left of the LRPC, real GDP is above potential output: the economy is overheating, as in an inflationary gap on the aggregate demand and supply graph. Right of it, real GDP is below potential: a recessionary gap.

The sides swap between the two graphs. More output means less unemployment, so a gap on the right of the aggregate demand and supply graph is on the left of the Phillips curve.

Watch out. Judge the gap by unemployment, not by inflation. A negative supply shock brings higher inflation and a recessionary gap.

Textbook

Figure 34.5. Point A, at 4% unemployment against a 6% natural rate, is to the left: an inflationary gap.

Numeric

The diagram above. Aggregate demand falls: unemployment of 8% against a natural rate of 6% — a recessionary gap.

Illustrative

Two countries. Both have a natural rate of 5%; one has unemployment of 3.5%, the other 7%. The first has an inflationary gap, the second a recessionary gap.

How it connects. It is read from the point (entry 2) against long-run equilibrium (entry 9); a demand shock (entry 3) opens one.

Check yourself

10.1 The natural rate is 5% and unemployment is 6.5%. Which gap?

Show answer

A recessionary gap — to the right.

10.2 On the aggregate demand and supply graph an inflationary gap is to the right of potential output. Where is it on the Phillips curve?

Show answer

To the left of the natural rate.

11

From the short run to the long run

★ Exam
Definition

In the long run, expected inflation catches up with actual inflation, the SRPC shifts, and unemployment returns to the natural rate.

Textbook pages 335–336 and Figure 34.5.

Start in long-run equilibrium and raise aggregate demand: unemployment falls and inflation rises, along the SRPC. Inflation is now above what people expected. As they come to expect it, wages rise, short-run aggregate supply shifts left and the SRPC shifts up. Unemployment returns to the natural rate at the higher inflation rate.

If policy keeps pushing unemployment below the natural rate, the process repeats: inflation rises again and again — 2%, then 4%, then 6% in your textbook. Holding unemployment below the natural rate means accelerating inflation, so it cannot be kept up.

Watch out. In the long run unemployment returns to the natural rate, but inflation does not return to where it started. It ends higher.

Textbook

A, B and C. Holding unemployment at 4%: inflation 2% at A, 4% at B, 6% at C.

Numeric

The diagram above. Press Aggregate demand rises, then Expectations catch up: from 4% unemployment and 4% inflation back to 6% unemployment, with inflation still 4%.

Illustrative

A recession left alone. Unemployment is above the natural rate and inflation falls below what was expected; expectations fall, the SRPC shifts down, and unemployment returns to the natural rate.

How it connects. It is expected inflation (entry 5) catching up and shifting the SRPC (entry 6) until the economy is back in long-run equilibrium (entry 9). Run in reverse, it is disinflation (entry 13).

Check yourself

11.1 Inflation turns out higher than expected. In the long run, where does unemployment go?

Show answer

Back to the natural rate, as expected inflation rises and the SRPC shifts up.

11.2 Why can’t policy keep unemployment below the natural rate for ever?

Show answer

Inflation would keep accelerating as expectations rise.

12

Shifts of the LRPC

★ Exam

⚑ Not in your textbook — learn it from here. Your textbook never moves the long-run Phillips curve. Your exam expects you to shift it when the natural rate changes; the causes are the ones you met in Module 13.

Definition

Anything that changes the natural rate of unemployment shifts the LRPC: a lower natural rate moves it left, a higher one right.

Not in the textbook. The causes are in Module 13, pages 128–131.

The natural rate changes with the labor force and with institutions. Job-matching websites and training programs lower it; more inexperienced workers, more generous unemployment benefits or a minimum wage above equilibrium raise it.

When the LRPC shifts, the SRPC moves with it, so that the two still cross at expected inflation. Potential output moves the other way round: a lower natural rate means the long-run aggregate supply curve shifts right.

Watch out. Changes in spending, supply shocks and expected inflation do not shift the LRPC. Only a change in the natural rate does.

Textbook

Module 13. Job websites cut frictional unemployment; a wave of young, inexperienced workers raises it.

Numeric

The diagram above. The natural rate falls from 6% to 5%; the LRPC moves left, and the economy settles at 5% unemployment with 2% inflation.

Illustrative

Longer benefits. Unemployment benefits are extended from six months to two years; people search longer, the natural rate rises and the LRPC shifts right.

How it connects. It moves the LRPC (entry 7) because it moves the natural rate (entry 8).

Check yourself

12.1 A government retraining program cuts structural unemployment. Which way does the LRPC shift?

Show answer

Left.

12.2 Does an increase in aggregate demand shift the LRPC?

Show answer

No. It moves the economy along the SRPC.

13

Disinflation and its cost

★ Exam
Definition

Disinflation is bringing the inflation rate down. Once high inflation is built into expectations, it needs unemployment above the natural rate for a time.

Textbook pages 337–338 and the FYI on the 1980s.

Run the long-run adjustment in reverse. Contractionary policy moves the economy down the SRPC: unemployment rises above the natural rate and inflation falls below what people expected. As expectations fall, the SRPC shifts down and unemployment returns to the natural rate at the lower inflation rate.

The cost is the output lost in between. A credible, clearly announced policy can lower expectations faster and so lower the cost.

Watch out. Disinflation is a fall in the rate of inflation — prices still rise, more slowly. Deflation is a fall in the price level.

Textbook

1980–1987. Core inflation fell from about 12% to about 4%; unemployment rose above 9%, output fell 7% below potential, and the cost was about 18% of a year’s real GDP.

Numeric

Down the curve. Expected inflation 6%, natural rate 6%: contractionary policy raises unemployment to 8%, and inflation falls to 4% on that curve before expectations follow.

Illustrative

A promise believed. A new central-bank head announces a 2% target and is believed; expected inflation falls quickly and less unemployment is needed.

How it connects. It is the long-run adjustment (entry 11) run backwards. Its opposite number in prices, deflation, brings debt deflation (entry 14).

Check yourself

13.1 Inflation falls from 5% to 3%. Disinflation or deflation?

Show answer

Disinflation — prices still rise, more slowly.

13.2 Why does disinflation raise unemployment for a while?

Show answer

Inflation has to fall below what people expect, which means moving down the SRPC until expectations adjust.

14

Debt deflation

○ Background
Definition

The fall in aggregate demand caused when deflation raises the real burden of debts.

Textbook pages 338–339, margin.

When the price level falls, each dollar owed is worth more. Borrowers lose and cut their spending sharply; lenders gain but spend little more. Aggregate demand falls, which can push prices down further.

Irving Fisher argued that this vicious circle deepened the Great Depression.

Watch out. Background only. Who gains and loses from unexpected deflation is Module 14; the spiral is not on your exam.

Textbook

The Great Depression. Prices fell, debts fixed in dollars grew heavier, and spending collapsed.

Numeric

A $10,000 loan. Prices fall 10%: repaying $10,000 now costs what about $11,111 did before.

Illustrative

A farmer. Crop prices fall and the mortgage does not; the farmer stops buying equipment.

How it connects. It follows from deflation, the opposite of disinflation (entry 13), and is one reason policy makers fear the zero bound (entry 15).

Check yourself

14.1 Who gains from deflation, borrowers or lenders?

Show answer

Lenders.

14.2 Why does debt deflation reduce aggregate demand?

Show answer

Borrowers cut their spending by more than lenders raise theirs.

15

The zero bound and the liquidity trap

○ Background
Definition

The zero bound: a nominal interest rate cannot fall below zero. A liquidity trap is when monetary policy cannot help because interest rates are already at zero.

Textbook pages 339–341, margins, and Figures 34.6–34.8.

Nobody lends at a negative nominal rate when holding cash pays zero. So when expected deflation drags the equilibrium rate towards zero, the central bank cannot cut it any further.

Japan reached it in the 1990s with its zero interest rate policy; the United States did in 2008–2009. It is one reason central banks prefer low but positive inflation.

Watch out. Background only. Your exam does not ask about the zero bound.

Textbook

Japan. The Lost Decade: deflation, and an interest rate cut all the way to zero.

Numeric

Your AP Review, free response 2. A 4% real rate with −2% expected inflation gives a nominal rate of 2%; with −6% it would be −2%, but it stops at 0%.

Illustrative

Cash under the mattress. If the bank charged you to lend it money, you would keep cash instead.

How it connects. It is where debt deflation (entry 14) leaves monetary policy.

Check yourself

15.1 Why can’t the nominal interest rate go below zero?

Show answer

Lenders would rather hold cash, which pays zero.

15.2 What is a liquidity trap?

Show answer

A situation in which monetary policy cannot cut interest rates further because they are already at zero.

The five things to get right from Module 34

  1. Label the axes as rates: inflation rate up, unemployment rate across. The SRPC slopes down; the LRPC is vertical at the natural rate.
  2. Along or shift. Demand moves the economy along the SRPC; supply shocks and expected inflation shift it; only the natural rate shifts the LRPC.
  3. Long-run equilibrium is where the two curves cross — at any inflation rate.
  4. Left is inflationary, right is recessionary — the opposite sides from the aggregate demand and supply graph.
  5. In the long run unemployment returns to the natural rate; inflation ends wherever expectations settle.
