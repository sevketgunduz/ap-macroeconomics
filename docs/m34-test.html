Section A — solutions

Question 1 — Short-run Phillips curve · Analysis

Answer: (D)

The short-run Phillips curve slopes downward (p. 331). Higher spending lowers unemployment and raises inflation, so the economy moves up and to the left along the curve.

  • (A) ✗ Reverses the slope. Tighter labor markets raise wages and prices.
  • (B) ✗ The vertical curve is the long-run curve. In the short run there is a trade-off.
  • (C) ✗ The short-run curve slopes downward, not upward.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ A change in spending moves the economy along the curve; it does not shift it.

Question 2 — Short-run Phillips curve · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Both axes are rates: inflation up, unemployment across. The curve slopes downward (Figure 34.2).

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Those are the aggregate demand curve's axes and slope.
  • (C) ✗ The right axes, but the curve slopes downward.
  • (D) ✗ Real GDP is not on a Phillips curve, and unemployment belongs on the horizontal axis.
  • (E) ✗ The price level is a level, not a rate, and a vertical curve is the long-run curve.

Question 3 — Reading a point · Application

Answer: (B)

The CED's sample question 14. The economy is always on the short-run curve (MOD-3.A.2), so read 6% off the SRPC: inflation falls 2 points for each point of unemployment, from 8% at 1%, so 6% inflation is at 2% unemployment. The LRPC at 4% is the natural rate, not the current rate.

  • (A) ✗ At 1% unemployment the SRPC gives 8% inflation, not 6%.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ At 3% the SRPC gives 4% inflation.
  • (D) ✗ 4% is where the LRPC stands — the natural rate, where the SRPC gives 2% inflation.
  • (E) ✗ At 5% the SRPC gives 0% inflation.

Question 4 — Reading a point · Application

Answer: (E)

5 = 2 + (6 − unemployment rate), so 6 − unemployment rate = 3 and the unemployment rate is 3%.

  • (A) ✗ 6% is the natural rate, where the LRPC stands. Inflation there would be 2%, not 5%.
  • (B) ✗ Reads the inflation rate as the unemployment rate.
  • (C) ✗ Adds instead of subtracting: 6 + 5 − 2 = 9.
  • (D) ✗ Subtracts expected inflation from 6 and ignores actual inflation.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 5 — Demand shocks · Analysis

Answer: (C)

Lower interest rates raise investment and consumption: aggregate demand rises. A demand shock moves the economy along the short-run curve (MOD-3.B.1), up and to the left — lower unemployment, higher inflation. Krugman's own case is policy driving unemployment to 4%: "point A on SRPC0" (p. 335).

  • (A) ✗ A demand shock does not shift the short-run curve.
  • (B) ✗ Only a change in the natural rate shifts the long-run curve.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ That is a fall in aggregate demand.
  • (E) ✗ A shift, and in the wrong direction.

Question 6 — Demand shocks · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

On the AD–AS graph, AD shifts left along a fixed SRAS: the price level and real GDP both fall. Less output means more unemployment, and a slower rise in prices means lower inflation: on the Phillips curve, a movement down and to the right along the short-run curve. The CED's Activity 1 for this topic is exactly this correspondence (p. 91).

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ A fall in AD lowers real GDP, it does not raise it.
  • (C) ✗ Describes a negative supply shock, not a fall in demand.
  • (D) ✗ The AD–AS half is right, but a demand shock moves along the short-run curve rather than shifting it.
  • (E) ✗ The AD–AS half is right, but more unemployment is a move down and to the right.

Question 7 — Supply shocks · Analysis

Answer: (D)

An oil-price surge is a negative supply shock. It shifts SRAS left and the short-run Phillips curve up (MOD-3.B.2; Figure 34.3), so inflation and unemployment can rise together — the stagflation of the 1970s (p. 334).

  • (A) ✗ A supply shock shifts the curve; it is not a movement along it.
  • (B) ✗ The long-run curve moves only with the natural rate.
  • (C) ✗ That is a positive supply shock.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Supply shocks shift the short-run curve too, not only expectations.

Question 8 — Supply shock, then the gap · Synthesis

Answer: (B)

Before the shock, the curve gave 2 + (6 − 7) = 1% inflation at 7% unemployment; the economy is at 4%, so the shock raised the curve by 3 points. But the gap is read from unemployment: 7% is to the right of the natural rate, a recessionary gap (MOD-3.A.5), however high inflation is.

  • (A) ✗ The gap is judged by unemployment against the natural rate, not inflation against expected inflation.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Long-run equilibrium needs unemployment at the natural rate.
  • (D) ✗ Higher inflation does not make a gap inflationary; the point is to the right of long-run equilibrium.
  • (E) ✗ Unemployment rose from 6% to 7%.

Question 9 — Expected inflation · Analysis

Answer: (E)

Krugman: people base their expectations on experience — "if the inflation rate has averaged around 5% lately, people will expect inflation to be around 5%" (p. 335) — and those expectations are built into wages and prices (p. 333).

  • (A) ✗ Expectations follow recent experience, not the distant past.
  • (B) ✗ Experience is exactly what forms them.
  • (C) ✗ The most frequent rate is not the recent rate; the last years run 2%, 4%, 6%.
  • (D) ✗ Nothing in the account makes high inflation lower expectations.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 10 — Expected inflation shifts the SRPC · Application

Answer: (C)

5 + (6 − 6) = 5%. The curve has risen 3 points, the amount of the increase in expected inflation, so at the natural rate inflation equals expected inflation (Figure 34.4).

  • (A) ✗ The old inflation rate, on the old curve.
  • (B) ✗ The size of the shift, not the new inflation rate.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Adds the new expected inflation to the old inflation rate: 5 + 2.
  • (E) ✗ Forgets to subtract the unemployment rate: 5 + 6.

Question 11 — Expected inflation shifts the SRPC · Analysis

Answer: (B)

A change in expected inflation shifts the short-run curve (p. 333); every other event listed changes aggregate demand and moves the economy along it.

  • (A) ✗ A tax cut raises aggregate demand: a movement along the curve.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ More government purchases raise aggregate demand: a movement along the curve.
  • (D) ✗ Less investment lowers aggregate demand: a movement along the curve.
  • (E) ✗ More money raises aggregate demand: a movement along the curve.

Question 12 — Long-run Phillips curve · Analysis

Answer: (E)

The trade-off exists only while inflation is unexpected. Once 6% is expected, the short-run curve shifts up and unemployment returns to the natural rate: the long-run curve is vertical (MOD-3.A.3; p. 336).

  • (A) ✗ The short-run curve shows a trade-off only for a given expected inflation rate.
  • (B) ✗ Inflation does not move the natural rate; it moves along the vertical long-run curve.
  • (C) ✗ Below the natural rate, inflation rises above expectations.
  • (D) ✗ The long-run curve is vertical, not upward-sloping.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 13 — The natural rate and the NAIRU · Application

Answer: (A)

The natural rate is frictional plus structural: 3.5 + 1.5 = 5%. The long-run curve stands there (MEA-1.E.2, MOD-3.A.3). Cyclical unemployment is the gap between the actual rate and the natural rate.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Adds cyclical unemployment: that is the actual rate, 6%.
  • (C) ✗ Cyclical unemployment alone.
  • (D) ✗ Frictional plus cyclical: 3.5 + 1.
  • (E) ✗ Frictional unemployment alone.

Question 14 — Long-run equilibrium · Analysis

Answer: (D)

Long-run equilibrium is where the short-run curve crosses the long-run curve (MOD-3.A.4): unemployment at the natural rate, 5%, and inflation equal to expected inflation, 3%.

  • (A) ✗ The natural rate is right, but on this curve inflation there is 3%, not 0%.
  • (B) ✗ Swaps the two numbers.
  • (C) ✗ Unemployment of zero is not the natural rate.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Expected inflation is right, but 3% unemployment is below the natural rate.

Question 15 — Inflationary and recessionary gaps · Analysis

Answer: (C)

4% is to the left of the natural rate: an inflationary gap (MOD-3.A.5). Less unemployment than the natural rate means more output than potential — the same gap that sits to the right of potential output on the AD–AS graph.

  • (A) ✗ Unemployment below the natural rate is not a recessionary gap.
  • (B) ✗ The right gap, but output is above potential, not below.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ The wrong gap; and a recessionary gap has output below potential.
  • (E) ✗ Long-run equilibrium needs unemployment at the natural rate.

Question 16 — The point and the gap · Synthesis

Answer: (E)

The situation of the CED's sample FRQ 1 (Exam Information p. 138), on the Phillips curve. Unemployment of 5% is below the natural rate of 6%, so the economy is up and to the left of long-run equilibrium on the short-run curve: inflation is above the 4% expected, and the gap is inflationary. On a curve with Krugman's slope, 4 + (6 − 5) = 5%.

  • (A) ✗ Both halves reversed.
  • (B) ✗ Long-run equilibrium would need unemployment at 6%.
  • (C) ✗ The inflation half is right, but unemployment below the natural rate is an inflationary gap.
  • (D) ✗ The gap is right, but left of long-run equilibrium inflation is above expected, not below.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 17 — From the short run to the long run · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

Inflation is now above what was expected. Expectations catch up, wages rise, SRAS shifts left and the short-run curve shifts up, until unemployment is back at the natural rate — at the higher inflation rate (pp. 335–336).

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Unemployment cannot stay below the natural rate once inflation is expected.
  • (C) ✗ Demand does not move the long-run curve.
  • (D) ✗ Expected inflation rises, not falls.
  • (E) ✗ Unemployment returns to the natural rate, but inflation stays higher; it does not return to 2%.

Question 18 — From the short run to the long run · Synthesis

Answer: (C)

This year: 2 + (6 − 4) = 4%. Next year expected inflation is 4%: 4 + (6 − 4) = 6%. Holding unemployment below the natural rate makes inflation accelerate — Krugman's points A, B and C (Figure 34.5).

  • (A) ✗ This year's inflation; it ignores the shift as expectations adjust.
  • (B) ✗ The original expected inflation.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Adds 4 points instead of 2: the gap is 6 − 4 = 2 points of unemployment.
  • (E) ✗ Averages the 2% expected and this year's 4%.

Question 19 — Shifts of the LRPC · Analysis

Answer: (D)

Longer job searches raise frictional unemployment and so the natural rate (Module 13). A higher natural rate shifts the long-run curve right (MOD-3.B.3).

  • (A) ✗ That would be a rise in aggregate demand.
  • (B) ✗ A downward shift of the short-run curve comes from a positive supply shock or lower expectations.
  • (C) ✗ The right curve, the wrong direction: the natural rate rises.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Benefits change how long people search, which changes the natural rate.

Question 20 — Shifts of the LRPC · Analysis

Answer: (B)

Faster job matching cuts frictional unemployment, lowering the natural rate: the long-run curve shifts left.

  • (A) ✗ Expected inflation shifts the short-run curve.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Demand moves the economy along the short-run curve.
  • (D) ✗ A supply shock shifts the short-run curve.
  • (E) ✗ A higher minimum wage raises structural unemployment, shifting the long-run curve right.

Question 21 — Disinflation and its cost · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Bringing down embedded inflation is disinflation (p. 338). Inflation must fall below what people expect, which means moving down the short-run curve: unemployment rises above the natural rate until expectations adjust. The 1980s cost about 18% of a year's real GDP (p. 337).

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Contractionary policy raises unemployment.
  • (C) ✗ The cost is right, but prices still rise at 3%: that is not deflation.
  • (D) ✗ The long-run curve is vertical, but the economy has to travel down the short-run curve to reach it.
  • (E) ✗ The rise is temporary, and the process is not deflation.

Question 22 — Credibility and the cost · Synthesis

Answer: (D)

A credible announcement lowers expected inflation sooner, shifting the short-run curve down, so less unemployment is needed to bring inflation down (p. 338).

  • (A) ✗ The surprise is what makes disinflation costly.
  • (B) ✗ The long-run outcome is the same; the short-run cost depends on how fast expectations fall.
  • (C) ✗ A believed plan to cut inflation lowers expectations.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ The economy cannot move along the long-run curve without a short-run cost unless expectations adjust at once.

Section B — solutions

Question 23 — Debt deflation · Analysis

Answer: (C)

Deflation raises the real value of debts. Borrowers, often short of cash, cut spending sharply; lenders gain but raise spending little. The net effect lowers aggregate demand (pp. 338–339).

  • (A) ✗ Lenders gain from deflation.
  • (B) ✗ Expected deflation lowers the nominal interest rate.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.

Question 24 — The zero bound and the liquidity trap · Application

Answer: (B)

3 + (−5) = −2%, but no one lends at a negative nominal rate when cash pays zero: the rate stops at 0%, the zero bound (p. 339).

  • (D) ✗ The real rate, not the nominal rate.
  • (E) ✗ Subtracts the wrong way round: 5 − 3.
