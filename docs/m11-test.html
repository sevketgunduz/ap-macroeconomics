Section A

1. In Year 2 an economy produces exactly the same quantity of every final good and service as in Year 1. Every price is 10% higher.

Which statement is correct?

  • (A) Aggregate output rose by 10%, because GDP rose by 10%.
  • (B) Nominal GDP and real GDP both rose by 10%.
  • (C) Aggregate output is unchanged; nominal GDP rose by 10% and real GDP is unchanged.
  • (D) Real GDP rose by 10% and nominal GDP is unchanged.
  • (E) Aggregate output fell, because the same spending now buys less.

2. From one year to the next, the quantity of every final good an economy produces falls by 5%, and every price rises by 8%.

What happens to nominal GDP?

  • (A) It rises by 2.6%.
  • (B) It rises by 8%.
  • (C) It falls by 5%.
  • (D) It rises by 13%.
  • (E) It rises by exactly 3%, because 8% − 5% = 3%.

3. A country's nominal GDP rose by 6% last year. A newspaper reports that the country produced 6% more goods and services than the year before.

What can correctly be concluded from the information given?

  • (A) The report is right: real GDP rose by 6%.
  • (B) Real GDP fell, because nominal growth always overstates real growth.
  • (C) Real GDP rose by more than 6%, because real GDP removes the effect of prices.
  • (D) Real GDP rose, but by less than 6%.
  • (E) Nothing about real GDP, without knowing how the overall price level changed.

4. Between two years, a country's nominal GDP rose by 11% while its GDP deflator rose by 15%.

What happened to the country's aggregate output?

  • (A) It rose by 11%.
  • (B) It fell, by about 3.5%.
  • (C) It rose by about 26%.
  • (D) It rose, by about 4%.
  • (E) It cannot have fallen, because nominal GDP rose.

5. A statistical agency reports real GDP "in 2017 dollars". Nominal GDP in 2017 was $19.5 trillion.

Which pair of values for 2017 is correct?

  • (A) Real GDP $19.5 trillion; GDP deflator 0.
  • (B) Real GDP cannot be known for 2017 until a later year is chosen as the base.
  • (C) Real GDP $19.5 trillion; GDP deflator 1.0.
  • (D) Real GDP $19.5 trillion; GDP deflator 100.
  • (E) Real GDP below $19.5 trillion, because real GDP removes inflation; GDP deflator 100.

What is the best interpretation?

  • (B) Real GDP did not really rise, because rebuilding only replaces what existed before.
  • (C) The rise is inflation: nominal GDP rose but real GDP did not.
  • (D) Measured GDP should have fallen, because the destroyed homes are subtracted from GDP.
  • (E) Real GDP did rise, but it measures spending to replace what was lost; GDP counts the rebuilding and never subtracts the destruction, so it overstates any gain in well-being.

  • (B) Neither nominal nor real GDP changes, so the improvement goes unmeasured — a limitation of GDP.
  • (C) Nominal GDP rises by the value of the improvement, but real GDP does not.
  • (D) Real GDP falls, because the same spending now buys a more expensive kind of product.
  • (E) The improvement is counted as investment spending, because it raises future productivity.

Solutions

Question 1 — Aggregate output · Application

Answer: (C)

Aggregate output is a quantity — the total of final goods and services produced — and no quantity changed. Nominal GDP values that output at current prices, so it rises by 10%. Real GDP values it at constant prices, so it does not move.

  • (A) ✗ Treats a rise in the value of GDP as a rise in output. This is the confusion the whole

module exists to remove.

  • (B) ✗ Real GDP holds prices constant, so a pure price rise cannot move it.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Exactly reversed. Nominal GDP is the one that moves with prices.
  • (E) ✗ Spending rose by 10% and bought exactly the same output. Nothing fell.

Question 2 — Nominal GDP · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Nominal GDP is how much is spent on output: quantity × price. Each quantity is multiplied by 0.95 and each price by 1.08, so nominal GDP is multiplied by 0.95 × 1.08 = 1.026. It rises by 2.6%, even though output fell.

  • (A) ✓ Correct. Nominal GDP can rise while production falls — Portugal in 1975.
  • (B) ✗ Counts the price change and ignores the quantity change.
  • (C) ✗ That is the change in real GDP, which removes the price effect.
  • (D) ✗ Adds the two magnitudes and ignores the sign of the quantity change.
  • (E) ✗ Subtraction is an approximation, and "exactly" makes it wrong. For changes this size it

is close; for the changes in this module it can be badly out.

Question 3 — Real GDP · Analysis

Answer: (E)

Nominal GDP moves with output and prices. A 6% rise could be 6% more output at the same prices, the same output at 6% higher prices, or any mix — including falling output with faster inflation. Real GDP needs the price information the report does not give.

  • (A) ✗ True only if the price level did not change, which nothing in the stem says.
  • (B) ✗ Nominal growth overstates real growth only when prices rise, and even then real GDP need

not fall.

  • (C) ✗ Removing a price rise lowers the growth figure. Real growth exceeds nominal only when

prices fall.

  • (D) ✗ The most likely outcome in practice, but not a conclusion the information supports. It

assumes positive inflation.

  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 4 — Why nominal GDP misleads · Analysis

Answer: (B)

Real GDP grows by the ratio of the two growth factors: 1.11 ÷ 1.15 = 0.965. Output fell by about 3.5%. This is Portugal between 1974 and 1975, where GDP measured in escudos rose 11% while output declined.

  • (A) ✗ That is the change in nominal GDP, not in output.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Adds the price rise to the nominal rise. Prices rising is a reason to lower the output

estimate.

  • (D) ✗ Subtracts the wrong way round. Prices rose faster than spending, so output fell.
  • (E) ✗ The error the textbook's Portugal story is built to correct. Nominal GDP rose because

prices rose.

Question 5 — The base year · Application

Answer: (D)

In the base year, real GDP is valued at that year's prices — which are also the current prices — so real and nominal GDP are equal, and the deflator, 100 × nominal ÷ real, is 100.

  • (A) ✗ A deflator of 0 would mean nominal GDP was zero.
  • (B) ✗ The base year is 2017, which the phrase "2017 dollars" states.
  • (C) ✗ The ratio of nominal to real is 1.0, but the deflator multiplies it by 100. Forgetting the

×100 is the commonest deflator slip.

  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ There is no inflation relative to the base year in the base year itself. The deflator

half is right and the real-GDP half contradicts it.

Question 6 — Calculating real GDP · Application

Answer: (A)

Year 2 quantities at Year 1 prices: (120 × $2) + (60 × $4) = $240 + $240 = $480.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Year 2 quantities at Year 2 prices: (120 × $3) + (60 × $5) = $660. That is nominal GDP.
  • (C) ✗ Year 1 quantities at Year 2 prices: (100 × $3) + (50 × $5) = $550. Quantities and prices

swapped between years.

  • (D) ✗ Year 1's GDP, $400. The question asks about Year 2.
  • (E) ✗ Pens at Year 2 prices, notebooks at Year 1 prices: $360 + $240 = $600. Only one price was

held constant.

Question 7 — The growth rate of real GDP · Analysis

Answer: (C)

Real GDP rose from $400 to $480: (480 − 400) ÷ 400 × 100 = 20%. Nominal GDP rose from $400 to $660, which is 65%, and the difference is all price.

  • (A) ✗ Nominal growth. It counts the price rises as output.
  • (B) ✗ Divides the $80 increase by the new value, $480. Percentage change is always over the

old value.

  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Subtracts inflation from nominal growth. The deflator rose from 100 to 137.5, and 65% −

37.5% = 27.5%. Subtraction is an approximation that fails badly at these sizes.

  • (E) ✗ Reports the $80 increase as a percentage.

Question 8 — The growth rate of real GDP · Analysis

Answer: (E)

Real growth is the nominal growth factor divided by the price growth factor. Country X: 1.10 ÷ 1.09 = 1.009, about 0.9%. Country Y: 1.04 ÷ 1.01 = 1.030, about 3.0%. Y's output grew more than three times as fast.

  • (A) ✗ Compares nominal growth, which in X is almost all inflation.
  • (B) ✗ Both grew, but by very different amounts. "Nominal above inflation" says only that real

growth is positive.

  • (C) ✗ Higher prices raise spending without raising output.
  • (D) ✗ The price-level change is all that is needed to convert nominal growth to real. That is

what a price index is for.

  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 9 — The GDP deflator · Application

Answer: (D)

GDP deflator = nominal GDP ÷ real GDP × 100 = 2,400 ÷ 2,000 × 100 = 120.

  • (A) ✗ Inverted: 2,000 ÷ 2,400 × 100. A deflator below 100 would mean prices are lower than in the

base year, but nominal GDP is above real GDP.

  • (B) ✗ The ratio without the ×100.
  • (C) ✗ The gap between nominal and real GDP in billions, not an index.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ The percentage by which prices exceed their base-year level. True as a statement about

prices, but the question asks for the index.

Question 10 — The GDP deflator · Analysis

Answer: (B)

The inflation rate is the percentage change in a price index, and the GDP deflator is one: (121 − 110) ÷ 110 × 100 = 10%.

  • (A) ✗ Measures the change from the base year, 100, not from the previous year.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Reports the change in index points as a percentage. The two are equal only when the

starting index is 100.

  • (D) ✗ Divides by the new value, 121.
  • (E) ✗ The framework says the inflation rate is the percentage change in *"a price index, such as

CPI or GDP deflator"*. Either one will do.

Question 11 — Converting nominal to real · Application

Answer: (E)

Real GDP = nominal GDP ÷ deflator × 100 = $450 ÷ 125 × 100 = $360 billion. This is the first scored point of the CED's own sample free-response question 2, with different numbers.

  • (A) ✗ Multiplies by the deflator instead of dividing, which adds inflation rather than

removing it. Real GDP must be below nominal when the deflator is above 100.

  • (B) ✗ Nominal GDP, unconverted.
  • (C) ✗ Subtracts the index from a dollar amount — not a meaningful operation.
  • (D) ✗ Divides by the deflator but forgets the ×100.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 12 — Converting nominal to real · Synthesis

Answer: (C)

Convert both years, then take the growth rate. Real GDP was 11,000 ÷ 110 × 100 = $10,000 billion and became 13,200 ÷ 120 × 100 = $11,000 billion: growth of 10%. Two steps from the framework — convert with the deflator, then compute a percentage change — combined.

  • (A) ✗ Nominal growth. The deflator is in the stem to be used.
  • (B) ✗ The subtraction shortcut, 20% − 9.1% ≈ 10.9%. Close, but not the answer, and it becomes

badly wrong at larger numbers.

  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ The inflation rate, (120 − 110) ÷ 110. It is the growth of prices, not of output.
  • (E) ✗ The deflator is precisely what makes a list of prices and quantities unnecessary.

Question 13 — A fixed base year can overstate growth · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

At Year 1 prices: Year 1 = (50 × $300) + (1,000 × $10) = $25,000; Year 2 = (100 × $300) + (1,000 × $10) = $40,000; growth 60%. At Year 2 prices: Year 1 = (50 × $150) + (1,000 × $30) = $37,500; Year 2 = (100 × $150) + (1,000 × $30) = $45,000; growth 20%.

Tablet output doubled while its price halved; haircut output did not change. Year 1 prices value each extra tablet at $300, a price nobody pays any more, and give the fast-growing good too much weight. That is the overstatement MEA-1.J.1 warns about.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ 80% is nominal growth, from $25,000 to $45,000. The two real calculations use the same

quantities but different prices, and the prices are what differ.

  • (C) ✗ The figures are reversed, and so is the reasoning. The general price level is not what

matters; relative prices are.

  • (D) ✗ The two figures are right, but nothing makes their average the "true" rate. Averaging is

one method statistical agencies use; it is not a discovery of the truth. Right arithmetic, wrong conclusion.

  • (E) ✗ Each calculation holds all prices constant, so neither contains inflation. They differ

because they weight the two goods differently.

Question 14 — A fixed base year can overstate growth · Analysis

Answer: (D)

Growth measured at fixed old prices weights each good by what it used to cost. A good whose price has collapsed and whose output has exploded is valued as if it were still expensive, so its growth counts for far more than its current importance. The framework: base-year weighting "can lead to overstatement of real GDP growth", which is why "statistical agencies actually use different methods."

  • (A) ✗ Confuses the overall price level with relative prices. Real GDP at any fixed prices

removes the price level; the problem is the weights.

  • (B) ✗ Real GDP removes the effect of the overall price level. It cannot remove the effect of

choosing which year's relative prices to weight by.

  • (C) ✗ Right direction, wrong reason. 1990 prices contain no inflation after 1990 — that is what

a base year means.

  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Computers' shrinking share of nominal GDP is the symptom of their falling price, and it

is exactly why 1990 prices over-weight them.

Question 15 — GDP per capita · Application

Answer: (B)

$840 billion ÷ 60 million = 840,000,000,000 ÷ 60,000,000 = $14,000 per person.

  • (A) ✗ A factor of ten lost in the units.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Multiplies instead of dividing.
  • (D) ✗ Divides 840 by 60 and ignores that one figure is in billions and the other in millions.
  • (E) ✗ Population divided by GDP — the ratio upside down.

Question 16 — GDP per capita · Analysis

Answer: (E)

P's economy is three times the size of Q's. But P's real GDP per capita is $3,000 billion ÷ 300 million = $10,000, and Q's is $1,000 billion ÷ 50 million = $20,000. Size and output per person are different questions, and they have different answers here.

  • (A) ✗ The first half is right; the second half confuses total output with output per person.
  • (B) ✗ The size of an economy is its total GDP, not its GDP per person.
  • (C) ✗ Per capita measures exist precisely to compare economies of different sizes.
  • (D) ✗ P produces three times as much in total. Per person it produces half as much.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 17 — Real GDP per capita and living standards · Synthesis

Answer: (C)

Convert, then divide. Real GDP in 2021 = $264,000 ÷ 110 × 100 = $240,000. Real GDP per capita: 2020 = $200,000 ÷ 80 = $2,500; 2021 = $240,000 ÷ 96 = $2,500. Unchanged. This is the structure of the CED's sample FRQ 2, whose scoring awards the point for stating that the standard of living stayed the same because real GDP per capita did not change.

  • (A) ✗ Nominal growth, which includes 10% inflation and ignores the 20% rise in population.
  • (B) ✗ Real output did rise 20% — but so did the population, by exactly the same proportion.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Rising prices do not by themselves lower living standards; what matters is real output

per person, and it is unchanged.

  • (E) ✗ True as arithmetic — $264,000 ÷ 96 = $2,750 — but that is nominal GDP per person, and

the rise is inflation.

Question 18 — Nonmarket transactions · Analysis

Answer: (A)

GDP counts output sold in markets. The cleaning is still produced, just as well, but it is no longer bought and sold, so it has become a nonmarket transaction and drops out of GDP. Measured GDP falls by the $4,000 the service used to be paid. This is the limitation MEA-1.B.1 names.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ It is unchanged production. GDP does not measure production that is not sold.
  • (C) ✗ Right direction, wrong reason. No less cleaning is done; it is simply no longer paid for.
  • (D) ✗ Unpaid household work is not counted however productive it is.
  • (E) ✗ Measured GDP falls, but the family is no worse off — it does its own cleaning instead of

paying for it. That gap is the limitation.

Question 19 — Nonmarket transactions · Synthesis

Answer: (D)

The reverse of question 18. The gardening done does not change — two gardens, tended as before — but it moves from nonmarket to market, so it starts being counted. Measured GDP rises; production does not.

  • (A) ✗ Volunteering is a nonmarket activity. It adds production but not measured GDP.
  • (B) ✗ Growing your own food replaces a market purchase with nonmarket production, so measured

GDP falls.

  • (C) ✗ Used goods are not counted, because they were counted when new. GDP does not change.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ It does raise GDP — inventories count as investment — but because goods really were

produced. The stem excludes that case.

Question 20 — The other limitations of GDP · Analysis

Answer: (E)

GDP measures the flow of new production, so the rebuilding counts. The destruction of existing homes is a loss of wealth, not of this year's production, so nothing is subtracted. Real GDP rises; the country is not better off than before the storm. The textbook's list (p. 115): real GDP "increases with expenditures on some things that make people unhappy, including … natural disasters."

  • (A) ✗ The error the limitation exists to prevent. The rebuilding restores what was lost.
  • (B) ✗ New construction is new production and is counted in full.
  • (C) ✗ The rebuilt houses are real output. Nothing in the stem is about prices.
  • (D) ✗ GDP never subtracts the destruction of existing capital. That is the asymmetry the right

answer describes.

  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 21 — The other limitations of GDP · Analysis

Answer: (B)

GDP values output at its price. The same 10 million phones at the same $800 give the same $8 billion this year as last, in current prices and in base-year prices alike. The phones really are better, and GDP records none of it. Changes in product quality are one of the limitations the CED's sample question lists (p. 135), and the textbook never mentions them.

  • (A) ✗ In the method of this module, output is quantities valued at prices, and neither changed.

Real GDP counts phones, not how good they are.

  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Nominal GDP is quantity × current price, and both are unchanged. There is no "value of the

improvement" in the calculation.

  • (D) ✗ Nothing got more expensive — the price is the same $800.
  • (E) ✗ Phones bought by households are consumption, and the stem gives no new spending of any

kind to classify.

Question 22 — The other limitations of GDP · Synthesis

Answer: (D)

The same real GDP per capita, but R produces it in 1,500 hours and S in 2,200. R's residents have far more leisure, and GDP does not count leisure. This is not a reason to prefer a different number on the exam — it is exactly what the framework means by a limitation of GDP.

  • (A) ✗ Reversed. Equal output in more hours means S produces less per hour.
  • (B) ✗ Reversed again. R produces the same output in fewer hours, so R's output per hour is

higher.

  • (C) ✗ Leisure is not bought or sold and is not in GDP at any price.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Nothing in the stem suggests hidden transactions. The difference in hours is fully

explained by a difference in productivity.

Question 23 — The whole topic · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

Nominal GDP is a record, but nominal GDP is how much is spent. Real GDP grows by the ratio of the two growth factors: 1.04 ÷ 1.06 = 0.981, a fall of about 1.9%. Spending rose because prices rose; output fell.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Treats nominal GDP as a quantity. A record nominal figure can come entirely from prices.
  • (C) ✗ Subtracts the wrong way round and gets the sign wrong. Prices rose faster than

spending.

  • (D) ✗ Adds inflation to nominal growth instead of removing it.
  • (E) ✗ The GDP deflator covers the prices of all final goods and services in GDP. The CPI is

the index limited to what consumers buy.

Question 24 — Chain-linking · Analysis

Answer: (C)

The textbook describes chain-linking as using "the average between the GDP growth rate calculated using an early base year and the GDP growth rate calculated using a late base year" (p. 115). The average of 15% and 15.4% is about 15.2%.

  • (A) ✗ That is the early-base-year figure, which is exactly what chain-linking replaces.
  • (B) ✗ The late-base-year figure. Neither year's prices is "more accurate"; the textbook says

neither answer is more correct.

  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Adds the two growth rates. That is not a growth rate at all.
  • (E) ✗ The difference is the size of the disagreement, not a growth rate.

Notes for the teacher

Content validity. All 15 concept entries appear across the 24 items. Entry 11, chain-linking, is the module's only background term and is tested once, in Section B. Questions 9 to 14 test material the textbook does not contain — the deflator (9–12) and the direction of the base-year error (13, 14) — and questions 18 and 19 test the word nonmarket, which the textbook never uses in this sense. That is eight of twenty-three Section A items on supplied content, and it is deliberate: they are the elements the CED names and the book omits, and the deflator is the first scored point of the CED's own sample FRQ.

The published sample question on what GDP counts is not repeated. The CED's sample MCQ 3 (p. 135) is the only published item that puts the limitations of GDP in front of a student — three of its four distractors are limitations — but the answer key tags it MEA-1.A, Topic 2.1, and its right answer is Module 10's inventory rule. The Module 10 test reproduces it (question 12 there). Question 21 here tests the distractor the textbook never mentions, product quality, instead.

Question 17 follows a published structure. It is the CED's sample FRQ 2 (pp. 139, 146) — nominal GDP, a deflator and a population, and "did the standard of living … increase, decrease, or stay the same?" — with new numbers. The scoring guideline's answer is the same: compute real GDP per capita, find it unchanged.

Bloom levels. Application 6 · Analysis 12 · Synthesis 6, a ratio of 1 : 2 : 1 against the standard's 1 : 3 : 1.75. The application share is high, and the reason is the topic: Topic 2.6's suggested skill is 1.C, identify … using quantitative data or calculations, and the CED's scored calculation points (Fehran a(i), a(ii)) are single-procedure tasks. Four of the fifteen calculation items are such tasks, set as application (6, 9, 11, 15); the other eleven combine a procedure with a judgement and are set higher. Application here means carrying out a stated procedure on new numbers; analysis means choosing which procedure the situation calls for; synthesis means combining two results to reach a conclusion the stem does not ask for directly.

Distractor design. Every wrong option encodes a documented error. The recurring families:

  • Nominal read as real — 1A, 3A, 4A, 7A, 12A, 17A, 23B
  • Real read as nominal — 1D, 2C, 6B
  • The subtraction shortcut — 2E, 7D, 12B, 23C
  • Deflator arithmetic — inverted (9A), the ×100 forgotten (5C, 9B, 11D), multiplied instead of

divided (11A, 23D), index points read as percent (10C, 9E)

  • Percentage change over the new value — 7B, 10D
  • Units and inversion in per capita — 15A, 15C, 15D, 15E
  • Size of an economy confused with output per person — 16A, 16B, 16D
  • The overall price level confused with relative prices — 13C, 13E, 14A, 14B
  • Nominal GDP per capita as the standard of living — 17E
  • GDP read as well-being — 18E, 20A, 22A

Right answer, wrong reasoning. Four options reach a defensible conclusion by a route that would fail on a written answer, and each is called out in its solution: 13D (right figures, wrong inference), 14C (right direction, wrong reason), 18C (right direction, wrong reason) and 19E (a real rise in GDP, but from real production).
