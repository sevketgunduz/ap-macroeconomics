Krugman's three stated objectives (p. 142):

  1. How the inflation rate is measured
  2. What a price index is and how it is calculated
  3. The importance of the consumer price index and other price indexes

All three are MEA-1.F. Objective 3's other price indexes include the PPI, which the framework excludes from calculation (below).

The essential knowledge statements, verbatim (CED p. 50):

MEA-1.F.1 — The consumer price index (CPI) measures the change in income a consumer would need in order to maintain the same standard of living over time under a new set of prices as under the original set of prices. MEA-1.F.2 — The CPI measures the cost of a fixed basket of goods and services in a given year relative to the base year. Exclusion statement: Calculating the producer price index (PPI) is beyond the scope of the course and AP Exam. MEA-1.F.3 — The inflation rate is determined by calculating the percentage change in a price index, such as CPI or the GDP deflator. MEA-1.G.1 — The CPI as a measure of inflation has some shortcomings, such as substitution bias, causing it to overstate the true inflation rate.

Two features of that wording matter.

MEA-1.F.1 defines the CPI as a cost-of-living measure; MEA-1.F.2 defines it as a fixed basket. The gap between those two definitions is MEA-1.G.1. A fixed basket is what the CPI actually prices; the income needed to keep the same standard of living is what it is meant to track; and because people change what they buy, the first overstates the second. Krugman makes the same argument (p. 144, the two families with $20,000 in 1985 and $40,000 in 2010) without naming it. Teach the two definitions side by side and the shortcoming falls out of them.

"Such as substitution bias" names one limitation and leaves the list open. The CED's Sample Instructional Activity 4 (p. 45), keyed to Topic 2.4, adds the second: "Discuss the limitations of the market basket, such as substitution and quality differences." Krugman's second reason is innovation — new goods (p. 145). Together they are the standard three: substitution, new goods, quality.

5. Corrections to make explicitly in class

Index points are not percentages

MCQ 2's trap, and the commonest error on the topic: from 80 to 120 is 40 points and 50%. Only when the earlier index is exactly 100 do the two coincide.

The base year's index is 100, not the base year's price

Students write the base-year basket cost ($95) as the index. The index is 100 by construction (p. 143); the dollar cost disappears into the ratio.

Krugman describes substitution bias without its name

The exam writes substitution bias (MEA-1.G.1). The book writes "consumers typically alter the mix of goods and services they buy" and never uses the phrase. Give students the name and the direction together: it makes the CPI overstate inflation.

An out-of-date basket can bias either way — but the CPI's net bias is upward

CYU 2(a)'s solution says an old basket with too few cars underestimates inflation. That is correct for that case, and it shows the mechanism is weighting, not a rule. The framework's statement is about the net effect of substitution, new goods and quality: the CPI overstates. Do not let CYU 2(a) leave students thinking the CPI understates.

The CPI is not the cost of everything

It prices a consumer basket for a typical urban family. The GDP deflator covers everything produced domestically, including investment and government goods, and no imports; the PPI covers producers' inputs. They usually move together (Figure 15.3) but answer different questions.

Do not calculate the PPI

The CED excludes it. Mention it as the "early warning signal" (p. 146) and move on.
