Section A

1. A country replaces its currency: 100 old pesos become 1 new peso. Every price, wage, debt and bank balance is converted at the same rate on the same day.

What is the effect?

  • (A) Everyone is poorer, because they hold fewer pesos.
  • (B) Real wages and real incomes are unchanged.
  • (C) The inflation rate falls by 99%.
  • (D) Borrowers gain, because their debts are smaller numbers.
  • (E) Everyone is richer, because prices are one-hundredth of what they were.

2. Over a year, a worker's wage rises by 3% and the price level rises by 5%.

What happened to the worker's real wage?

  • (A) It rose by about 8%.
  • (B) It rose by about 2%.
  • (C) It was unchanged.
  • (D) It fell by about 2%.
  • (E) It fell by about 5%.

3. A worker earned $18.00 an hour when the price index was 120. A year later she earns $19.80 an hour, and the price index is 132.

What is her real wage in base-year dollars in each year?

  • (A) $15 in both years, so her real wage is unchanged.
  • (B) $15 and then $16.50, so her real wage rose.
  • (C) $21.60 and then $26.14, so her real wage rose.
  • (D) 15 cents in both years.
  • (E) $18.00 and then $19.80, so her real wage rose by 10%.

4. A household's nominal income rises by 6% in a year in which the inflation rate is 2.5%.

By approximately how much did its real income change?

  • (A) +8.5%
  • (B) +6%
  • (C) +2.5%
  • (D) −3.5%
  • (E) +3.5%

5. A price index rises from 180 in one year to 189 in the next.

What is the inflation rate?

  • (A) 9%
  • (B) 4.8%
  • (C) 5%
  • (D) 189%
  • (E) 105%

6. A price index falls from 150 in one year to 147 in the next.

Which statement is correct?

  • (A) Inflation was 3%, and the economy experienced disinflation.
  • (B) Inflation was −2%, and the economy experienced deflation.
  • (C) Inflation was −3%, and the economy experienced deflation.
  • (D) Inflation was −2%, and the economy experienced disinflation.
  • (E) Inflation was 2%, and prices rose.

7. A country's inflation rate was 6% last year and is 3% this year.

Which statement is correct?

  • (A) Prices fell this year.
  • (B) The country is experiencing deflation.
  • (C) The price level is lower than it was a year ago.
  • (D) Real wages must have risen this year.
  • (E) Prices rose this year, but more slowly than last year.

8. A country's inflation rate is 5% in year 1, 2% in year 2 and −1% in year 3.

Which statement correctly describes years 2 and 3?

  • (A) Year 2 is deflation; year 3 is disinflation.
  • (B) Both years are deflation.
  • (C) Only year 3 is disinflation.
  • (D) Year 2 is disinflation; year 3 is deflation, and the price level fell that year.
  • (E) The price level fell in both years.

9. A savings account pays a nominal interest rate of 6%. Over the year, the inflation rate is 9%.

What real interest rate did the saver earn?

  • (A) −3%
  • (B) 3%
  • (C) 15%
  • (D) 6%
  • (E) 9%

10. A lender wants a real return of 3% on a one-year loan and expects inflation of 4%.

What nominal interest rate should she ask for?

  • (A) 1%
  • (B) 3%
  • (C) 7%
  • (D) 12%
  • (E) 4%

11. A bank lends at a nominal rate of 10%. When the loan is signed, both the bank and the borrower expect 7% inflation. Inflation turns out to be 7%.

Who gains from the inflation?

  • (A) The borrower, because inflation was high.
  • (B) Nobody gains, but the bank loses, because inflation cut the value of the repayment.
  • (C) Both lose, because high inflation is costly.
  • (D) The bank, because it earned 10%.
  • (E) Neither: the real rate is 3%, exactly as both planned.

12. A family took out a mortgage at a fixed 5% nominal rate when everyone expected 2% inflation. Inflation turns out to be 6% a year.

Which statement is correct?

  • (A) The lender gains; the real rate is 4%.
  • (B) The family gains; the real rate is −1% instead of the 3% expected.
  • (C) The family gains; the real rate is 3%.
  • (D) Neither gains, because the nominal rate is fixed.
  • (E) The lender gains; the real rate is −1%.

13. After a year of loans signed at fixed nominal rates, inflation turns out lower than borrowers and lenders expected.

What is the effect?

  • (A) Borrowers gain, because they pay less interest.
  • (B) Nobody is affected, because the nominal rates were fixed.
  • (C) Lenders lose, because inflation is lower.
  • (D) Lenders gain at the expense of borrowers.
  • (E) Both gain, because lower inflation is good for everyone.

14. Hector has a mortgage at a fixed 6% nominal rate, taken out five years ago. Inflation has crept up unexpectedly to 7%.

Which statement best describes the effect on the economy as a whole?

  • (A) No net cost: Hector's gain is the bank's loss, so inflation redistributes rather than destroys.
  • (B) A net cost: the bank must reprint its rate tables, a menu cost.
  • (C) A net cost: Hector makes more trips to the bank, a shoe-leather cost.
  • (D) Both Hector and the bank lose.
  • (E) The bank gains, because it receives more dollars.

15. Prices fall unexpectedly by 3% a year for several years. Most debts carry fixed nominal rates.

What is the effect on borrowers?

  • (A) They gain, because prices are falling.
  • (B) None, because their nominal payments are fixed.
  • (C) They lose, because the real value of their debts rises.
  • (D) They gain, because lenders' interest is worth less.
  • (E) They gain, because the real interest rate falls.

16. Two retirees each receive $2,000 a month. Ana's pension is fixed at $2,000. Ben's is indexed to the consumer price index. Inflation turns out unexpectedly high.

Which statement is correct?

  • (A) Both lose equally.
  • (B) Neither loses, because a pension is income, not a loan.
  • (C) Only Ben loses, because indexed pensions rise slowly.
  • (D) Both gain, because their nominal incomes are protected.
  • (E) Ana loses purchasing power; Ben is protected.

17. Workers sign a three-year contract with raises of 2% a year, when everyone expects 2% inflation. Inflation turns out to be 5% a year.

What happens to the workers' real wages, and who gains?

  • (A) Real wages rise by about 3% a year; the workers gain.
  • (B) Real wages fall by about 3% a year; the employer gains.
  • (C) Real wages are unchanged, because the raises were agreed.
  • (D) Real wages fall by about 5% a year; nobody gains.
  • (E) Real wages fall by about 3% a year; the workers and the employer both lose.

18. Inflation turns out much higher than people expected. Which of the following is most likely to gain?

  • (A) A saver who keeps cash at home.
  • (B) A retiree with a pension fixed in dollars.
  • (C) A bank holding fixed-rate mortgages.
  • (D) A homeowner with a fixed-rate mortgage.
  • (E) A worker whose wage is fixed for three years.

19. A $2,000 one-year loan is made at a nominal rate of 7%, when both sides expect 3% inflation. Inflation turns out to be 5%.

What were the planned and actual real interest rates, and roughly how much purchasing power moved, and to whom?

  • (A) Planned 4%, actual 2%; about $40 moved to the borrower.
  • (B) Planned 4%, actual 2%; about $40 moved to the lender.
  • (C) Planned 7%, actual 5%; about $40 moved to the borrower.
  • (D) Planned 4%, actual 2%; about $80 moved to the borrower.
  • (E) Planned 10%, actual 12%; the lender gained.

Section A — solutions

Question 1 — Inflation and the price level · Analysis

Answer: (B)

This is Krugman's "new dollar" (pp. 134–135): divide every price and every income by the same number and nobody is richer or poorer. "The level of prices doesn't matter."

  • (A) ✗ Fewer pesos, but each buys a hundred times as much. Purchasing power is unchanged.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Inflation is the rate at which prices change. A one-off relabelling changes no rate.
  • (D) ✗ Debts shrink by the same factor as incomes. Their real burden is unchanged.
  • (E) ✗ Incomes fall by the same factor as prices.

Question 2 — Real wage and real income · Application

Answer: (D)

The real wage is the wage divided by the price level. With the wage up 3% and prices up 5%, the real wage changes by about 3 − 5 = −2%.

  • (A) ✗ Adds inflation to the raise instead of subtracting it.
  • (B) ✗ The subtraction run backwards: prices rose faster than the wage.
  • (C) ✗ The real wage is unchanged only if the wage keeps pace with prices.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Ignores the 3% raise.

Question 3 — Calculating changes in real variables · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Real wage = nominal wage ÷ (price index ÷ 100). Year 1: 18.00 ÷ 1.20 = $15. Year 2: 19.80 ÷ 1.32 = $15. Her wage rose 10% and so did prices.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Divides the second year's wage by the first year's index: 19.80 ÷ 1.20 = $16.50.
  • (C) ✗ Multiplies by the index instead of dividing: 18 × 1.2 = $21.60.
  • (D) ✗ Divides by the index itself, not the index over 100: 18 ÷ 120 = $0.15.
  • (E) ✗ Those are the nominal wages.

Question 4 — Calculating changes in real variables · Analysis

Answer: (E)

Percentage change in real income ≈ percentage change in nominal income − inflation rate = 6 − 2.5 = 3.5%.

  • (A) ✗ Adds inflation instead of subtracting it.
  • (B) ✗ That is the nominal change.
  • (C) ✗ That is the inflation rate.
  • (D) ✗ The subtraction run backwards.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 5 — The inflation rate · Application

Answer: (C)

(189 − 180) ÷ 180 × 100 = 9 ÷ 180 × 100 = 5%.

  • (A) ✗ The change in index points, not the percentage change. It is 9% only when the base is 100.
  • (B) ✗ Divides by the second year's value: 9 ÷ 189.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ The index itself.
  • (E) ✗ 189 ÷ 180 × 100: the ratio, not the change.

Question 6 — The inflation rate and deflation · Analysis

Answer: (B)

(147 − 150) ÷ 150 × 100 = −3 ÷ 150 × 100 = −2%. A negative inflation rate means the price level fell: deflation.

  • (A) ✗ Wrong sign, and disinflation means prices still rising.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ The fall in index points, not the percentage.
  • (D) ✗ The rate is right. A falling price level is deflation, not disinflation.
  • (E) ✗ The index fell, so prices fell.

Question 7 — Disinflation · Analysis

Answer: (E)

Disinflation is "the process of bringing the inflation rate down" (p. 139). At 3% the price level is still rising, only more slowly than at 6%.

  • (A) ✗ A positive inflation rate means prices rose.
  • (B) ✗ Deflation requires a negative inflation rate.
  • (C) ✗ The price level is 3% higher than a year ago.
  • (D) ✗ Real wages depend on wages too, which the stem does not give.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 8 — Deflation against disinflation · Synthesis

Answer: (D)

Year 2: inflation fell from 5% to 2%, still positive — disinflation. Year 3: inflation is negative — the price level fell — deflation.

  • (A) ✗ The two labels swapped.
  • (B) ✗ Prices rose 2% in year 2.
  • (C) ✗ Year 2 is the disinflation; year 3 has gone below zero.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ The price level rose in year 2.

Question 9 — Nominal and real interest rates · Application

Answer: (A)

Real = nominal − inflation = 6 − 9 = −3%. The saver's balance buys less at the end of the year than at the start.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ The subtraction run backwards.
  • (C) ✗ Adds the two rates.
  • (D) ✗ That is the nominal rate.
  • (E) ✗ That is the inflation rate.

Question 10 — Expected inflation and the real interest rate · Analysis

Answer: (C)

The nominal rate is roughly the real rate wanted plus the inflation expected: 3 + 4 = 7%.

  • (A) ✗ Subtracts where it should add.
  • (B) ✗ Asking only the real rate leaves nothing for inflation.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Multiplies the two rates.
  • (E) ✗ Covers inflation but earns no real return.

Question 11 — Expected inflation and the real interest rate · Analysis

Answer: (E)

Planned real rate = 10 − 7 = 3%. Actual real rate = 10 − 7 = 3%. Inflation was exactly what both expected, so it was already in the nominal rate and moves nothing.

  • (A) ✗ High inflation helps borrowers only when it is higher than expected.
  • (B) ✗ The bank allowed for 7% when it set 10%.
  • (C) ✗ Expected inflation creates no winners or losers between these two.
  • (D) ✗ The bank's real return is 3%, as planned. Nominal earnings are not gains.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 12 — Unexpected inflation: borrowers gain, lenders lose · Analysis

Answer: (B)

Planned real rate = 5 − 2 = 3%. Actual real rate = 5 − 6 = −1%. "If the actual inflation rate is higher than expected, borrowers gain at the expense of lenders" (p. 138).

  • (A) ✗ The direction is backwards, and 4% is not any of the rates here.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ The family gains, but 3% is the rate they expected, not the one they paid.
  • (D) ✗ The nominal rate is fixed; its real value is not.
  • (E) ✗ The rate is right; at −1% the lender loses.

Question 13 — Unexpected inflation: borrowers gain, lenders lose · Analysis

Answer: (D)

"If the inflation rate is lower than expected, lenders will gain at the expense of borrowers" (p. 138): repayments are worth more than planned.

  • (A) ✗ They pay the same nominal interest, and it is worth more than they planned.
  • (B) ✗ The fixed nominal rate is exactly why the surprise moves purchasing power.
  • (C) ✗ Backwards: lower inflation raises the real rate lenders receive.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Redistribution has a loser for every winner.

Question 14 — Unexpected inflation: borrowers gain, lenders lose · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

The review's FRQ 1(c), rubric on p. 141: "There is no net cost to the economy. Hector gains and the bank loses because the money Hector pays back is worth less than expected." Unexpected inflation redistributes wealth — the framework's word.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Nothing in the stem involves changing listed prices.
  • (C) ✗ Nothing in the stem involves avoiding holding money.
  • (D) ✗ Hector gains: he repays in cheaper dollars.
  • (E) ✗ More dollars, but each worth less. In real terms the bank loses.

Question 15 — Unexpected deflation · Analysis

Answer: (C)

Krugman (p. 139): "unexpected deflation — a surprise fall in the price level — creates winners and losers, too"; in 1929–33 debtors "saw a sharp rise in the real value of their debts."

  • (A) ✗ Falling prices make each dollar they repay worth more.
  • (B) ✗ Fixed nominal payments are what deflation makes heavier.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Backwards: interest received is worth more in deflation.
  • (E) ✗ The real rate rises when prices fall.

Question 16 — Fixed incomes, savers and contracts · Analysis

Answer: (E)

Ana is owed a fixed number of dollars every month — a lender in disguise — so unexpected inflation cuts what her pension buys. Ben's indexed pension rises with the price index (Module 15, p. 146).

  • (A) ✗ Indexing is what separates them.
  • (B) ✗ Anyone owed fixed dollars loses from unexpected inflation, whether or not it is a loan.
  • (C) ✗ An indexed pension keeps pace with prices; that is its purpose.
  • (D) ✗ Ana's nominal income is fixed, not protected.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 17 — Fixed incomes, savers and contracts · Synthesis

Answer: (B)

Real wage change ≈ 2 − 5 = −3% a year. The employer pays fixed raises while the prices it charges rise faster: the workers' loss is its gain.

  • (A) ✗ The subtraction run backwards.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ The nominal raises were agreed; their real value was not.
  • (D) ✗ Ignores the 2% raises, and redistribution always has a winner.
  • (E) ✗ The employer gains what the workers lose.

Question 18 — Who gains · Synthesis

Answer: (D)

Unexpected inflation helps anyone who owes fixed dollars and hurts anyone owed them. The homeowner owes fixed dollars; the other four are each owed them.

  • (A) ✗ Cash loses purchasing power, and pays no interest to make up for it.
  • (B) ✗ Owed fixed dollars every month.
  • (C) ✗ The lender on those mortgages.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Paid fixed dollars while prices rise.

Question 19 — The whole loan · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

Planned real rate = 7 − 3 = 4%. Actual real rate = 7 − 5 = 2%. The lender expected a real return of 4% of $2,000 and got 2%: about 2% × $2,000 = $40 of purchasing power moved to the borrower.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ The rates are right; higher-than-expected inflation helps the borrower.
  • (C) ✗ Those are the nominal rate and the actual inflation rate. Neither is a real rate.
  • (D) ✗ Uses the planned real rate, 4% of $2,000, instead of the 2-point gap.
  • (E) ✗ Adds inflation to the nominal rate.

Section B — solutions

Question 20 — Why disinflation is costly · Analysis

Answer: (C)

Krugman (p. 139): disinflation "is very difficult and costly once a higher rate of inflation has become well established." The 1981–82 disinflation came with 10.8% unemployment.

  • (A) ✗ The textbook finds little evidence that 2% to 5% does great harm in itself.
  • (B) ✗ Inflation does not make everyone poorer; the textbook argues the opposite.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ At low inflation menu costs are small.
  • (E) ✗ Deflation is a falling price level.

Question 21 — Shoe-leather costs and hyperinflation · Application

Answer: (B)

"Increased costs of transactions caused by inflation are known as shoe-leather costs" (p. 137). They are largest in hyperinflations, like Germany's in 1921–23.

  • (A) ✗ Menu costs are about changing listed prices.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Unit-of-account costs are about money as a measuring rod.
  • (D) ✗ The time spent is a real cost, not a transfer between groups.
  • (E) ✗ Not a cost the textbook names.

Question 22 — Menu costs · Application

Answer: (E)

"Menu costs are the real costs of changing listed prices" (p. 137).

  • (A) ✗ Shoe-leather costs come from avoiding holding money.
  • (B) ✗ Unit-of-account costs come from money being a less reliable measure.
  • (C) ✗ Reprinting uses up real resources; it is not a transfer.
  • (D) ✗ Not a cost the textbook names.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 23 — Unit-of-account costs · Analysis

Answer: (C)

$50,000 grown by 6% inflation is $53,000: the sale price buys no more than the purchase price did. But the tax system counts the $3,000 nominal gain — Krugman's phantom gain (p. 137).

  • (A) ✗ The $3,000 is only inflation; there is no real gain.
  • (B) ✗ No real gain, and the nominal gain is taxed.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ The sale exactly kept pace with inflation; there is no real loss either.
  • (E) ✗ Adds inflation to the gain.

Question 24 — Uncertainty and long-term contracts · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Krugman (p. 139): "In countries with high and uncertain inflation, long-term loans are rare." A lender cannot tell what real rate a fixed contract will earn.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Locking in a nominal rate is exactly the risk lenders refuse.
  • (C) ✗ Lenders lose whenever inflation comes in above expectations.
  • (D) ✗ Real rates go negative whenever inflation beats the nominal rate.
  • (E) ✗ Nothing here bears on unemployment.

Notes for the teacher

Content validity. All 17 concept entries appear across the 24 items. The six background entries — the cost of disinflation, the three named costs, uncertainty and hyperinflation — are tested in Section B, hyperinflation together with shoe-leather costs in question 21. That puts the textbook's most prominent section, the three named costs, in the separately scored part of the paper: the framework never names them. Questions 3, 4 and 16–18 test material the textbook does not contain — calculating a change in a real variable, and the groups beyond borrowers and lenders that the CED's own activity names.
