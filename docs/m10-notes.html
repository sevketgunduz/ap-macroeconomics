The headline. Module 10 teaches everything Topic 2.1 requires — and names almost none of it. The framework's three statements (MEA-1.A.1–A.3, CED p. 46) ask for GDP as final output, the circular flow as a flow of income and expenditure, and the expenditures, income and value-added approaches. Krugman delivers all three in substance, with a worked three-firm economy (Figure 10.3) that proves the approaches agree. But the three approach names occur 0 times in the entire book, final output occurs 0 times, and the learning objective's verb — "calculate nominal GDP" — meets a module in which nominal occurs 0 times. The teaching problem is labels, not content. Four of them have to be supplied; section 3 lists them.

The enduring understanding, verbatim (CED p. 46):

MEA-1 — "An economy's performance can be measured by different indicators such as gross domestic product (GDP), the inflation rate, and the unemployment rate."

Krugman's three stated objectives (p. 102):

  1. How economists use aggregate measures to track the performance of the economy
  2. The circular flow diagram of the economy
  3. What gross domestic product, or GDP, is and the three ways of calculating it

All three are on-syllabus; the third is MEA-1.A.1 and MEA-1.A.3 word for word, apart from the names of the three ways.

Verdict on alignment. Strong in substance, weak in vocabulary, and nothing off-syllabus except the opening page on national accounts. Every figure in the module serves MEA-1.A, and the free-response item Krugman prints is almost exactly the kind of "is it in GDP?" question the CED's own sample question asks (section 4). Teach from the book and write the framework's labels on top of it.

The knowledge elements, in the CED's own words

Learning objective MEA-1.A (CED p. 46):

a. Define (using the circular flow diagram as appropriate) how GDP is measured and its components. b. Calculate nominal GDP.
MEA-1.A.1 — "GDP is a measure of final output of the economy." (CED p. 46)
MEA-1.A.2 — "GDP as a total flow of income and expenditure can be represented by the circular flow diagram." (CED p. 46)
MEA-1.A.3 — "There are three ways of measuring GDP: the expenditures approach, the income approach, and the value-added approach." (CED p. 46)

Three sentences, and each one carries a word the book never prints.

"Final output" occurs 0 times in Krugman and twice in the CED (pp. 46, 150). Krugman's phrase is final goods and services. They mean the same thing; say so once and move on.

"A total flow of income and expenditure." MEA-1.A.2 is the reason the circular flow is on the syllabus: the diagram is examinable because it shows that every dollar spent in product markets is a dollar of income in factor markets. Krugman states the principle — "the flow of money into each market or sector is equal to the flow of money coming out" (p. 102) — and his solutions to Check Your Understanding 1 say it explicitly. Draw the diagram as the picture of that identity, not as a map to memorise.

"The expenditures approach, the income approach, and the value-added approach." Counted across all 927 pages of the book:

Framework nameIn the bookKrugman's description (p. 106)Module 10 exhibit

Krugman even orders them differently: production (value added) first, spending second, income third, and he says he "won't emphasize the income method as much as the other two" (p. 108). The framework lists expenditures first. Follow the framework's order and names; use the book's figures.

5. Corrections to make explicitly in class

Total sales is not GDP

The whole of MEA-1.A.1 in one number: Figure 10.3's firms sell $34,700 of goods and GDP is $21,500. The $13,200 difference is intermediate goods counted again — $4,200 of ore inside the steel, $9,000 of steel inside the car. Put both numbers on the board and ask where the gap went.

"Investment" means new physical capital, not buying shares

Students arrive with the personal-finance meaning. In the national accounts, buying a share is a transfer of ownership and not in GDP; building a factory, buying a machine, building a new house and adding to inventories are investment spending and are. FRQ 1(a) and 1(c) are the contrast.

Unsold output is still counted

Books that do not sell go into inventory, and additions to inventories are investment spending, so they are in GDP this year (FRQ 1(f)). When they sell next year, the sale is offset by the fall in inventories. This is also why the three approaches always agree (CYU 1).

Transfers are not government purchases

A Social Security cheque or unemployment insurance payment buys nothing, so it is not G and not in GDP. The government's purchase of chalk and teachers' services is.

Imports are subtracted because they were already counted

C, I and G include spending on foreign goods. Subtracting IM removes production that happened elsewhere; it is not a claim that imports are bad for GDP. Krugman's "leaked across national borders" (p. 108) is the right image.

Used goods and financial assets: no new production

A used car was counted when it was new; a stock or bond is a claim, not an output. Both are out. The dealer's service in selling a used car is new production — use it as the extension question for strong students.

Say "nominal"

Every GDP in this module is at current prices. Use the word from the first lesson, so that Module 11's real GDP arrives as a correction to something named rather than as a new idea.
