Section A — examinable content (Questions 1–20)

1. A commentator writes: "The last three U.S. recessions began roughly seven years apart, so with the current expansion in its eighth year we are overdue for a downturn." Which of the following is the strongest objection?

  • (A) Recessions are dated by an independent panel, so the commentator has no authority to date them.
  • (B) The last three recessions were unusually mild, so the pattern says nothing about severity.
  • (C) The business cycle is an alternation, not a regular oscillation; postwar expansions have run from 18 months to over ten years, so elapsed time is not itself a cause.
  • (D) Expansions always last exactly as long as the recessions that precede them, so the relevant figure is the length of the last recession.
  • (E) Business cycles apply to individual industries rather than to the economy as a whole.

2. In Country Z, aggregate output fell for one quarter, rose slightly for the next, then fell sharply for two more. Employment fell in three of the four quarters. Under the approach the United States actually uses, how would this most likely be classified?

  • (A) Not a recession, because output did not fall for two consecutive quarters.
  • (B) A recession, because a panel of experts weighing employment and production across the whole period would judge it one.
  • (C) Not a recession, because employment is irrelevant to the definition.
  • (D) A depression, because the downturn spanned four quarters.
  • (E) An expansion, because output rose in one of the four quarters.

3. In Country Y the unemployment rate is 9.4% and has fallen in each of the last five months. Aggregate output has risen in each of those months. Which phase is Country Y in?

  • (A) A recession, because 9.4% unemployment is high.
  • (B) A trough, because the rate is still close to its worst level.
  • (C) Neither phase, because the economy has not yet returned to its previous output level.
  • (D) An expansion, because output and employment are both rising.
  • (E) A depression, because unemployment remains in single digits but above 9%.

4. A student is given a business-cycle diagram and asked to label a peak, a trough, a recession and an expansion. Which statement describes the labelling correctly?

  • (A) The peak and the trough are single points on the curve; the recession and the expansion are stretches of the curve between them.
  • (B) All four are stretches of the curve, differing only in direction.
  • (C) The peak and the trough are stretches at the top and bottom of the curve; the recession and expansion are the points where direction changes.
  • (D) The peak is the whole upper half of the curve and the trough the whole lower half.
  • (E) All four are single points, since each marks one moment in the economy's history.

5. At the peak of a long expansion, a finance minister says: "Output is at its highest level in our history, so the economy is in its healthiest possible state." Which of the following best explains why an economist would object?

  • (A) Output at a peak is always below what the economy could sustainably produce, so there is unused capacity.
  • (B) A peak is a period rather than a moment, so no single claim about it can be correct.
  • (C) The unemployment rate is at its highest at a peak, which contradicts the claim.
  • (D) Peaks are determined only after the fact, so the minister cannot know the economy is at one.
  • (E) At a peak actual output is typically above potential output — a rate of production the economy cannot sustain, which is why the next phase is a recession.

6. In a small economy: 620,000 people work for pay; 40,000 have no job and applied for work last month; 55,000 are retired; 90,000 are full-time students with no job and no search; 30,000 are under working age. What is the labor force?

  • (A) 620,000
  • (B) 835,000
  • (C) 715,000
  • (D) 660,000
  • (E) 805,000

7. Four residents of the same town describe their situation. Which one is counted as unemployed?

  • (A) A woman who left her job eight months ago to care for a parent and has not looked for work since.
  • (B) A man laid off in March who has applied for six jobs in the last two weeks.
  • (C) A 16-year-old who would like a Saturday job but is not old enough to be counted in the adult population.
  • (D) A retired engineer who does occasional unpaid work for a charity.
  • (E) A graduate who says she would take a job if one were offered but stopped applying in January.

8. A country reports 132 million employed, 8 million unemployed, and an adult population of 210 million. What is its unemployment rate, to one decimal place?

  • (A) 3.8%
  • (B) 6.1%
  • (C) 5.7%
  • (D) 66.7%
  • (E) 4.0%

9. Over one year, the number of employed people in Country W rose by 400,000 and the unemployment rate also rose. Which of the following is consistent with both facts?

  • (A) The labor force grew by more than 400,000, so the number of unemployed rose as well.
  • (B) This combination is impossible; if employment rises the rate must fall.
  • (C) Aggregate output must have fallen during the year.
  • (D) The adult population must have fallen.
  • (E) Discouraged workers must have left the labor force.

10. In Country V the unemployment rate fell from 8.0% to 7.2% over six months. Employment did not change at all over those six months. Which of the following must have happened?

  • (A) Aggregate output rose, since a falling unemployment rate always accompanies rising output.
  • (B) The adult population fell.
  • (C) Firms replaced full-time posts with part-time ones, which are not counted as employment.
  • (D) The natural rate of unemployment fell.
  • (E) Some unemployed people stopped searching for work and so left the labor force.

11. Using the same country as Question 8 — 132 million employed, 8 million unemployed, adult population 210 million — what is the labor force participation rate, to one decimal place?

  • (A) 62.9%
  • (B) 66.7%
  • (C) 5.7%
  • (D) 3.8%
  • (E) 33.3%

12. A government announces that the unemployment rate has fallen for a fourth consecutive month. A journalist wants one further statistic to judge whether the labour market has genuinely improved. Which should she ask for, and why?

  • (A) Aggregate output, because output and unemployment always move together.
  • (B) The number of unemployed people, because a rate can be misleading.
  • (C) The adult population, because the unemployment rate is a share of it.
  • (D) The labor force participation rate: if it has fallen alongside the unemployment rate, people have left the labour market rather than found work.
  • (E) The depth of the last recession, because recovery should be judged against it.

13. A country's factories produce exactly the same number of cars, tonnes of steel and haircuts this year as last year, but every price in the economy is 6% higher. What has happened to aggregate output?

  • (A) It has risen 6%, because the value of what was produced is 6% higher.
  • (B) It has risen, but by less than 6%, because some prices rose more than others.
  • (C) It is unchanged, because aggregate output is a quantity of goods and services, not a sum of money.
  • (D) It has fallen 6%, because higher prices reduce what people can buy.
  • (E) It cannot be determined without knowing the population.

14. Country U's potential output this year is $19.50 trillion. Its actual output is $18.72 trillion. What is the output gap, expressed as a percentage of potential output?

  • (A) −4.0%
  • (B) −0.78%
  • (C) +4.0%
  • (D) −4.2%
  • (E) −7.8%

15. Country T's actual output has been running above its potential output for two years. Which of the following best describes its position?

  • (A) The economy is producing more than it can sustain, which is the state typically found near a peak and which precedes a downturn.
  • (B) This is impossible: potential output is the maximum an economy can produce.
  • (C) The country is in a recession, since output above potential cannot be sold.
  • (D) Unemployment must be zero, since all resources are in use.
  • (E) The country has permanently raised its productive capacity, which is what economic growth means.

16. Over one year, the price of coffee in Country S triples after a drought, while the prices of almost all other goods and services are steady. Which of the following is correct?

  • (A) Country S has experienced inflation, because a price rose substantially.
  • (B) Country S has not experienced inflation; the opportunity cost of buying coffee has risen relative to other goods, but the overall price level has barely moved.
  • (C) Country S has experienced deflation, because consumers can now afford less coffee.
  • (D) Country S has experienced inflation only if coffee is in the consumer price index.
  • (E) Nothing can be said without knowing whether wages rose.

17. A student writes: "The cost of inflation is that people are discouraged from holding cash, because cash loses value as prices rise." An examiner marking against the AP Macroeconomics framework would judge this answer to be

  • (A) correct and complete, since it is the cost the textbook gives.
  • (B) incorrect, because inflation does not affect the value of cash.
  • (C) incorrect, because that is a cost of deflation rather than of inflation.
  • (D) correct, because any true statement about inflation earns the point.
  • (E) economically sound but not the cost the framework requires, which is that unexpected inflation arbitrarily redistributes wealth between groups, such as from lenders to borrowers.

18. A central bank states that its inflation target is 2% a year rather than 0%. Which of the following best explains the choice?

  • (A) Price stability means the price level changing slowly rather than not at all, and a small positive rate keeps a safe distance from deflation, which discourages investment.
  • (B) At 0% the price level would never change, which would prevent any economic growth.
  • (C) A 2% target is easier to hit than 0%, so the bank is more likely to meet it.
  • (D) Inflation of 2% raises real wages each year, which is the bank's objective.
  • (E) A 0% target would require the bank to abandon monetary policy entirely.

19. Country R's real GDP per capita rose from $48,000 to $49,200 over one year. What was its rate of economic growth over that year?

  • (A) 0.25%
  • (B) 1.2%
  • (C) 2.4%
  • (D) 12.0%
  • (E) 2.5%

20. Over four years, Country Q's aggregate output fell 5% in year 1, then rose 2%, 2% and 1% in years 2 to 4. Its population was unchanged and its price level was unchanged throughout. Which statement best describes what happened?

  • (A) Four years of economic growth, since output rose in three of them.
  • (B) A recession followed by economic growth, because output ended above its year-1 level.
  • (C) Economic growth throughout, since real GDP per capita is what matters and prices did not change.
  • (D) A recession in year 1 and a depression thereafter, because the recovery was so slow.
  • (E) A recession followed by an expansion; the economy has roughly recovered its earlier output, which is not the same as an increase in what it can produce.

Section B — background terms (Questions 21–23)

These three terms carry no AP Macroeconomics essential knowledge statement. Score them separately.

21. A country's output falls by 3% over nine months and recovers within a year. A newspaper calls it "a depression". Which response is most accurate?

  • (A) Correct, since any period of falling output may be called a depression.
  • (B) Not accurate: a depression is a very deep and prolonged downturn, and although no numerical threshold exists, a 3% fall recovered within a year is a recession by any reasonable reading.
  • (C) Not accurate, because a depression is formally defined as a fall in output of more than 10%.
  • (D) Correct, because a depression is simply the contraction phase of the business cycle.
  • (E) Impossible to judge, because economists have never used the word since the 1930s.

22. A student objects to the production possibilities curve on the grounds that "no real economy produces only two goods". Which response is best?

  • (A) The objection is decisive; the model should be replaced with one using realistic assumptions.
  • (B) The objection is irrelevant; a model's assumptions are never meant to be examined.
  • (C) The objection is correct but unimportant, because economists know the model is wrong and use it anyway.
  • (D) The assumption is deliberately unrealistic, which is what makes a trade-off visible as a measurable curve; the test of a model is whether the simplification changes the answer to the question being asked.
  • (E) The objection is mistaken, because some small economies do produce only two goods.

23. An exam question states: "Other things equal, a decrease in household income reduces consumer spending." A student answers that spending might not fall, because interest rates could drop at the same time and encourage borrowing. How should this answer be assessed?

  • (A) Correct: the student has recognised that several variables change at once in a real economy.
  • (B) Correct, provided the student states which interest rate is meant.
  • (C) Incorrect: "other things equal" instructs the student to hold interest rates and every other relevant factor constant so that the effect of the income change alone can be identified.
  • (D) Incorrect, because interest rates do not affect consumer spending.
  • (E) Partly correct: the student should receive half credit for identifying a second channel.

Solutions — every option explained

Question 1 — Business cycle · Analysis

Answer: (C)

The business cycle is the short-run alternation between downturns and upturns. "Cycle" claims that the two states follow one another, not that they follow a timetable. Postwar U.S. expansions have run from 18 months to more than ten years, so elapsed time carries no information about when the next turn comes.

  • (A) ✗ True that the NBER dates recessions, but dating authority is not the flaw in the argument. A commentator may reason about timing without dating anything.
  • (B) ✗ Severity is a different question from timing, and the stem makes no claim about severity.
  • (C) ✓ Correct. Alternation is not periodicity, and the observed range of expansion lengths is the evidence.
  • (D) ✗ Invented regularity, and a stronger version of the error in the stem. No such relationship exists.
  • (E) ✗ Backwards. Business cycles are defined for the economy as a whole; that is what distinguishes them from one industry's ups and downs.

Question 2 — Recession · Analysis

Answer: (B)

Many countries use the two-consecutive-quarters rule of thumb. The United States does not: an independent panel at the National Bureau of Economic Research weighs several indicators, chiefly employment and production, and makes a judgement. Three falling quarters out of four with employment falling in three would very likely be judged a recession.

  • (A) ✗ Applies the rule the question explicitly excludes. This is the most attractive distractor and is exactly the case Krugman's FYI box uses to argue against the rule.
  • (B) ✓ Correct. Judgement across indicators, not a mechanical rule.
  • (C) ✗ Employment is half the definition — "output and employment are falling" — and is one of the panel's two main focuses.
  • (D) ✗ A depression is a very deep and prolonged downturn. Four quarters is neither.
  • (E) ✗ One rising quarter inside a falling year does not make an expansion; the direction over the period is down.

Question 3 — Expansion · Analysis

Answer: (D)

An expansion is defined by the direction of output and employment, not their level. Both are rising, so the economy is expanding — however unpleasant 9.4% unemployment is.

  • (A) ✗ Tests the level instead of the direction. This is the most common error on this term, and the 1930s recovery is the standard counterexample: output rose from 1933 while joblessness stayed high.
  • (B) ✗ A trough is the single moment at which the direction reverses. Five months of rising output puts the economy past it.
  • (C) ✗ There is no third phase between recession and expansion. Returning to a previous level is not part of the definition.
  • (D) ✓ Correct. Both variables rising is the definition of an expansion.
  • (E) ✗ A depression is a very deep and prolonged downturn, and output here is rising. The reasoning about single digits is irrelevant in any case.

Question 4 — Peak and trough · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Phases are periods; turning points are moments. A recession and an expansion each occupy a stretch of time with a length, and a peak and a trough are the single points at which the direction reverses. On a diagram the phases are segments of the curve and the turning points are dots.

  • (A) ✓ Correct. Two dots, two segments.
  • (B) ✗ Makes all four periods, which leaves no way to mark where one phase ends and the next begins.
  • (C) ✗ Exactly inverted. This is the error to expect from a student who has never seen the four labelled together, which is every student taught only from Module 2 — trough appears nowhere in the textbook.
  • (D) ✗ Halves of a curve are not phases, and this reading makes the peak and the recession the same thing.
  • (E) ✗ Makes all four moments, which leaves the cycle with no duration and contradicts the postwar averages.

Question 5 — Peak and trough · Synthesis

Answer: (E)

Near a peak actual output typically runs above potential output — the level the economy can sustain with its resources fully employed. Producing above that rate is not sustainable, and the downturn that follows is what the peak marks the start of. Highest is not healthiest.

  • (A) ✗ Reverses the sign. Unused capacity is the trough's condition, not the peak's.
  • (B) ✗ A peak is a moment, not a period — and even if it were a period, that would not make claims about it incorrect.
  • (C) ✗ Unemployment is at or near its lowest at a peak. The statement contradicts the claim by asserting the opposite of the fact.
  • (D) ✓ Reaches a defensible conclusion — peaks are indeed dated after the fact — by reasoning that misses the point. The minister's error is about what a peak is, not about when it can be identified. A student choosing D has a true fact and the wrong argument.
  • (E) ✓ Correct. Above potential, hence unsustainable, hence the turning point.

Question 6 — Employment, unemployment and the labor force · Analysis

Answer: (D)

Labor force = employment + unemployment = 620,000 + 40,000 = 660,000. The retired, the full-time students who are not searching, and those below working age are all outside the labor force.

  • (A) ✗ 620,000 is employment alone. The unemployed are in the labor force too.
  • (B) ✗ 835,000 adds the retired and the students: 620 + 40 + 55 + 90 + 30 = 835 thousand. This is "everyone", which is the population, not the labor force.
  • (C) ✗ 715,000 adds the retired to the labor force (660 + 55). Retirement puts a person outside it.
  • (D) ✓ Correct. Employed plus actively searching, and nothing else.
  • (E) ✗ 805,000 is everyone of working age (835 − 30). Being old enough to work is not the test; working or searching is. This is the error behind option B of the textbook's own MCQ 2.

Question 7 — Employment, unemployment and the labor force · Analysis

Answer: (B)

Unemployment requires two conditions together: no job, and actively looking for one. Only the man laid off in March, with six applications in the last fortnight, satisfies both.

  • (A) ✗ No job and no search. She is outside the labor force — caring for a family member is one of the standard reasons for being out of it.
  • (B) ✓ Correct. Jobless and actively searching.
  • (C) ✗ Below working age, so outside the adult population and outside the labor force, whatever he would like.
  • (D) ✗ Unpaid voluntary work is neither employment nor a job search. He is retired and outside the labor force.
  • (E) ✗ The classic discouraged worker: willing but no longer searching, so counted outside the labor force rather than as unemployed. This is the limitation MEA-1.D.1 names, and the reason the measured rate understates joblessness.

Question 8 — Unemployment rate · Application (numeric)

Answer: (C)

Labor force = 132 + 8 = 140 million. Unemployment rate = 8 ÷ 140 × 100 = 5.7%.

  • (A) ✗ 8 ÷ 210 = 3.8%: divides by the adult population. The single most expensive error in Unit 2, and the reason the population figure is in the stem.
  • (B) ✗ 8 ÷ 132 = 6.1%: divides by the employed rather than the labor force.
  • (C) ✓ Correct. 8 ÷ 140 = 0.0571.
  • (D) ✗ 140 ÷ 210 = 66.7%: this is the labor force participation rate, the answer to Question 11.
  • (E) ✗ 4.0% is a rounded version of the population error in A, included to catch students who divide by 210 and then round generously.

Question 9 — Unemployment rate · Analysis

Answer: (A)

The rate is a ratio. Employment can rise while the rate rises, provided the labor force grows faster — new entrants who do not immediately find work raise the number of unemployed and the denominator at once. This is normal in a strong recovery, when people re-enter the labour market.

  • (A) ✓ Correct. More entrants than new jobs raises both employment and the rate.
  • (B) ✗ Treats the rate as though employment were its only input. It has two, and the denominator moves.
  • (C) ✗ Output and unemployment usually move oppositely, but nothing here requires output to have fallen; employment rose.
  • (D) ✗ The adult population is the participation rate's denominator, not the unemployment rate's. It plays no part.
  • (E) ✗ Discouraged workers leaving the labor force would push the rate down, not up. Right mechanism, wrong direction.

Question 10 — Unemployment rate · Synthesis

Answer: (E)

With employment flat, the rate can only fall if the number of unemployed falls — and since nobody became employed, those people must have left the labor force by giving up the search. Numerator and denominator both fall, the rate drops, and nothing has improved.

  • (A) ✗ Asserts a rule that does not hold here. Output may well have been flat; the arithmetic of the rate does not require it to have risen.
  • (B) ✗ The adult population is not in the unemployment-rate formula at all.
  • (C) ✗ Part-time work counts as employment, so such a switch would leave employment unchanged and the rate unchanged.
  • (D) ✗ The natural rate is a long-run concept about frictional and structural unemployment; it does not move the measured rate over six months.
  • (E) ✓ Correct. The discouraged-worker effect, and the reason the participation rate has to be read alongside.

Question 11 — Labor force participation rate · Application (numeric)

Answer: (B)

Labor force = 132 + 8 = 140 million. Participation rate = 140 ÷ 210 × 100 = 66.7%.

  • (A) ✗ 132 ÷ 210 = 62.9%: uses employment rather than the labor force as the numerator, dropping the unemployed.
  • (B) ✓ Correct. Labor force ÷ adult population.
  • (C) ✗ 5.7% is the unemployment rate from Question 8 — the same inputs with the other denominator.
  • (D) ✗ 3.8% is the population-denominator error from Question 8, offered again for a student who has not noticed which rate is being asked for.
  • (E) ✗ 33.3% is the share outside the labor force (70 ÷ 210), the complement of the right answer.

Question 12 — Labor force participation rate · Synthesis

Answer: (D)

The two rates answer different questions and only together reveal discouraged workers. If participation is steady or rising while unemployment falls, people are genuinely finding work; if both fall, people are leaving the labour market and the improvement is hollow.

  • (A) ✗ Output is a reasonable thing to want, but it does not distinguish a real improvement from a statistical one — the discouraged-worker case can occur with output flat.
  • (B) ✗ The count of unemployed falls in both the genuine and the hollow case, so it cannot separate them.
  • (C) ✓ Names a genuinely relevant statistic — the adult population is the participation rate's denominator — but gives the wrong reason: the unemployment rate is a share of the labor force, not of the adult population. A student picking C has confused the two denominators.
  • (D) ✓ Correct. Read the two rates together; both falling is the signal.
  • (E) ✗ Historical context, not a test of whether this month's fall is genuine.

Question 13 — Aggregate output · Analysis

Answer: (C)

Aggregate output is the economy's total production of goods and services — a quantity, not a sum of money. The same cars, steel and haircuts were produced, so aggregate output is unchanged. What rose is the price level, which is the distinction between nominal and real measures.

  • (A) ✗ Measures output in current prices, which is nominal GDP. Nominal GDP rose 6%; production did not.
  • (B) ✗ Relative price movements are irrelevant here — the stem says every price rose 6% — and the quantity produced is unchanged either way.
  • (C) ✓ Correct. Same goods and services, so same aggregate output.
  • (D) ✗ Confuses output with purchasing power. What consumers can buy with a fixed income has fallen; what the economy produced has not.
  • (E) ✗ Population matters for output per capita, not for aggregate output.

Question 14 — Potential output and the output gap · Application (numeric)

Answer: (A)

Output gap = actual − potential = 18.72 − 19.50 = −$0.78 trillion. As a percentage of potential: 0.78 ÷ 19.50 = 0.04 = −4.0%. Negative, so a recessionary gap.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ −0.78 is the gap in trillions of dollars, reported as though it were the percentage.
  • (C) ✗ Right magnitude, wrong sign. Actual output is below potential, so the gap is negative.
  • (D) ✗ 0.78 ÷ 18.72 = 4.2%: divides by actual output rather than potential. The framework defines the gap against potential.
  • (E) ✗ 7.8% misplaces the decimal point of the dollar figure.

Question 15 — Potential output and the output gap · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

Potential output is the level the economy can sustain with unemployment at its natural rate, not a physical ceiling. Actual output can exceed it for a period — overtime, deferred maintenance, an overheating labour market — and that state is characteristic of the approach to a peak.

  • (A) ✓ Correct. Unsustainable production, typical near a peak, followed by a downturn.
  • (B) ✗ States the misconception the question is built on. Potential means sustainable, not maximal.
  • (C) ✗ A recession is falling output. Output above potential is the opposite situation.
  • (D) ✗ Even at full employment there is frictional and structural unemployment — that is the natural rate, and it is why potential output is defined through it rather than through zero unemployment.
  • (E) ✗ Confuses a temporary positive output gap with economic growth. Growth is a rise in potential output itself, not actual output running above it.

Question 16 — Inflation and deflation · Analysis

Answer: (B)

Inflation is a rise in the overall price level. One price tripling while others hold steady is a change in relative prices: coffee now costs more in terms of everything else, which is a change in its opportunity cost, not a general rise in prices.

  • (A) ✗ "A price rose, therefore inflation." The error the module's second free-response question is built on.
  • (B) ✓ Correct. Relative price change, not a change in the price level.
  • (C) ✗ Deflation is a fall in the overall price level. Affording less coffee is not deflation; if anything the price level edged up.
  • (D) ✓ Contains a true fact — the CPI's basket does determine what the measured rate captures — but reaches the wrong conclusion. Whether coffee is in the index affects the measurement, not whether one price rising constitutes inflation. It does not.
  • (E) ✗ Wages bear on real income, not on whether inflation occurred. The price level is the whole test.

Question 17 — Inflation and deflation · Synthesis

Answer: (E)

The student's statement is sound economics and is the cost Krugman gives on p. 12. It is not the cost the framework states. MEA-1.H.1 is specific: "Unexpected inflation arbitrarily redistributes wealth from one group of individuals to another group, such as lenders to borrowers" (CED p. 51). A student who has read only Module 2 will not have met it — the phrase unexpected inflation appears three times in the whole textbook.

  • (A) ✗ Textbook agreement is not the standard being applied. The examiner marks against the framework.
  • (B) ✗ Inflation does erode the value of cash; the statement is economically correct.
  • (C) ✗ Deflation makes holding cash more attractive, not less. This reverses both costs.
  • (D) ✗ Mark schemes award points for specified content, not for any true statement on the topic.
  • (E) ✓ Correct. Right economics, wrong requirement.

Question 18 — Price stability · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Price stability means the aggregate price level changing only slowly, not not at all. Targeting zero risks overshooting into deflation, which makes holding cash more attractive than investing and can deepen a downturn. A small positive target keeps a margin.

  • (A) ✓ Correct. Slow change is the definition; the margin against deflation is the reason.
  • (B) ✗ Economic growth is a rise in productive capacity and does not require any particular inflation rate. The two are separate.
  • (C) ✗ Ease of hitting a target is not a reason to prefer one number over another, and it says nothing about why 2% rather than 4% or −1%.
  • (D) ✗ Inflation erodes real wages when nominal wages lag; it does not raise them. And no central bank targets real wages.
  • (E) ✗ A 0% target would still be pursued with the same tools. The objection is about the risk of deflation, not about abandoning policy.

Question 19 — Economic growth · Application (numeric)

Answer: (E)

Growth rate = (49,200 − 48,000) ÷ 48,000 × 100 = 1,200 ÷ 48,000 = 0.025 = 2.5%. Economic growth is measured as the growth rate in real GDP per capita over time (MEA-2.B.1).

  • (A) ✗ 0.25% is the right calculation with the decimal point moved one place.
  • (B) ✗ 1,200 read as a percentage after dividing by 1,000 — a decimal slip, not an economic error.
  • (C) ✗ 1,200 ÷ 49,200 = 2.4%: divides by the end value rather than the start. Growth rates are computed on the initial value.
  • (D) ✗ 1,200 ÷ 10,000: a misplaced decimal point.
  • (E) ✓ Correct. 1,200 ÷ 48,000 = 0.025.

Question 20 — Economic growth and real GDP per capita integrated · Synthesis

Answer: (E)

Year 1 is a recession: output falling. Years 2 to 4 are an expansion: output rising. Compounding 0.95 × 1.02 × 1.02 × 1.01 = 0.998 — the economy finishes fractionally below where it started. With population and prices unchanged, real GDP per capita has not risen, so no growth has occurred. The economy has recovered lost ground, which is what an expansion does.

  • (A) ✗ Counts rising years as growth. Three years of recovery after a fall is an expansion, and output did not even regain its starting level.
  • (B) ✗ Gets the recession right and the ending wrong: output finished at 99.8% of its year-1 starting level, not above it. Worth checking the arithmetic rather than assuming three rises beat one fall.
  • (C) ✗ Notes correctly that with constant prices and population real GDP per capita tracks output, then draws the wrong conclusion — output did not rise over the four years.
  • (D) ✗ A slow recovery is not a depression. A depression is a very deep and prolonged downturn, and years 2 to 4 were upturns.
  • (E) ✓ Correct. Recession, then expansion, and recovering lost ground is not an increase in capacity.

Question 21 — Depression · Analysis (background)

Answer: (B)

A depression is a very deep and prolonged downturn. There is no agreed numerical threshold, which is why the judgement is qualitative — but a 3% fall over nine months with recovery inside a year is an ordinary recession by any reading. The United States has not had a depression since the 1930s.

  • (A) ✗ Would make every recession a depression and leave the word doing no work.
  • (B) ✓ Correct. Depth and duration, judged rather than measured, and this episode has neither.
  • (C) ✗ Invents a threshold. No such formal definition exists, and that is precisely why the word is used sparingly.
  • (D) ✗ The contraction phase is a recession. Depression is not a phase of the cycle; the framework names only two.
  • (E) ✗ The word is still used — the Great Depression is discussed constantly. What is rare is the event.

Question 22 — Model · Analysis (background)

Answer: (D)

A model is a deliberately simplified representation. The two-good assumption is what makes a trade-off visible as a curve that can be measured; a model with every real good in it would be the economy itself and no more useful. The test of a simplification is whether it changes the answer to the question being asked, not whether it is realistic.

  • (A) ✗ Every model has unrealistic assumptions. This standard would rule out all of them, including every diagram in Units 3 to 6.
  • (B) ✗ Assumptions are exactly what should be examined. The objection is worth taking seriously; it is just not decisive.
  • (C) ✓ Arrives near the right conclusion — keep the model — through a confused argument. A model is not "wrong"; it is incomplete on purpose, and the distinction is the whole point of the entry.
  • (D) ✓ Correct. The simplification is the method, and the test is whether it alters the answer.
  • (E) ✗ Defends the model by claiming its assumption is realistic somewhere, which concedes the wrong standard.

Question 23 — Other things equal · Synthesis (background)

Answer: (C)

"Other things equal" is an instruction, not a description. It tells the student to hold every other relevant factor — interest rates included — constant, so that any change in spending can be attributed to the change in income alone. On the exam this is what stops a student shifting a second curve.

  • (A) ✗ True about real economies and irrelevant to the question asked. The phrase removes exactly that complication by instruction.
  • (B) ✗ Specifying the interest rate does not rescue an answer that has ignored the assumption.
  • (C) ✓ Correct. One change, everything else held still.
  • (D) ✗ Interest rates do affect consumer spending. The answer fails because of the assumption, not because the channel is imaginary.
  • (E) ✗ There is nothing partly correct here. The student has answered a different question from the one set.

Notes for the teacher

Content validity. All 16 concept entries appear. Weighting follows Unit 2: the unemployment rate gets three items, the participation rate and the output gap two each, and the three context terms one each in a separately scored section. Section A's 20 items are exam-representative; Section B's 3 are not, and mixing the scores would flatter or punish a student for material the exam does not test.

Bloom levels, as operationalised here. Application means executing a defined procedure on given numbers — the four numeric items (8, 11, 14, 19). Analysis means separating a case into its parts and deciding which part governs — direction against level, one price against the price level. Synthesis means holding two ideas together to reach a conclusion neither gives alone — the discouraged-worker items (10, 12), the potential-output items (5, 15), the framework-against-textbook item (17), and the integrated item (20). Counts: 4 · 12 · 7, a ratio of 1 : 3 : 1.75.

Distractor design. Every wrong option encodes a documented error rather than filler. The recurring ones: dividing by the population instead of the labor force (8A, 8E, 11D); treating a rate's numerator as its whole story (9B); testing the level of unemployment instead of its direction (3A); reading "one price rose" as inflation (16A); confusing a positive output gap with growth (15A); and reversing the two costs of inflation (17C). Questions 6 and 11 are built so that the wrong answers are each produced by one identifiable slip, which makes the paper diagnostic rather than merely scored.

Four distractors are correct-conclusion-by-wrong-reasoning, and are flagged as such in the solutions: 5D, 12C, 16D and 22C. Students who choose them hold a true fact and a broken argument, and they need different feedback from students who simply did not know. Marking these wrong without explanation produces the complaint "but that's true" — the solutions answer it in advance.

Answer distribution. A 5 · B 5 · C 4 · D 4 · E 5. No key repeats more than twice in a row, and the keys were rearranged after drafting to break an arithmetic pattern — an early version had A on every fifth item and E on every fifth item after that, which a student could have exploited without reading the stems.

Six items test material the textbook does not contain — 4, 5, 11, 12, 14 and 15, covering peak and trough, the participation rate, and potential output and the output gap. All six are Topic 2.7 or Topic 2.3 content and all are examinable in Unit 2. If a class scores poorly on precisely these and well on the rest, the test has done its job: the gap is in the textbook, not in the students.
