Section A

1. A factory worker takes two weeks of unpaid leave to care for a sick relative. Her employer is holding her job for her, and she will return to it at the end of the leave.

How is she classified during the two weeks?

  • (A) Unemployed, because she is not working.
  • (B) Not in the labor force, because she is caring for a relative.
  • (C) Discouraged, because she is not looking for work.
  • (D) Employed, because she still holds a job.
  • (E) Unemployed, because she is not being paid.

2. Which of the following people is counted as unemployed?

  • (A) A 70-year-old who retired last year and has no plans to work again.
  • (B) A full-time university student who is not looking for a job.
  • (C) A welder laid off last month who has sent applications to four employers this week.
  • (D) A welder laid off a year ago who stopped applying six months ago because no one is hiring.
  • (E) A writer who works ten hours a week and would like a full-time job.

3. A household has five adults. The mother works full time. The father was laid off and has been applying for jobs. The grandmother is retired. The son, 19, is a full-time student and is not looking for work. The daughter, 17, works ten hours a week at a café.

How many of them are in the labor force, and how many are unemployed?

  • (A) Three in the labor force; one unemployed.
  • (B) Four in the labor force; one unemployed.
  • (C) Five in the labor force; three unemployed.
  • (D) Two in the labor force; one unemployed.
  • (E) Four in the labor force; two unemployed.

4. A father left his job a year ago to stay at home with his young children. He is not looking for work and does not plan to for several years.

How is he classified?

  • (A) Unemployed, because he has no job.
  • (B) Discouraged, because he has stopped looking.
  • (C) Employed, because caring for children is work.
  • (D) Underemployed, because he could be working full time.
  • (E) Not in the labor force, because he is neither working nor looking.

5. An economy has an adult population of 80,000. Of them, 46,000 are employed and 4,000 are unemployed.

What is the labor force participation rate?

  • (A) 57.5%
  • (B) 62.5%
  • (C) 8%
  • (D) 92%
  • (E) 5%

6. In a country, 9.2 million people are employed and 0.8 million are unemployed.

What is the unemployment rate?

  • (A) 8.7%
  • (B) 92%
  • (C) It cannot be calculated without the adult population.
  • (D) 8%
  • (E) 0.08%

How many people are unemployed?

  • (A) 2.5 million
  • (B) 30.4 million
  • (C) 18 million
  • (D) 1.52 million
  • (E) 1.6 million

9. An economy has an adult population of 250 million, a labor force of 160 million and 8 million unemployed. Then 2 million of the unemployed stop looking for work because they believe no jobs are available. Nothing else changes.

What happens to the two rates?

  • (A) The unemployment rate falls to about 3.8% and the participation rate falls from 64% to 63.2%.
  • (B) The unemployment rate is unchanged and the participation rate falls.
  • (C) The unemployment rate rises, because more people are without work, and the participation rate is unchanged.
  • (D) Neither changes, because nobody found a job.
  • (E) The unemployment rate falls to 3.75% and the participation rate is unchanged.

10. An unemployed accountant accepts a job offer and starts work. Nothing else changes.

What happens to the unemployment rate and the labor force participation rate?

  • (A) Both fall.
  • (B) Both rise.
  • (C) The unemployment rate falls; the participation rate is unchanged.
  • (D) The unemployment rate is unchanged; the participation rate rises.
  • (E) The unemployment rate falls; the participation rate rises.

11. Over a year, a country's unemployment rate falls from 8% to about 4.2%. The number of people employed does not change at all, and neither does the adult population.

What is the best explanation?

  • (A) Employers hired about half of the unemployed.
  • (B) The unemployment rate must have been miscalculated, since employment did not rise.
  • (C) More people entered the labor force and found jobs.
  • (D) About half of the unemployed stopped looking for work and left the labor force; the participation rate fell.
  • (E) Some employed people moved to part-time work, which lowers the unemployment rate.

12. An economy has an adult population of 160, with 95 employed and 5 unemployed. Five of the employed workers then retire. Nothing else changes.

What happens?

  • (A) The participation rate falls from 62.5% to about 59.4%, and the unemployment rate rises from 5% to about 5.3% — although nobody lost a job.
  • (B) Both rates fall, because fewer people are working.
  • (C) The unemployment rate rises, because five people lost their jobs.
  • (D) The participation rate falls and the unemployment rate is unchanged, because retirees are not unemployed.
  • (E) Nothing changes, because retirees were never in the labor force.

13. Which of the following people is a discouraged worker?

  • (A) A teacher who is not working during the summer holiday.
  • (B) A laid-off engineer who is applying for jobs every week.
  • (C) A student who is not interested in working until she graduates.
  • (D) A chef who works 20 hours a week and would prefer 40.
  • (E) A miner who lost his job two years ago, would take any job, and stopped searching last year because no mine is hiring.

14. A musician works 15 hours a week playing at parties. He would like a full-time job and has been unable to find one.

How is he treated in the official statistics?

  • (A) As unemployed, because he wants a job he does not have.
  • (B) As employed — so his lack of hours is invisible in the unemployment rate.
  • (C) As not in the labor force, because he is not working full time.
  • (D) As half employed and half unemployed.
  • (E) As discouraged, because he cannot find the job he wants.

15. In a recession, firms cut the hours of one million full-time workers to part time. All of them keep their jobs, and all would prefer to work full time. Nothing else changes.

What happens to the measured unemployment rate, and what does that show?

  • (A) It rises, because a million people are now partly unemployed.
  • (B) It falls, because the same work is spread across more jobs.
  • (C) It does not change — which is one way the rate understates joblessness.
  • (D) It does not change, because the recession has not yet reached the labor market.
  • (E) It rises, because part-time workers are left out of the labor force.

16. A country's labor force is 100 million and 5 million are unemployed. There are also 2 million discouraged workers.

If discouraged workers were counted as unemployed and in the labor force, what would the rate be?

  • (B) 5%
  • (C) 2%
  • (D) 3%
  • (E) About 6.9%

17. Countries X and Y both report an unemployment rate of 6%. In Country X, a large number of people have given up looking for work after years of high unemployment; in Country Y, very few have.

Which statement is correct?

  • (A) Joblessness is understated by more in Country X, and a measure that counted discouraged workers would be higher there.
  • (B) The two countries have the same level of joblessness, because their rates are equal.
  • (C) Country X has less joblessness, because its discouraged workers have left the labor force.
  • (D) Country Y's rate is overstated, because it includes discouraged workers.
  • (E) No comparison is possible without the participation rates.

18. In a given year a country's real GDP falls by 2%.

Based on the pattern in the U.S. data, what is most likely to have happened to the unemployment rate?

  • (A) It fell, because firms need fewer hours from each worker.
  • (B) It did not change, because unemployment responds only to prices.
  • (C) It fell, because discouraged workers returned to the labor force.
  • (D) It rose.
  • (E) It is impossible to say; the two are unrelated.

19. In one year a country's real GDP grows by 1%, well below its long-run average of about 3.3%. The unemployment rate rises.

Is that consistent with the relationship shown in the textbook?

  • (A) No — positive growth always lowers unemployment.
  • (B) Yes — growth below the average rate is usually accompanied by rising unemployment, even without a recession.
  • (C) No — unemployment rises only when real GDP falls.
  • (D) Yes — because unemployment always rises when real GDP rises.
  • (E) No — the unemployment rate and real GDP growth are not related.

20. A recession officially ends. For the next year, real GDP grows, yet the unemployment rate keeps rising.

What is the best explanation?

  • (A) The recession has not really ended.

  • (D) Workers are becoming discouraged, which raises the unemployment rate.
  • (E) The economy is growing, but not fast enough to reduce unemployment — as after the 1990–91 and 2001 recessions.

Solutions

Question 1 — Employed · Application

Answer: (D)

Employed means currently holding a job, full time or part time. She has a job to return to; being absent from it for a fortnight does not change that.

  • (A) ✗ "Not working" is not the test for unemployment. The unemployed have no job at all.
  • (B) ✗ Caring for a relative would put someone outside the labor force only if they had no job

and were not looking. She has a job.

  • (C) ✗ A discouraged worker has no job and has given up looking. She has not needed to look.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Pay is not part of the classification. What matters is whether she holds a job.

Question 2 — Unemployed · Analysis

Answer: (C)

The unemployed are "jobless, looking for jobs, and available for work", having searched in the past four weeks. Only the recently laid-off welder meets all three conditions.

  • (A) ✗ Retired and not looking: not in the labor force.
  • (B) ✗ Not looking: not in the labor force.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Jobless and willing, but has not searched in the past four weeks — a discouraged worker,

and not counted.

  • (E) ✗ Has a job, so is employed. Wanting more hours makes the writer underemployed, not

unemployed.

Question 3 — Labor force · Analysis

Answer: (A)

The labor force is the employed plus the unemployed. Mother: employed. Father: unemployed. Daughter: employed — ten hours is part time, and she is over 16. Grandmother and son: neither working nor looking, so not in the labor force. Three in the labor force, one unemployed.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Counts the student, who is not looking for work.
  • (C) ✗ Treats every adult as in the labor force and everyone not working full time as

unemployed.

  • (D) ✗ Leaves out the daughter. Part-time workers are employed.
  • (E) ✗ Counts the student as unemployed. Not working is not enough; he would have to be

looking.

Question 4 — Not in the labor force · Application

Answer: (E)

He has no job and is not looking for one, so he is in neither part of the labor force. He is an adult not in the labor force — the category your textbook describes and never names.

  • (A) ✗ Having no job is only one of three conditions. He is not looking.
  • (B) ✗ A discouraged worker wants a job and has stopped looking because of the job market. He

stopped by choice.

  • (C) ✗ Unpaid work at home is a nonmarket activity. It does not make anyone employed.
  • (D) ✗ The underemployed have jobs — part time, wanting full time. He has none.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 5 — Labor force participation rate · Application

Answer: (B)

Labor force = 46,000 + 4,000 = 50,000. Participation rate = 50,000 ÷ 80,000 × 100 = 62.5%.

  • (A) ✗ 46,000 ÷ 80,000: the employed only. The unemployed are part of the labor force too.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ 4,000 ÷ 50,000: the unemployment rate.
  • (D) ✗ 46,000 ÷ 50,000: the employed share of the labor force.
  • (E) ✗ 4,000 ÷ 80,000: the unemployed as a share of the adult population, which is no standard

measure.

Question 6 — Unemployment rate · Application

Answer: (D)

Labor force = 9.2 + 0.8 = 10 million. Unemployment rate = 0.8 ÷ 10 × 100 = 8%.

  • (A) ✗ 0.8 ÷ 9.2: divides by the employed instead of the labor force.
  • (B) ✗ 9.2 ÷ 10: the employed share of the labor force.
  • (C) ✗ The adult population is the denominator of the participation rate. The unemployment

rate needs only the labor force.

  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ 0.08 is the fraction; the percentage is 8%.

Question 7 — Calculating the two rates · Analysis · the College Board's own sample question

Answer: (B)

Unemployed = labor force − employed = 200,000 − 175,000 = 25,000. Unemployment rate = 25,000 ÷ 200,000 × 100 = 12.5%.

The published answer key (CED p. 140) gives B, tagged Learning Objective MEA-1.C, Unit 2, Skill 1.C. Every distractor is a wrong denominator or a wrong numerator.

  • (A) ✗ 25,000 ÷ 250,000: divides by the population instead of the labor force.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ 50,000 ÷ 250,000: the share of the population not in the labor force.
  • (D) ✗ 200,000 ÷ 250,000: the labor force participation rate.
  • (E) ✗ 175,000 ÷ 200,000: the employed share of the labor force.

Question 8 — Calculating the two rates · Synthesis

Answer: (E)

Work back through both formulas. Labor force = 64% × 50 million = 32 million. Unemployed = 5% × 32 million = 1.6 million.

  • (A) ✗ 5% of the adult population. The unemployment rate is a share of the labor force.
  • (B) ✗ 32 − 1.6: the number employed.
  • (C) ✗ 50 − 32: the number not in the labor force.
  • (D) ✗ 5% of the employed. The rate is a share of the whole labor force, employed and

unemployed.

  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 9 — How changes move the two rates · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Before: 8 ÷ 160 = 5%; 160 ÷ 250 = 64%. The 2 million leave the unemployed and the labor force. After: 6 ÷ 158 = 3.8%; 158 ÷ 250 = 63.2%. Both rates fall, and nobody found a job.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ The unemployment rate does change: its numerator falls by more, proportionally, than its

denominator.

  • (C) ✗ They are still without work, but they are no longer counted as unemployed. That is the

understatement.

  • (D) ✗ Leaving the labor force changes both rates, with or without new jobs.
  • (E) ✗ 6 ÷ 160: removes them from the unemployed but forgets that they also leave the labor

force. Wrong on both counts.

Question 10 — How changes move the two rates · Analysis

Answer: (C)

The accountant moves from unemployed to employed. The labor force is the same size — the accountant was in it before and is in it now — so the participation rate does not move. The unemployment rate falls.

  • (A) ✗ The participation rate falls only if people leave the labor force.
  • (B) ✗ Getting a job lowers unemployment.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ The accountant was already in the labor force, as an unemployed job-seeker.
  • (E) ✗ The first half is right. The second treats finding a job as joining the labor force.

Question 11 — How changes move the two rates · Synthesis

Answer: (D)

Employment is fixed, so nobody found a job. The rate can fall only if unemployed people leave the labor force. Take a labor force of 100 with 8 unemployed: if 4 give up, 4 ÷ 96 = 4.2%. Half the unemployed stopped looking, and the participation rate fell with them.

  • (A) ✗ Hiring would raise employment, which the stem rules out.
  • (B) ✗ A falling rate with constant employment is entirely possible. It is the discouraged-worker

effect.

  • (C) ✗ New entrants who found jobs would raise employment.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Part-timers are still employed. A cut in hours changes neither rate.

Question 12 — How changes move the two rates · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Before: labor force 100; participation 100 ÷ 160 = 62.5%; unemployment 5 ÷ 100 = 5%. The five retirees leave the employed and the labor force. After: labor force 95; participation 95 ÷ 160 = 59.4%; unemployment 5 ÷ 95 = 5.3%. The same five unemployed are now a share of a smaller labor force.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ The unemployment rate rises: its numerator is unchanged and its denominator shrinks.
  • (C) ✗ Right direction, wrong reason. Nobody lost a job; the retirees left the labor force.
  • (D) ✗ Retirees are not unemployed, but their leaving changes the denominator.
  • (E) ✗ They were in the labor force until they retired — they were employed.

Question 13 — Discouraged workers · Application

Answer: (E)

A discouraged worker is capable of working but has given up looking for a job due to the state of the job market. The miner wants work, is capable of it, and stopped searching because of the market.

  • (A) ✗ Employed — the teacher holds a job.
  • (B) ✗ Unemployed — still searching.
  • (C) ✗ Not in the labor force by choice, not because of the job market.
  • (D) ✗ Underemployed — employed part time, wanting full time.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 14 — Part-time workers and the underemployed · Analysis

Answer: (B)

He holds a job, so he is employed — and counted exactly like a full-time worker. That he wants more hours is invisible in the unemployment rate. This is what the framework means when it says the rate excludes part-time workers.

  • (A) ✗ He has a job. Wanting a better one does not make him unemployed.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Part-time workers are in the labor force.
  • (D) ✗ The statistics classify people, not hours. There is no half category.
  • (E) ✗ Discouraged workers have no job and have stopped looking.

Question 15 — Part-time workers and the underemployed · Synthesis

Answer: (C)

Everyone keeps a job, so employment, unemployment and the labor force are all unchanged, and so is the rate. But a million people now have less work than they want. The rate cannot see it — one of the two reasons the framework gives for its understating joblessness.

  • (A) ✗ There is no "partly unemployed" category. They are employed.
  • (B) ✗ Nobody new was hired; the same workers work fewer hours.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Right that the rate does not change, wrong reason. The recession has reached the labor

market — as lost hours rather than lost jobs.

  • (E) ✗ Part-time workers are in the labor force, counted as employed.

Question 16 — Why the rate understates joblessness · Analysis

Answer: (E)

Add them to the unemployed and to the labor force: (5 + 2) ÷ (100 + 2) × 100 = 7 ÷ 102 ≈ 6.9%, against the official 5%.

  • (A) ✗ 7 ÷ 100: adds them to the unemployed but not to the labor force.
  • (B) ✗ The official rate — the one that leaves them out.
  • (C) ✗ 2 ÷ 100: the discouraged workers alone, not a rate of anything.
  • (D) ✗ Seems to take 5 − 2. Counting more jobless people cannot lower the rate.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 17 — Why the rate understates joblessness · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

Equal official rates can hide unequal joblessness. Country X's discouraged workers want work and are left out of its rate, so its rate understates joblessness by more. A measure that counted them — like the broader measures in Figure 12.2 — would be higher in X.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ The official rates are equal; joblessness is not.
  • (C) ✗ Leaving the labor force hides joblessness; it does not reduce it.
  • (D) ✗ The official rate never includes discouraged workers.
  • (E) ✗ The participation rates would help quantify the difference, but the direction is

already clear from the stem.

Question 18 — Unemployment over the business cycle · Analysis

Answer: (D)

Figure 12.5: "Unemployment always rose when real GDP fell." Less output needs fewer workers, and every recession since 1948 has raised the rate.

  • (A) ✗ Fewer hours and fewer workers both come with falling output; unemployment does not fall.
  • (B) ✗ Unemployment responds to output, which is what the figure plots.
  • (C) ✗ Discouraged workers return in good times, and returning raises the rate anyway.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ The scatter shows a strong negative relationship.

Question 19 — Unemployment over the business cycle · Synthesis

Answer: (B)

In Figure 12.5, years to the left of the 3.3% line — below-average growth — are usually years of rising unemployment, whether or not growth was negative. Positive but slow growth can raise the rate with no recession at all.

  • (A) ✗ Only above-average growth usually lowers unemployment.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Falling GDP always raises unemployment, but so does slow growth, usually.
  • (D) ✗ Right that it is consistent, wrong reason. The relationship is negative.
  • (E) ✗ The two are strongly related.

Question 20 — Unemployment over the business cycle · Analysis

Answer: (E)

Figure 12.4: after both the 1990–91 and 2001 recessions, "the unemployment rate continued to rise for more than a year after the recession was officially over … the economy was growing, [but] it was not growing fast enough to reduce the unemployment rate."

  • (A) ✗ A recession ends when output stops falling. That happened.
  • (B) ✗ Rising real GDP usually lowers unemployment; here it was too slow to.
  • (C) ✗ The measurement is the same throughout.
  • (D) ✗ Discouraged workers leaving the labor force lower the unemployment rate.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 21 — The whole calculation · Synthesis

Answer: (C)

Labor force = 160 + 8 = 168 million. Participation = 168 ÷ 260 × 100 = 64.6%. Unemployment = 8 ÷ 168 × 100 = 4.8%. Each rate has its own denominator.

  • (A) ✗ Both denominators wrong: 160 ÷ 260 and 8 ÷ 160 leave the unemployed out of the labor

force.

  • (B) ✗ Participation right; unemployment divided by the employed, 8 ÷ 160.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Unemployment right; participation leaves the unemployed out, 160 ÷ 260.
  • (E) ✗ Participation right; unemployment divided by the adult population, 8 ÷ 260.

Section B — solutions

Question 22 — Marginally attached workers and U6 · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Figure 12.2: the broader measures are "substantially higher" than the official rate and "move very much in parallel" with it. A recession raises all of them.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ The broader measure counts discouraged workers, so more of them raise it.
  • (C) ✗ It rises with the official rate.
  • (D) ✗ It counts everyone the official rate counts, and more. It cannot be lower.
  • (E) ✗ The measures move together; they do not converge.

Question 23 — Why the rate can overstate · Analysis

Answer: (D)

She has no job and is actively looking, so she is unemployed — although she is confident of work within days. Krugman: "the unemployment rate never falls to zero, even in boom times." Module 13 names this frictional unemployment.

  • (A) ✗ She is searching, so she is in the labor force.
  • (B) ✗ An offer is not a job. And her case inflates the rate rather than understating it.
  • (C) ✗ Discouraged workers have stopped looking. She is interviewing.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ The underemployed have part-time jobs. She has none.

Question 24 — Differences across groups and places · Analysis

Answer: (C)

Figure 12.3: an overall rate of 4.7%, low by historical standards, alongside 31.2% for African-American teenagers. "Even at a time when the overall unemployment rate was relatively low, jobs were hard to find for some groups."

  • (A) ✗ Rates differ widely across groups; nothing is miscalculated.
  • (B) ✗ A high rate in a small group does not make that group most of the unemployed.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ A teenager is unemployed only if looking for work.
  • (E) ✗ The national rate is a weighted average, and small groups with high rates barely move it.

Content validity. All 15 concept entries appear across the 24 items. The three background entries — marginally attached workers and U6, overstatement, and differences across groups — are tested once each in Section B. Questions 9 to 12 test material the textbook does not contain: the module defines both rates and never moves anyone between categories, although learning objective MEA-1.C asks students to "explain how changes in employment and the labor market affect" them. Four of the twenty-one Section A items are on it, because it is where the counter-intuitive exam answers are — a discouraged worker lowers the rate, a retirement raises it.
