Section A — solutions

Question 1 — Disposable income · Analysis

Answer: (B)

Disposable income is income after taxes are paid and government transfers are received (p. 161). Unemployment benefits are a transfer, so they raise disposable income at once, with no new production.

  • (A) ✗ A tax rise lowers disposable income.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Road building raises incomes only as output is produced and workers are paid — through output, which the stem rules out.
  • (D) ✗ Spending savings changes spending, not income.
  • (E) ✗ A lower price level raises what income buys, not disposable income in dollars.

Question 2 — Marginal propensity to consume · Application

Answer: (D)

MPC = change in consumer spending ÷ change in disposable income = 75 ÷ 100 = 0.75 (equation 16-1).

  • (A) ✗ 680 ÷ 800: total spending over total income in the first year.
  • (B) ✗ 755 ÷ 900: the same error in the second year.
  • (C) ✗ That is the MPS, 1 − 0.75.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ 100 ÷ 75: the ratio upside down.

Question 3 — Marginal propensity to consume · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Spending rose by 45,000 − 38,000 = 7,000 when income rose by 10,000: 7,000 ÷ 10,000 = 0.7. The student's 0.95 is 38,000 ÷ 40,000, a ratio of totals.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ 45,000 ÷ 50,000 is the second year's ratio of totals — the same error, and not what the student did.
  • (C) ✗ The MPC is a ratio of changes.
  • (D) ✗ 0.3 is the MPS, 3,000 ÷ 10,000.
  • (E) ✗ 10,000 ÷ 7,000: the change in income over the change in spending, upside down.

Question 4 — Marginal propensity to save · Analysis

Answer: (E)

MPS = 100 ÷ 500 = 0.2; MPC = 1 − 0.2 = 0.8; of the extra $500 billion, 500 − 100 = $400 billion is spent. All three statements hold.

  • (A) ✗ True, but II and III are true too.
  • (B) ✗ True, but I and III are true too.
  • (C) ✗ Statement III is true as well: whatever is not saved is spent.
  • (D) ✗ Statement II is true as well: MPC + MPS = 1.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 5 — Autonomous change in spending · Analysis

Answer: (C)

The autonomous change is "the cause, not the result" of the chain (p. 160): the firms' $50 billion. The households' $30 billion and $18 billion are induced by the rise in income — later rounds, at an MPC of 0.6.

  • (A) ✗ 50 + 30 + 18: adds the induced rounds to the cause.
  • (B) ✗ 30 + 18: only the induced rounds.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ 50 + 30: counts the second round as autonomous.
  • (E) ✗ The second round, induced by the first.

Question 6 — The multiplier process · Application

Answer: (B)

Round 1 is $40 billion; round 2 is 0.75 × 40 = $30 billion; round 3 is 0.75 × 30 = $22.5 billion.

  • (A) ✗ The second round.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ 0.25 × 40: the amount saved in round 2.
  • (D) ✗ 4 × 40: the total of all rounds.
  • (E) ✗ 0.75 × 22.5: the fourth round.

Question 7 — The multiplier process · Analysis

Answer: (E)

The multiplier runs downward too (CYU 1, p. 170): one group's lost spending is another group's lost income, which then cuts its own spending.

  • (A) ✗ Nothing in the stem moves interest rates; the link is through income.
  • (B) ✗ The MPC is a property of households' behaviour and does not change here.
  • (C) ✗ No such rule; consumer spending is far larger than investment.
  • (D) ✗ Falling sales do not raise prices, and the stem gives no price change.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 8 — Expenditure multiplier · Application

Answer: (A)

Expenditure multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − 0.9) = 10; 10 × 15 = $150 billion, the maximum.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ 15 ÷ 0.9: divides by the MPC instead of multiplying by 1 ÷ (1 − MPC).
  • (C) ✗ 9 × 15: the tax multiplier applied to spending.
  • (D) ✗ 0.9 × 15: only the second round.
  • (E) ✗ 0.1 × 15: the MPS times the change.

Question 9 — MPS and the multiplier · Synthesis

Answer: (D)

Country A: multiplier = 1 ÷ MPS = 1 ÷ 0.25 = 4, so 4 × 10 = $40 billion. Country B: 1 ÷ (1 − 0.8) = 5, so 5 × 10 = $50 billion. One country states the MPS and the other the MPC; convert before comparing.

  • (A) ✗ B's figure is 0.8 × 10, the second round.
  • (B) ✗ A's figure is 0.25 × 10: the MPS used as a multiplier.
  • (C) ✗ B's figure is 10 ÷ 0.8: the MPC used as if it were the MPS.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ A's figure is 10 ÷ 0.75: the MPS treated as the MPC.

Question 10 — The multiplier shifts aggregate demand · Analysis

Answer: (C)

The multiplier, 5, gives the shift in aggregate demand: 5 × 20 = $100 billion. With an upward-sloping short-run aggregate supply curve, part of that shift raises the price level, so real GDP rises by less. That is why exam answers say real GDP rises "by a maximum of" the multiplied amount.

  • (A) ✗ Exactly $100 billion holds only if the price level is fixed, as the textbook assumes — the stem says it is not.
  • (B) ✗ The shift is the multiplied amount, and real GDP cannot rise by more than the shift.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ 20 ÷ 0.8 divides by the MPC; the multiplier is 1 ÷ 0.2.
  • (E) ✗ Higher prices absorb part of the shift; they do not add to it.

Question 11 — Tax multiplier · Application

Answer: (E)

Tax multiplier = −0.75 ÷ 0.25 = −3. A tax cut is a change of −40, so −3 × −40 = +$120 billion.

  • (A) ✗ 4 × 40: the expenditure multiplier, as if the whole cut were spent in round 1.
  • (B) ✗ 0.75 × 40: only the first round of spending.
  • (C) ✗ The sign is reversed: a tax cut raises real GDP.
  • (D) ✗ 40 ÷ 0.75: divides by the MPC.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 12 — Tax multiplier · Application

Answer: (B)

Tax multiplier = −0.6 ÷ 0.4 = −1.5; −1.5 × 30 = −$45 billion.

  • (A) ✗ 2.5 × 30: the expenditure multiplier used for a tax.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ A tax increase lowers real GDP.
  • (D) ✗ 0.6 × 30: the first-round fall in spending only.
  • (E) ✗ 30 ÷ 0.6: divides by the MPC.

Question 13 — Transfers and purchases together · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

The transfers work like a tax cut: 9 × 10 = +$90 billion. The cut in purchases uses the expenditure multiplier: 10 × −10 = −$100 billion. Together: 90 − 100 = −$10 billion.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Equal amounts do not cancel; purchases have the bigger multiplier.
  • (C) ✗ 90 + 100: adds the two sizes and ignores that the purchases were cut.
  • (D) ✗ −90 − 100: treats the transfer rise as contractionary too.
  • (E) ✗ Right size, wrong sign: treats the purchase cut as the expansion and the transfer rise as the contraction.

Question 14 — The spending multiplier is bigger than the tax multiplier · Analysis

Answer: (D)

Government purchases are spending in round 1, all of them. A tax cut raises disposable income, and households spend only the MPC share of it — the rest is saved before round 1. So the tax chain starts one round behind (Module 21, Table 21.1).

  • (A) ✗ Taxes do affect real GDP, through disposable income.
  • (B) ✗ The stem fixes the MPC; the gap comes from the first round, not a different MPC.
  • (C) ✗ Not the reason, and not given.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ The negative sign means a tax cut raises real GDP.

Question 15 — Changing spending and taxes together · Synthesis

Answer: (C)

Spending: 1 ÷ (1 − 0.8) = 5, so 5 × 100 = +$500 million. Taxes: −0.8 ÷ 0.2 = −4, so −4 × 100 = −$400 million. Together: 500 − 400 = +$100 million — the amount of the change, whatever the MPC. The options' "$100" means $100 million. "A maximum of" because rising prices may absorb part of it.

  • (A) ✗ The spending effect, with the wrong sign.
  • (B) ✗ The tax effect alone.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ The size of the tax effect, with the wrong sign, and without the spending.
  • (E) ✗ The spending effect alone, ignoring the tax.

Question 16 — Changing spending and taxes together · Synthesis

Answer: (E)

Purchases: 2.5 × −25 = −$62.5 billion. Taxes: the tax multiplier −1.5 times a change of −25 = +$37.5 billion. Together: −62.5 + 37.5 = −$25 billion, the size of the change.

  • (A) ✗ The two parts do not cancel; purchases have the bigger multiplier.
  • (B) ✗ The purchase cut alone, ignoring the tax cut.
  • (C) ✗ Right size, wrong sign: a balanced cut shrinks the economy.
  • (D) ✗ −62.5 − 37.5: treats the tax cut as contractionary too.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 17 — Working backwards · Application

Answer: (A)

Needed change = target ÷ multiplier = 200 ÷ 5 = $40 billion.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ 200 × 5: multiplies instead of dividing.
  • (C) ✗ 200 × 0.8: neither a multiplier nor a division.
  • (D) ✗ 200 ÷ 4: divides by the size of the tax multiplier, but the policy uses purchases.
  • (E) ✗ 200 ÷ 0.8: divides by the MPC.

Question 18 — Working backwards · Synthesis

Answer: (B)

Purchases: 120 ÷ 4 = $30 billion. A tax cut: 120 ÷ 3 = $40 billion. The tax cut must be bigger, because part of it is saved.

  • (A) ✗ Uses the expenditure multiplier for both.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ The two multipliers swapped.
  • (D) ✗ 120 × 4 and 120 × 3: multiplies instead of dividing.
  • (E) ✗ 30 × 0.75: scales the purchase figure down by the MPC; the tax cut must be larger, not smaller.

Question 19 — What shifts consumer spending · Analysis

Answer: (D)

A fall in expected future disposable income shifts the consumption function down (Figure 16.4, panel b): households spend less at every current income. That is an autonomous fall in spending, which the multiplier amplifies.

  • (A) ✗ Expectations of future income also matter (p. 165).
  • (B) ✗ The textbook's worker who fears a layoff cuts spending "to save for a rainy day".
  • (C) ✗ The function shifts down; its slope is not what changes.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ A movement along the function needs a change in current income, which the stem rules out.

Question 20 — What shifts consumer spending · Analysis

Answer: (C)

A rise in wealth (I) and in expected future income (III) raise spending at every price level and shift aggregate demand right. A fall in the price level (II) raises real wealth too, but that is the real wealth effect: a movement along aggregate demand, not a shift (Module 17).

  • (A) ✗ III shifts it as well.
  • (B) ✗ II is a movement along the curve.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ II is a movement along; I is a shift.
  • (E) ✗ II is a movement along the curve.

Question 21 — Planned investment spending · Analysis

Answer: (A)

A higher interest rate means fewer projects beat the cost of funds, and spare capacity means firms do not need new plant (pp. 167–168). Both push planned investment down.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Both changes raise planned investment.
  • (C) ✗ Retained earnings carry the same opportunity cost, the interest forgone; nothing changes.
  • (D) ✗ Expected growth with no spare capacity raises planned investment.
  • (E) ✗ That is unplanned inventory investment, and it raises actual investment.

Question 22 — Investment and the AD shift · Synthesis

Answer: (C)

A $12 billion fall in planned investment is an autonomous fall in spending. The expenditure multiplier is 5, so aggregate demand shifts left by $60 billion.

  • (A) ✗ Right size, wrong direction.
  • (B) ✗ Leaves out the multiplier.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ 4 × 12: the tax multiplier, but investment is spending.
  • (E) ✗ 12 ÷ 0.8: divides by the MPC.

Section B — solutions

Question 23 — The consumption function · Application

Answer: (E)

c = 8,000 + 0.75 × 24,000 = 8,000 + 18,000 = $26,000.

  • (A) ✗ 8,000 + 24,000: as if the MPC were 1.
  • (B) ✗ 0.75 × 24,000: leaves out autonomous consumer spending.
  • (C) ✗ That is the income.
  • (D) ✗ Autonomous consumer spending only.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 24 — Inventories and unplanned investment · Analysis

Answer: (B)

Unsold goods of 500 − 470 = $30 billion go into inventories: unplanned inventory investment of +$30 billion. Actual investment = planned + unplanned = 60 + 30 = $90 billion (equation 16-10).

  • (A) ✗ Ignores the unsold goods.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Sales below forecast make unplanned inventory investment positive, not negative.
  • (D) ✗ Leaves the unplanned part out of actual investment.
  • (E) ✗ Right total, but the sign of the unplanned part is reversed.

Question 25 — Why current income is not the whole story · Analysis

Answer: (D)

The permanent income hypothesis says spending depends mainly on the income people expect over the long term (p. 166). A permanent raise changes that; a one-time bonus barely does.

  • (A) ✗ The hypothesis is precisely that the same $10,000 has different effects depending on whether it lasts.
  • (B) ✗ Timing is not the point; permanence is.
  • (C) ✗ The raise does change Ben's expected long-term income.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ The reverse: a windfall is mostly saved.
