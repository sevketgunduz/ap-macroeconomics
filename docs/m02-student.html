01

Business cycle

★ Exam
Definition

The business cycle is the short-run alternation between economic downturns, known as recessions, and economic upturns, known as expansions.

Textbook page 10.

Two words do the work. Short-run — this is about the next few months and years, not decades. Alternation — it goes back and forth. It is not a cycle in the sense of a clock; the phases are nothing like equal in length, and nobody can tell you when the next turn comes.

Since World War II the United States has had 11 recessions. The average recession lasted 10 months, the average expansion 57 months, and a full cycle averaged 5 years 7 months — but the shortest was 18 months and the longest 10 years 8 months. Learn the shape, not the numbers.

Watch out. "Cycle" does not mean regular. If a question implies you can predict the next recession's date because the last three were four years apart, that is the trap.

Textbook

The 1930s. The Great Depression was the downturn that created macroeconomics. Shantytowns called "Hoovervilles" grew in Central Park, and economists began studying the whole economy because no one understood what had happened.

Numeric

Why averages mislead. 10 + 57 = 67 months, which is 5 years 7 months — the average cycle. But a cycle has actually run as short as 18 months. An average built from wildly different numbers does not predict the next one.

Illustrative

Your local economy. When a big employer in town lays people off, shops, restaurants and builders all slow at once. That "all at once" is what makes it macroeconomic rather than one industry having a bad year.

How it connects. The cycle is made of recessions (entry 2) — a very severe one being a depression (entry 4) — and expansions (entry 3), separated by peaks and troughs (entry 5). It is the short-run story; economic growth (entry 13) is the long-run one, and the two are constantly confused.

Check yourself

1.1 Why do economists talk about a business cycle for the whole economy rather than just the ups and downs of particular industries?

Show answer

Because the ups and downs happen in most industries at the same time. One industry can shrink while others grow — that is just a change in what people buy. A business cycle is when output and employment move together across the economy, which means something is affecting all markets at once.

1.2 A politician says "we are due a recession — we have not had one for seven years." What is wrong with the reasoning?

Show answer

Cycles are not regular. One U.S. expansion ran nearly ten years. Time elapsed is not itself a cause; recessions come from falling output and employment, not from a calendar.

02

Recession

★ Exam
Definition

Recessions are periods of economic downturn when output and employment are falling.

Textbook page 10.

Note what the definition contains: two things, both falling. Not "growing slowly". Not "prices falling". Output down and employment down. That pairing is why a recession hurts — fewer goods produced, fewer people paid to produce them, so incomes and living standards fall together.

Many countries use a rule of thumb: two consecutive quarters (six months) of falling aggregate output. The United States does not. A panel at the National Bureau of Economic Research looks at a range of indicators, mainly employment and production, and makes a judgement call. Your textbook is blunt about it: "there is no exact definition."

Watch out. A recession is not "a time when the economy is doing badly." Output must be falling, not merely low. An economy can have high unemployment and still be expanding, because it is recovering from somewhere worse.

Textbook

December 2007. The most recent downturn in your textbook began then and pushed unemployment into double digits — the deepest since the 1930s.

Numeric

The two-quarter rule fails. Three months of sharply falling output, then three months of slightly positive growth, then three months of rapid decline. No two consecutive quarters of decline — so the rule says no recession, while common sense says nine bad months. This is exactly why the U.S. uses a panel.

Illustrative

The 2001 argument. The NBER dated it March to November 2001. Critics said it started earlier, when industrial production fell; others said it never really ended, because the job market stayed weak for another 18 months. Reasonable people disagree, because the definition is a judgement.

How it connects. A recession is one phase of the business cycle (entry 1) and the opposite of an expansion (entry 3). A very deep, very long one is a depression (entry 4), and every recession runs from a peak to a trough (entry 5). It pushes the unemployment rate (entry 7) up, and employment (entry 6) and aggregate output (entry 9) down.

Check yourself

2.1 Describe who gets hurt in a recession, and how.

Show answer

Workers who lose their jobs lose their income; workers who keep their jobs may get fewer hours or smaller raises. Firms sell less and earn less, so some close. Owners lose profits and asset values. Governments collect less tax while needing to spend more. Everyone consumes less, so living standards fall.

2.2 Output fell for one quarter, rose slightly for one, then fell for two. Is this a U.S. recession?

Show answer

Possibly — and the two-consecutive-quarters rule is not how the U.S. decides. The NBER panel would look at employment and production across the whole period and judge. Three falling quarters out of four would very likely be called a recession.

03

Expansion (recovery)

★ Exam
Definition

Expansions, or recoveries, are periods of economic upturn when output and employment are rising.

Textbook page 10.

Same two variables as a recession, both moving the other way. An expansion runs from a trough to the next peak, which means it covers the whole climb — including the early part when the economy still feels terrible because unemployment is high and only just starting to come down.

This is the single most useful thing to hold on to: an expansion is defined by the direction of output and employment, not their level.

Watch out. Do not equate "expansion" with "good times." Recovery starts at the bottom. In 2009 the recession had technically ended while unemployment was still near its worst.

Textbook

1933. The U.S. economy began a partial recovery in 1933, yet joblessness stayed high for the rest of the 1930s. Rising is not the same as recovered.

Numeric

The asymmetry. The average postwar recession lasted 10 months; the average expansion 57 months — about 5.7 times as long. Economies spend far more time climbing than falling.

Illustrative

The late 1990s. A long boom pushed unemployment down to 4% or lower. That was an expansion in its late, comfortable stage — the part everyone notices.

How it connects. The other phase of the business cycle (entry 1) and the opposite of a recession (entry 2). It begins at a trough and ends at a peak (entry 5), with employment (entry 6) and aggregate output (entry 9) rising throughout and the unemployment rate (entry 7) falling. It is not economic growth (entry 13) — see the comparison table there.

Check yourself

3.1 Unemployment is 9% and falling. Recession or expansion?

Show answer

Expansion. Falling unemployment means employment is rising, which is the definition — even though 9% is a bad level. Direction, not level.

3.2 Why does your textbook give "recovery" as a second name for the same thing, and when is that name misleading?

Show answer

Because the phase begins with the economy climbing back from a downturn. It misleads late in an expansion, when the economy has long since passed its old level and "recovery" no longer describes anything.

04

Depression

○ Background
Definition

A depression is a very deep and prolonged downturn.

Textbook page 10.

A depression is not a separate phase of the cycle — it is a recession that is much worse and much longer. There is no numerical threshold, which is why the word is used so rarely. The United States has not had one since the 1930s.

It matters historically rather than technically: the Great Depression is why macroeconomics exists as a field. Before it, economists had no serious body of theory about the economy as a whole, and no policy toolkit for a slump.

Watch out. Do not call a bad recession a depression on an exam answer unless the question gives you a reason. Use "recession" — it is the term the framework uses for the contraction phase.

Textbook

Hoovervilles. Shantytowns named after President Hoover sprang up across America after the 1929 slump — one of them in New York's Central Park.

Numeric

How rare. 11 recessions since World War II; zero depressions. Roughly 80 years with none.

Illustrative

Why the word matters. In 2008 people asked whether the downturn was "a depression." The question was really about depth and duration, not a different kind of event.

How it connects. An extreme form of a recession (entry 2), and therefore part of the business cycle (entry 1).

Check yourself

4.1 What exactly distinguishes a depression from a recession?

Show answer

Only depth and duration — a depression is much deeper and lasts much longer. There is no agreed cut-off, so it is a matter of degree, not of kind.

4.2 Why does the study of macroeconomics date from the 1930s?

Show answer

The Great Depression was a catastrophe that existing economics could not explain and policy makers could not fix. Economists started studying the economy as a whole, and looking for policies to prevent a repeat — which is still the core of the subject.

05

Peak and trough

★ Exam
Definition

The peak is the turning point where an expansion ends and a recession begins — the highest point of output before the downturn. The trough is the turning point where a recession ends and an expansion begins — the lowest point of output before the recovery.

Not in your textbook.

⚑ Learn this from here. The word trough appears nowhere in your textbook, and peak is never used in this sense. The AP framework still requires both: the phases of the cycle are recession and expansion, and its turning points are peak and trough. Expect a diagram question that asks you to label all four.

Keep the grammar straight. Recessions and expansions are periods — stretches of time with length. Peaks and troughs are moments — single points where the direction reverses. On a graph the phases are segments of the curve and the turning points are dots.

The names feel backwards to students, because the peak is the start of bad news and the trough is the start of good news. That is exactly the point: the peak is where the economy is at its highest and about to fall.

Watch out. The peak is not where the economy is doing best in any useful sense, and the trough is not where it is worst for people. Unemployment usually keeps rising for a while after the trough. The turning point is about output turning, not about how life feels.

Figure 1

On the diagram above. Press Peak and Trough. Peaks sit at the tops of the wave, troughs at the bottoms, and the shaded recessions run from each peak to the next trough.

Numeric

Dating a cycle. If a peak is December 2007 and the next trough is June 2009, the recession lasted 18 months. If the following peak is February 2020, the expansion lasted 128 months. A full cycle is peak to peak, or trough to trough.

Illustrative

A hill walk. The summit is a moment, not a stretch. You are at the top for an instant; the climb and the descent are the periods.

How it connects. Peaks and troughs are the turning points of the business cycle (entry 1). A peak ends an expansion and starts a recession; a trough does the reverse (entries 2 and 3). The output gap (entry 10) is at its largest at both.

Check yourself

5.1 Put these in order round one full cycle: trough, recession, peak, expansion.

Show answer

Peak → recession → trough → expansion → back to peak. Starting anywhere is fine as long as each period is bounded by the right turning points.

5.2 A country's output falls for a year, then rises for three years. Where is the trough?

Show answer

At the end of the falling year — the moment output stops falling and starts rising. It is the lowest point, and it separates the recession from the expansion.

06

Employment, unemployment and the labor force

★ Exam
Definitions

Employment is the number of people currently employed in the economy.

Unemployment is the number of people who are actively looking for work but are not currently employed.

The labor force is equal to the sum of employment and unemployment.

Textbook page 12.

Three definitions, one idea. Everybody in the country falls into exactly one of three boxes: working, looking for work, or neither. The first two boxes together are the labor force. The third box — retired people, full-time students, people caring for family, anyone who has stopped looking — is not in the labor force at all, and this is where nearly every mistake comes from.

"Actively looking" is doing real work in that definition. Wanting a job is not enough. A person who would take a job but has given up searching is counted as out of the labor force, not as unemployed.

Watch out. The labor force is not everyone old enough to work, and not everyone capable of working. It is the employed plus the actively-searching unemployed. This is the most commonly missed multiple-choice question in the whole module.

Textbook

Reading the job market. Your textbook treats the labor force as the base for judging the job market: employment and unemployment only mean something relative to it.

Numeric

Sorting a town. 150 million employed, 10 million looking, 90 million retired or studying. Labor force = 150 + 10 = 160 million. Adult population = 250 million. The 90 million are in neither of the first two boxes.

Illustrative

Four people. A full-time barista — employed. A graduate sending out applications — unemployed. A retired teacher — not in the labor force. A 15-year-old — not in the labor force, being below working age.

How it connects. These three numbers are what you need before you can calculate the unemployment rate (entry 7) or the labor force participation rate (entry 8). Employment is also the variable that rises in an expansion and falls in a recession (entries 2 and 3).

Check yourself

6.1 Which of these is in the labor force? (a) a full-time student with no job and no search, (b) a part-time waiter, (c) someone laid off and applying for jobs, (d) a retired 70-year-old.

Show answer

(b) and (c). The waiter is employed — part-time still counts as working. The laid-off applicant is unemployed, because they are actively looking. The student and the retiree are not in the labor force.

6.2 Your friend wants a job but stopped applying three months ago. Are they unemployed?

Show answer

Not in the official sense. They are not actively looking, so they are counted as out of the labor force — not as unemployed. Wanting a job is not the test; searching is.

07

Unemployment rate

★ Exam
Definition

The unemployment rate is the percentage of the labor force that is unemployed.

Textbook page 12.

In symbols: unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labor force × 100. The denominator is the labor force, not the population. Getting that denominator wrong is the single most expensive error in Unit 2, because it wrecks every calculation built on it.

The rate is a good, fast read on the job market: high means jobs are hard to find, low means they are easy. It rises during recessions and falls during expansions. But it is never zero — even in a boom, people are between jobs. The late-1990s boom pushed it to 4% or lower; the recession that began in 2007 pushed it into double digits.

Watch out. A falling unemployment rate is not automatically good news. If unemployed people give up searching, they leave the labor force — the numerator falls and the denominator falls, and the rate drops with no one having found a job. Textbook

The 1989–2009 record. Figure 2.1 in your book shows the rate rising sharply in every shaded recession and falling through the expansions between them.

Numeric

Do it by hand. 10 million unemployed, 150 million employed. Labor force = 160 million. Rate = 10 ÷ 160 = 0.0625 = 6.25%. Dividing by the 250 million population instead gives 4% — wrong, and a whole question lost.

Illustrative

The discouraged-worker trap. In the recession scenario above the rate is 12.5%. Five million then stop searching: 15 ÷ 155 = 9.7%. The rate falls by nearly three points and not one person got a job.

How it connects. Built directly on employment, unemployment and the labor force (entry 6), and easily confused with the participation rate (entry 8), which uses a different denominator. It rises in a recession and falls in an expansion (entries 2 and 3), moving opposite to aggregate output (entry 9) — and it never falls to zero, because some unemployment remains even at potential output (entry 10).

Check yourself

7.1 Employed 96 million, unemployed 4 million, adult population 160 million. What is the unemployment rate?

Show answer

Labor force = 96 + 4 = 100 million. Rate = 4 ÷ 100 = 4%. The 160 million is a distractor — it belongs to the participation rate, not this one.

7.2 The unemployment rate fell from 8% to 7% and the number of employed people did not change. What must have happened?

Show answer

Unemployed people left the labor force — they stopped searching. With employment flat, the only way the rate falls is for the unemployed count to fall without those people becoming employed. The labor force shrank, and nothing actually improved.

08

Labor force participation rate

★ Exam
Definition

The labor force participation rate is the percentage of the adult population that is in the labor force.

Not in Module 2 — it appears later in your textbook.

⚑ Learn this from here. Module 2 never mentions the participation rate, but the AP framework requires you to define and calculate it alongside the unemployment rate. Learn the two together now, while the labor force is fresh, rather than meeting this for the first time in a later chapter.

In symbols: participation rate = labor force ÷ adult population × 100. Compare that with the unemployment rate: unemployed ÷ labor force × 100. Same world, different question. The unemployment rate asks of the people in the job market, how many cannot find work? The participation rate asks of the adults who could be in the job market, how many are?

The pair is what makes discouraged workers visible. When people give up searching, the unemployment rate falls — and so does the participation rate. Seeing both numbers move down together is the signal that the "improvement" is not one.

Watch out. Do not mix the denominators. Labor force on the bottom means unemployment rate; adult population on the bottom means participation rate. Write the formula down before you put any numbers in it.

Calculator

Above, in entry 7. Press 5 million give up looking and watch both rates fall at once. That is the whole reason this measure exists.

Numeric

Do it by hand. Labor force 160 million, adult population 250 million. 160 ÷ 250 = 0.64 = 64%. The other 36% are retired, studying, caring for family, or not searching.

Illustrative

A long-run shift. When many more women entered paid work over the twentieth century, the participation rate rose sharply. The unemployment rate says nothing about that change; only participation shows it.

How it connects. Built on the labor force (entry 6), and deliberately contrasted with the unemployment rate (entry 7) — same inputs, different denominator.

Check yourself

8.1 Adult population 200 million, employed 110 million, unemployed 10 million. Find both rates.

Show answer

Labor force = 120 million. Unemployment rate = 10 ÷ 120 = 8.3%. Participation rate = 120 ÷ 200 = 60%.

8.2 A newspaper reports unemployment down for a fourth month. What second number would you ask for, and why?

Show answer

The participation rate. If it is steady or rising while unemployment falls, people really are finding jobs. If it is falling too, people are leaving the labor force and the drop is hollow.

09

Aggregate output

★ Exam
Definitions

Output is the quantity of goods and services produced.

Aggregate output is the economy's total production of goods and services for a given time period, usually a year.

Textbook page 12.

"Aggregate" is the macroeconomist's favourite word and it always means the same thing: added up across the whole economy. One bakery's bread is output. Everything every firm in the country produces in a year is aggregate output.

Output and unemployment move in opposite directions over the cycle, and the reason is mechanical: producing more requires more workers, so when output rises the unemployment rate falls. Aggregate output falls in recessions and rises in expansions — which is the other half of the definition of each phase.

Watch out. Aggregate output is a quantity of goods and services, not a pile of money. If prices rise but the same number of cars roll off the line, aggregate output has not changed. Entry 14 is about exactly this distinction.

Textbook

The link to jobs. Your textbook puts it plainly: at lower levels of output fewer workers are needed, so unemployment is relatively high; growth in output requires more workers, which lowers unemployment.

Numeric

Adding up. A tiny economy makes 400 loaves, 20 haircuts and 3 bicycles this year. Aggregate output is all of it, for the year. Next year the same three industries produce less: aggregate output has fallen, and some of those bakers, barbers and mechanics will lose work.

Illustrative

Why "for a given time period" matters. Output is a flow, like the water leaving a tap, not a stock, like the water in the bath. "The economy produced $20 trillion" is meaningless without "in 2024".

How it connects. Falling aggregate output is half of what defines a recession and rising output half of an expansion (entries 2 and 3). It moves opposite to the unemployment rate (entry 7). Compared against potential output it gives the output gap (entry 10), and divided by population it becomes real GDP per capita (entry 14).

Check yourself

9.1 Aggregate output rises for two years. What happens to unemployment, and why?

Show answer

It falls. Producing more goods and services takes more workers, so firms hire. Output and unemployment move in opposite directions over the cycle.

9.2 A firm's output doubles while the rest of the economy shrinks. Has aggregate output risen?

Show answer

Not necessarily — probably not. Aggregate means the total across every producer. One firm's gain can easily be outweighed by everyone else's losses.

10

Potential output and the output gap

★ Exam
Definitions

Potential output (also called full-employment output) is the level of output the economy produces when unemployment is at its natural rate — the level it can sustain.

The output gap is the difference between actual output and potential output.

Not in Module 2 — developed much later in your textbook.

⚑ Learn this from here. Module 2 gives you the wave but never the line it waves around. The AP framework needs both: the output gap is a required part of describing the business cycle, and you will meet it again in every AD–AS diagram you ever draw. Meet it now as a picture; the arithmetic comes in Unit 3.

Go back to Figure 1 and press Potential output. The dashed line is what the economy could produce with its people and machinery fully — but not over — employed. The solid line is what it actually produced. The vertical distance between them is the gap.

Below the line, at a trough, the economy is wasting capacity: workers idle, factories quiet. Above the line, near a peak, it is running hotter than it can sustain, which is where inflation pressure comes from. This is why a peak is not simply "good".

Watch out. Potential output is not a maximum or a speed limit the economy can never exceed. Actual output can and does run above it for a while — that is what an unsustainable boom is.

Figure 1

The amber marker. Press Output gap on the diagram. The amber bar at the trough is the distance from actual output up to the potential-output line.

Numeric

Sign and size. Potential output $20.0 trillion, actual output $19.2 trillion. The gap is −$0.8 trillion, or −4% of potential: a recessionary gap. If actual were $20.4 trillion the gap would be +2%.

Illustrative

A factory. A plant built to make 1,000 cars a week makes 700 in a slump and 1,050 in a rush, running overtime it cannot keep up. 1,000 is potential; the other two are the gaps.

How it connects. Measured against aggregate output (entry 9). It is largest at peaks and troughs (entry 5), and it is the reason the unemployment rate (entry 7) never reaches zero. It is also the bridge to economic growth (entry 13), which is growth of the dashed line itself.

Check yourself

10.1 Potential output is $18 trillion and actual output is $17.1 trillion. State the gap in dollars and as a percentage of potential.

Show answer

17.1 − 18 = −$0.9 trillion. As a percentage: 0.9 ÷ 18 = 0.05 = −5%. Actual output is below potential, so resources are going unused.

10.2 On Figure 1, where is the gap biggest, and where is it roughly zero?

Show answer

Biggest at the peaks and troughs, where the wave is furthest from the dashed line. Roughly zero where the wave crosses it, partway through each recession and each expansion.

11

Inflation and deflation

★ Exam
Definitions

A rising overall price level is inflation.

A falling overall price level is deflation.

Textbook pages 12–13.

Both definitions turn on the word overall. A change in the price of a few goods changes what those goods cost you relative to everything else — it changes their opportunity cost. It is not inflation. These terms are reserved for general movements in prices throughout the economy.

Each one causes trouble, in opposite ways. Inflation makes holding cash unattractive, because a dollar buys less each month; in extreme cases people stop using money and barter instead. Deflation makes holding cash too attractive — why build a factory when simply sitting on your money makes it worth more? — and that can deepen a recession.

Watch out. "Prices went up" is not inflation. Donuts getting more expensive is one price. Inflation means the general price level rose. Expect this to be tested directly — it is one of the free-response questions at the end of the module.

Textbook

1970 to 2009. Average hourly pay rose from $3.40 to $18.74. But a dozen eggs went from $0.58 to $1.60, bread from $0.20 to $1.39, and gasoline from $0.33 to $2.61. The typical paycheck went about as far in 2009 as in 1970.

Numeric

Check it. Pay multiplied by 18.74 ÷ 3.40 ≈ 5.5. Gasoline multiplied by 2.61 ÷ 0.33 ≈ 7.9, bread by 1.39 ÷ 0.20 ≈ 7.0, eggs by 1.60 ÷ 0.58 ≈ 2.8. Prices rose roughly as much as wages — so the raise was mostly an illusion.

Illustrative

One price is not the price level. If streaming subscriptions get cheaper while everything else holds steady, that is not deflation. It is streaming becoming cheap relative to everything else.

How it connects. Both are departures from price stability (entry 12), and they are the reason a wage figure has to be adjusted before it means anything — the same adjustment that turns output into real GDP per capita (entry 14).

Check yourself

11.1 A drought triples the price of coffee while other prices are steady. Has inflation occurred?

Show answer

No. One price rose. The overall price level has barely moved. What changed is the opportunity cost of buying coffee — you now give up more of other things to get it.

11.2 Why might a firm postpone building a factory when prices are falling?

Show answer

Because cash is gaining value by doing nothing. Holding money beats investing in productive assets, so investment dries up — which reduces output and employment and can deepen a recession.

12

Price stability

★ Exam
Definition

The economy has price stability when the aggregate price level is changing only slowly — or not at all.

Textbook page 13.

Read the definition carefully: stability is slow change, not no change. Economists do not aim for zero inflation, because deflation is dangerous and a small positive rate keeps a safe distance from it. Slow and predictable is the goal.

The reason is planning. When prices move slowly, a wage agreed for next year, a loan repaid over thirty years and a price list printed today all still mean roughly what they meant when they were written. That is what "helps keep the economy stable" amounts to.

Watch out. Price stability does not mean prices are fixed, and it does not mean the economy is doing well overall. An economy can have perfectly stable prices and high unemployment at the same time.

Textbook

Why economists want it. Your textbook gives the reason directly: price stability is desirable because it helps keep the economy stable.

Numeric

Slow versus fast. At 2% a year prices double in about 35 years — a working lifetime. At 20% they double in under four. The first is a background condition; the second reorganises everyone's behaviour.

Illustrative

A thirty-year loan. You agree a fixed monthly repayment for thirty years. That contract only makes sense for both sides if neither expects the price level to do anything dramatic.

How it connects. Defined against inflation and deflation (entry 11) — it is the state in which neither is a problem.

Check yourself

12.1 Why do economists not aim for zero inflation?

Show answer

Because aiming at zero risks overshooting into deflation, which discourages investment and can deepen a downturn. A small, steady, predictable rise is safer than trying to hit exactly nothing.

12.2 Prices rose 1.5% this year and 1.7% last year. Is this price stability?

Show answer

Yes. The price level is changing only slowly, and predictably. Households and firms can plan around it, which is the point of the goal.

13

Economic growth

★ Exam
Definition

Economic growth is an increase in the maximum amount of goods and services an economy can produce.

Textbook page 13.

Every word of that definition is chosen to keep growth apart from an expansion. Growth is an increase in capacity — in what the economy could produce. An expansion is an increase in what it is producing, from a low starting point, and it lasts only until the next downturn. Growth raises the dashed line in Figure 1; an expansion is the wave climbing back towards it.

Growth is why living standards rise. In 1955 Americans felt prosperous, and only 33% of homes had a washing machine; hardly anyone had air conditioning. Nothing about 1955 was a recession — the economy was simply able to produce less. A sustained rise in output per person is what pays for higher wages and a better standard of living, and it is the central concern of policy in poorer countries.

Watch out. This is the trap of the module, and the free-response question at the end tests it. Expansion ≠ economic growth. Learn the table below and you have banked a mark.

One more thing your textbook flags, and it surprises people: the goal of growth can conflict with the goal of a quick recovery. What is good for long-run capacity is not always good for short-run stabilisation, and the other way round.

Textbook

1955 and 1905. Americans in 1955 were rightly proud of their prosperity and would count as poor by today's standards. Go back to 1905 and life was "startlingly primitive". Neither year was a slump.

Numeric

The long view. U.S. aggregate output per person in 2009 was almost nine times its 1900 level. Against that, your textbook notes, recessions are almost invisible on the graph.

Illustrative

Two ways to make more bread. A bakery with idle ovens hires back its staff — that is an expansion. A bakery that buys a second oven can now bake more than it ever could before — that is growth.

How it connects. Deliberately contrasted with an expansion (entry 3) and with the whole short-run business cycle (entry 1). It is a rise in potential output (entry 10), and it is measured by real GDP per capita (entry 14).

Check yourself

13.1 Output rises 4% this year after falling 4% last year. Growth or expansion?

Show answer

Expansion. The economy has returned to roughly where it already was, so its capacity has not increased. Recovering lost ground is not growth.

13.2 Why is growth described as "urgent" in poorer countries?

Show answer

Because a lack of basic necessities is a shortage of productive capacity, not a temporary downturn. Only a sustained rise in output per person can fix it, which makes growth the central concern of policy there.

14

Real GDP per capita

★ Exam
Definition

Real GDP per capita is the value of final goods and services produced in a country, per person, with the effect of price changes removed.

Textbook page 13.

Take the phrase apart, because each piece is doing a job. GDP — total output. Real — measured at constant prices, so a rise means more stuff, not just higher prices. Per capita — divided by population, so a country does not look richer merely for being bigger.

That combination is what makes it the standard measure of a country's standard of living, and the standard way to measure economic growth over time. Your textbook's Figure 2.2 plots it from 1900 to 2009 and the point of the picture is the slope: over the long run, growth dwarfs the business cycle. Apart from the Great Depression, the recessions are almost invisible.

Watch out. Per capita is an average, so it says nothing about how output is shared out. Two countries with the same real GDP per capita can have very different living standards for most of their people.

Textbook

Figure 2.2. U.S. real GDP per capita, 1900–2009, measured in constant 2005 dollars so that the whole century can be compared on one axis.

Numeric

Why each word matters. Output $20 trillion across 250 million people is $80,000 each. If output rises 5% to $21 trillion but prices also rose 5%, real output is unchanged — and if population rose too, real GDP per capita has actually fallen.

Illustrative

Washing machines. In 1955 only a third of American homes had one. That gap is what nine-fold growth in output per person bought — and no unemployment statistic would have shown it.

How it connects. This is how economic growth (entry 13) is measured. It is aggregate output (entry 9) adjusted for population and for inflation (entry 11).

Check yourself

14.1 A country's total output rises 3% while its population rises 4%. Is the typical person better off?

Show answer

No. Output per person has fallen by roughly 1%. There is more produced in total, but less to go round each person — which is exactly why growth is measured per capita.

14.2 Why does your textbook's growth chart use "2005 dollars"?

Show answer

To hold prices constant, so the line shows changes in real production rather than inflation. Without it, a century of rising prices would look like a century of rising output.

15

Model

○ Background
Definition

A model is a simplified representation used to better understand a real-life situation.

Textbook pages 14–15. Not assessed on its own — but you will use models in every single question.

The Wright brothers built a wind tunnel a year after their first glider flight. Testing a wing in a box of moving air is cheaper and safer than building a full aircraft and hoping. A model is economics' wind tunnel: deliberately not the real thing, and useful for that reason.

Economists model in three ways: finding a real but simplified economy (the classic case is a World War II prison camp where cigarettes became money, even among prisoners who did not smoke), simulating one on a computer, or — most often — running a thought experiment on a graph. Nearly every diagram in your course is a model.

Watch out. Do not reject a model because an assumption is unrealistic. Every assumption is unrealistic; that is what makes it an assumption. Ask whether the simplification changes the answer to the question you are asking.

Textbook

Tax models. When a change in tax law is proposed, officials run large computer programs to see how it would affect different groups — a model standing in for an experiment nobody can run.

Numeric

Garbage in, garbage out. A simulation is only as good as its data. Feed a tax model the wrong income distribution and every number it produces is wrong, however sophisticated the program.

Illustrative

The next module. Module 3 asks you to imagine an economy producing exactly two goods. No economy does. That assumption is precisely what makes the trade-off visible as a curve you can measure.

How it connects. Models work by holding everything else constant, which is the other things equal assumption (entry 16). Figure 1 at the top of this guide is a model, and so is the production possibilities curve in Module 3.

Check yourself

15.1 Why is a wind tunnel a good description of what an economic model does?

Show answer

It reproduces the one force you care about — air over a wing — while leaving out everything else about flying. It is cheap, safe, and lets you change one thing at a time. An economic model does the same with an economy.

15.2 Two economists study the same question and reach opposite conclusions. Must one have made a mistake?

Show answer

No. They may have simplified differently — kept different things in the model — and both simplifications can be defensible. The disagreement is then about which simplification fits the facts, which is settled by evidence.

16

Other things equal (ceteris paribus)

★ Exam
Definition

The other things equal assumption means that all other relevant factors remain unchanged. The Latin phrase ceteris paribus means the same thing.

Textbook page 14.

This is the assumption that lets a model answer anything at all. In the real world a dozen things change at once and you can never tell which caused what. Holding everything else fixed lets you trace the effect of one change, which is the only way to reason about cause.

You will use it in every diagram for the rest of the course. When an exam question says a curve shifts, it is telling you that one thing changed and the rest did not. Say so in your answer — it costs one clause and shows you know why the diagram works.

Watch out. "Other things equal" does not mean you assume nothing changes. It means you change one thing and hold the rest still on purpose, so you can see what that one thing does.

Textbook

Why models simplify. Your textbook's reason for the assumption is exactly this: simplicity "allows economists to focus on the effects of only one change at a time."

Numeric

Untangling two causes. Output rose 3% in a year when both taxes fell and oil got cheaper. Without holding one fixed there is no way to say how much of the 3% each caused.

Illustrative

A fair test. The same idea as a school science experiment: change one variable, keep the rest constant, or the result tells you nothing.

How it connects. The working assumption inside every model (entry 15), including Figure 1 and every graph in the rest of this course.

Check yourself

16.1 A question says "other things equal, a fall in income reduces spending." What is being held constant?

Show answer

Everything else that could affect spending — prices, interest rates, expectations, wealth, taxes. Only income is allowed to move, so any change in spending must be down to income.

16.2 Is "other things equal" a realistic description of the world?

Show answer

No, and it is not meant to be. It is a deliberate device for isolating one effect. Once you know each effect separately you can reason about several at once.

The five things to get right from Module 2

  1. Recession and expansion are about direction, not level. Output and employment falling, or rising. An economy with 9% unemployment can be expanding.
  2. The labor force is employed plus actively looking — not everyone old enough to work. Every unemployment calculation stands or falls on this.
  3. Divide by the right thing. Unemployment rate over the labor force; participation rate over the adult population. Write the formula before the numbers.
  4. Expansion is not economic growth. One is the wave, the other is the trend line. This is the module's free-response question.
  5. Learn peak, trough and output gap from this guide. The framework requires them and your textbook does not give them to you here.
