The questions

1. A market for wheat is in equilibrium. A change in one of the determinants of demand then occurs.

Which statement best describes what follows?

  • (A) The market is now in disequilibrium and will return to the same equilibrium price and quantity as before.
  • (B) The quantity traded changes but the equilibrium price cannot change, because no price was set.
  • (C) A permanent surplus or shortage is created.
  • (D) The market moves to a new equilibrium, with a different price and a different quantity.
  • (E) Nothing happens until sellers decide to change their price.

2. A widely reported study finds that a popular breakfast cereal contains more sugar than manufacturers had claimed. Sales fall sharply.

Which curve moves, and in which direction?

  • (A) Supply, to the left — the product now has a problem.
  • (B) Supply, to the right — manufacturers will try to clear their stock.
  • (C) Neither — the news does not change the price of the cereal.
  • (D) Demand, to the right — publicity raises awareness of the product.
  • (E) Demand, to the left — buyers want less of it at every price.

3. The price of tea rises sharply. Tea and coffee are substitutes in consumption.

What happens in the market for coffee?

  • (A) The equilibrium price of coffee rises and the equilibrium quantity rises.
  • (B) The equilibrium price of coffee rises and the equilibrium quantity falls.
  • (C) The equilibrium price of coffee falls and the equilibrium quantity rises.
  • (D) The equilibrium price of coffee falls and the equilibrium quantity falls.
  • (E) Nothing, because the price of coffee has not changed.

4. A cable company cuts the price of pay-per-view films. Local cinemas find that they have more unfilled seats than before.

What has happened in the market for cinema tickets?

  • (A) Supply increased, so the price fell and the quantity rose.
  • (B) Nothing yet — cinemas must first lower their prices for anything to change.
  • (C) Demand decreased, so the equilibrium price fell and the equilibrium quantity rose.
  • (D) Supply decreased, so the price rose and the quantity fell.
  • (E) Demand decreased, so both the equilibrium price and the equilibrium quantity fell.

5. Technological innovation lowers the cost of producing recycled paper.

What happens to the equilibrium price and quantity of recycled paper?

  • (A) Price rises, quantity rises.
  • (B) Price rises, quantity falls.
  • (C) Price falls, quantity rises.
  • (D) Price falls, quantity falls.
  • (E) Price falls, and the quantity cannot be determined.

6. A drought destroys part of a country's coffee crop. The price of coffee rises and less coffee is bought than before.

Which statement describes this correctly?

  • (A) Supply decreased and demand decreased.
  • (B) Supply decreased and demand was unchanged, so quantity supplied rose.
  • (C) Demand decreased and quantity supplied decreased.
  • (D) Supply decreased and quantity demanded decreased.
  • (E) Both curves shifted left, which is why both the price and the quantity changed.

7. Demand for a good increases while supply is unchanged. Consider the instant before the price has adjusted.

What exists at the old price, and what does it do?

  • (A) A surplus, which pushes the price down.
  • (B) A shortage, which pushes the price up.
  • (C) A surplus, which pushes the price up.
  • (D) Nothing — the price adjusts instantly, so no imbalance ever exists.
  • (E) A shortage, which shifts the supply curve right.

8. In each of the four single-shift cases, one curve moves and the other does not.

Which curve do you travel along to reach the new equilibrium?

  • (A) The curve that shifted, because that is where the change happened.
  • (B) It depends on whether the price rose or fell.
  • (C) Both curves, one after the other.
  • (D) Neither — the new equilibrium is found by drawing a vertical line from the old one.
  • (E) The curve that did not shift.

9. Two events are described in the market for bread. First, the price of flour rises. Second, separately, a bakery raises the price it charges for a loaf.

Which pair of effects is correct?

  • (A) Both shift the supply curve for bread left.
  • (B) Both are movements along the supply curve for bread.
  • (C) The flour price rise shifts supply left; the loaf price rise is a movement along the supply curve.
  • (D) The flour price rise is a movement along the supply curve; the loaf price rise shifts it left.
  • (E) The flour price rise shifts supply left; the loaf price rise shifts demand left.

10. A question describes an event that shifts both the demand curve and the supply curve.

What can be said about the two answers — the change in equilibrium price and the change in equilibrium quantity?

  • (A) Exactly one will be determinate and exactly one will be indeterminate.
  • (B) Neither will be indeterminate; two shifts give more information, not less.
  • (C) Both will be indeterminate.
  • (D) Both will be determinate if the shifts are in opposite directions, and both indeterminate if they are in the same direction.
  • (E) It depends entirely on the relative sizes of the two shifts, so nothing general can be said.

11. In the market for a good, demand increases and supply decreases at the same time.

What can be concluded?

  • (A) The equilibrium price rises; the change in equilibrium quantity is indeterminate.
  • (B) The equilibrium quantity rises; the change in equilibrium price is indeterminate.
  • (C) The equilibrium price falls; the change in equilibrium quantity is indeterminate.
  • (D) Both the equilibrium price and the equilibrium quantity rise.
  • (E) The equilibrium quantity is unchanged and the price rises.

12. Demand and supply both decrease.

What can be concluded?

  • (A) The equilibrium price falls; the change in equilibrium quantity is indeterminate.
  • (B) The equilibrium quantity falls; the change in equilibrium price is indeterminate.
  • (C) Both the price and the quantity fall.
  • (D) The equilibrium price rises; the change in equilibrium quantity is indeterminate.
  • (E) The equilibrium quantity falls and the equilibrium price is unchanged, because the two effects on price cancel exactly.

13. Reproduced from the College Board's published sample questions.

Which of the following changes would result in an indeterminate change in the equilibrium price in a perfectly competitive market?

  • (A) An increase in demand and a decrease in supply.
  • (B) An increase in demand and an increase in supply.
  • (C) A decrease in demand and an increase in supply.
  • (D) A decrease in demand with no change in supply.
  • (E) A decrease in supply with no change in demand.

14. In a market, demand increases and supply decreases. A student correctly says the equilibrium price rises but that the quantity cannot be determined.

What single additional piece of information would settle the quantity?

  • (A) The original equilibrium price.
  • (B) Whether the good is normal or inferior.
  • (C) Which of the two curves shifted by the greater amount.
  • (D) Whether the market is competitive.
  • (E) Nothing — the quantity is permanently unknowable in this case.

15. In a market, there is a large increase in demand and a small decrease in supply.

What happens to the equilibrium price and quantity?

  • (A) Price rises; quantity is indeterminate.
  • (B) Price rises; quantity rises.
  • (C) Price rises; quantity falls.
  • (D) Price is indeterminate; quantity rises.
  • (E) Both are indeterminate, because two curves moved.

16. A question asks what happens to equilibrium price and quantity when demand decreases and supply increases. A student writes: "It depends on the relative sizes of the two shifts."

What is wrong with that answer?

  • (A) Nothing — that is the complete and correct response.
  • (B) It is wrong: with two shifts, both variables are always determinate.
  • (C) It throws away the half of the question that is answerable: the price definitely falls, and only the quantity is indeterminate.
  • (D) It should have said the price is indeterminate and the quantity definitely falls.
  • (E) It is wrong because relative sizes never matter; only directions do.

17. A student is asked to show that the effect on equilibrium quantity is indeterminate when demand increases and supply decreases. They draw one careful, correctly labelled diagram in which the quantity rises.

What is the problem?

  • (A) Nothing, provided the diagram is accurate.
  • (B) The diagram needs three panels: quantity rising, falling and unchanged.
  • (C) The quantity should have fallen, so the diagram is simply wrong.
  • (D) Indeterminate results cannot be shown on a diagram at all and must be described in words.
  • (E) The diagram shows one of the possible outcomes, so it demonstrates a determinate result rather than an indeterminate one; a second panel with the opposite relative shift sizes is needed.

18. Two scenarios in the same market.

Scenario I: the market is in equilibrium, and the government does nothing, but a supplier posts a price above the equilibrium price. Scenario II: the market is in equilibrium, and then an input price rises.

Which statement compares them correctly?

  • (A) Scenario I ends at the original equilibrium; Scenario II ends at a new one.
  • (B) Both end at a new equilibrium.
  • (C) Both end at the same equilibrium as before.
  • (D) Scenario I ends at a new equilibrium; Scenario II ends at the original one.
  • (E) Neither reaches an equilibrium, because in both cases something has been disturbed.

19. A free-response question asks a student to draw a market in equilibrium and show the effect of an increase in demand. The printed rubric awards five marks.

A student draws labelled axes, both curves labelled, the original equilibrium with P₁ and Q₁ labelled on the axes, and a correctly placed labelled second demand curve — but does not mark the new price and quantity.

How many of the five marks have they earned?

  • (A) Five — the new equilibrium is visible at the intersection.
  • (B) Two, because an incomplete answer scores only the setup marks.
  • (C) Three, because the shift and the new equilibrium are scored together.
  • (D) Four, losing only the mark for identifying and labelling the new equilibrium price and quantity.
  • (E) One, because the question is about the change and the change is not shown.

20. In the market for cups of coffee, the price of coffee beans falls sharply.

Trace the whole chain: which curve moves, what imbalance appears at the old price, and where the market ends up?

  • (A) Demand shifts right; a shortage appears; the price rises and the quantity rises.
  • (B) Supply shifts left; a shortage appears; the price rises and the quantity falls.
  • (C) Supply shifts right; a shortage appears at the old price; the price rises and the quantity rises.
  • (D) Supply shifts right; a surplus appears at the old price; the price falls and the quantity rises.
  • (E) Demand shifts right; a surplus appears; the price falls and the quantity rises.

Solutions

Question 1 — Change in equilibrium · Application

Answer: (D)

MKT-2.G.1 says changes in the determinants of supply and/or demand result in a new equilibrium price and quantity. A curve moves, so the intersection moves, and the market settles somewhere else.

  • (A) ✗ This is Module 6's situation — a price away from an unchanged intersection — and there

the market does return to the same equilibrium. Here the intersection itself has moved.

  • (B) ✗ Equilibrium prices are not set by anyone; they are where the curves cross, and they move

when a curve moves.

  • (C) ✗ The imbalance at the old price is transient. It disappears once the price reaches the new

equilibrium.

  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Sellers responding to an imbalance is how the price moves, not a reason for nothing to

happen.

Question 2 — Diagnosing the event · Application

Answer: (E)

The diagnostic question is: did the event change what buyers want at a given price, or what sellers offer at a given price? Bad publicity changes what buyers want. Manufacturers can still produce exactly as much cereal as before at any given price.

  • (A) ✗ The commonest error: "the product has a problem" reads as a supply event. Nothing has

happened to the ability to produce it.

  • (B) ✗ Wanting to clear stock is a response to the demand fall, not a shift of supply.
  • (C) ✗ A shift does not require the good's own price to change first — that is precisely what

distinguishes a shift from a movement.

  • (D) ✗ Right curve, wrong direction. The publicity is unfavourable.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 3 — An increase in demand · Application

Answer: (A)

Tea gets dearer, so some tea drinkers switch to coffee: demand for coffee shifts right. At the old coffee price there is now a shortage, the price rises, and sellers move up along an unchanged supply curve. Price and quantity both rise.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ This is the decrease-in-supply outcome. No supply determinant changed.
  • (C) ✗ This is the increase-in-supply outcome.
  • (D) ✗ This is the decrease-in-demand outcome — right curve, wrong direction.
  • (E) ✗ The price of coffee changes as a result; it does not need to change first for anything

to happen.

Question 4 — A decrease in demand · Analysis

Answer: (E)

Cheaper pay-per-view is a cheaper substitute, so demand for cinema tickets shifts left. At the old price there is a surplus — the unfilled seats — the price falls, and cinemas move down along an unchanged supply curve. Both price and quantity fall.

  • (A) ✗ Wrong curve. Nothing changed about cinemas' costs or capacity.
  • (B) ✗ The empty seats are the surplus, and the surplus is what pushes the price down. The

process has already started.

  • (C) ✗ Right curve and right price direction, but a decrease in demand lowers the quantity too.

When demand moves, price and quantity go the same way.

  • (D) ✗ Wrong curve and wrong directions.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 5 — An increase in supply · Application

Answer: (C)

Lower production costs shift supply right. At the old price there is a surplus, the price falls, and buyers move down along an unchanged demand curve, buying more. Price down, quantity up.

  • (A) ✗ This is the increase-in-demand outcome.
  • (B) ✗ This is the decrease-in-supply outcome — right curve, wrong direction.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ This is the decrease-in-demand outcome.
  • (E) ✗ Only one curve moved, so both answers are determinate. Indeterminacy requires two shifts.

Question 6 — A decrease in supply · Analysis

Answer: (D)

Precision matters here and the exam rewards it. Supply decreased — that is a shift, caused by the drought. Quantity demanded decreased — that is a movement, caused by the higher price. Demand itself is exactly where it was.

  • (A) ✗ Demand did not decrease. Buyers' tastes, incomes and numbers are unchanged; they simply

face a higher price.

  • (B) ✗ The first half is right, but quantity supplied falls as well — the new equilibrium

quantity is lower, and quantity supplied equals quantity demanded there.

  • (C) ✗ Exactly reversed. It was supply that shifted and the demand side that moved along.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Only one curve shifted. Two leftward shifts would make the price change indeterminate,

and the stem tells you the price rose.

Question 7 — The adjustment chain · Analysis

Answer: (B)

Demand shifts right while the price is momentarily still at its old level, so at that price the quantity demanded now exceeds the quantity supplied: a shortage. Shortages push prices up.

  • (A) ✗ A surplus arises when the price is above equilibrium, which happens after a demand

decrease or a supply increase.

  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Internally inconsistent: a surplus pushes the price down, never up.
  • (D) ✗ The imbalance is the reason the price adjusts. Removing it removes the mechanism.
  • (E) ✗ A shortage moves the price, which produces a movement along the supply curve. The

supply curve itself does not shift — nothing about sellers' costs changed.

Question 8 — The adjustment chain · Analysis

Answer: (E)

The curve that shifted has moved to a new position; the curve that did not shift is the one you travel along as the price changes. Demand shifts → you move along supply. Supply shifts → you move along demand.

  • (A) ✗ The shifted curve is where you end up, not what you travel along. Every point on it is

available at the new position.

  • (B) ✗ The direction of the price change tells you which way you move along the stationary

curve, not which curve it is.

  • (C) ✗ Only one curve is stationary, so there is only one to move along.
  • (D) ✗ A vertical line would hold the quantity constant, which is exactly what does not happen.
  • (E) ✓ Correct. It is why two cases that both begin with a shortage can end with

opposite quantity answers.

Question 9 — Diagnosing the event · Analysis

Answer: (C)

Two events, two mechanisms. Flour is an input for bread, so a higher flour price shifts the bread supply curve left. A loaf's price is bread's own price, so changing it is a movement along the supply curve.

  • (A) ✗ Only one of the two shifts the curve.
  • (B) ✗ Only one of the two is a movement.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Exactly reversed — the error made by anyone who sees the word "price" and stops reading

which good it belongs to.

  • (E) ✗ A seller raising its own price is a movement along its supply curve, not a shift of

anybody's demand curve.

Question 10 — Simultaneous shifts · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Each shift pushes the price one way and the quantity one way. Across the two shifts, one variable receives two pushes in the same direction and is therefore settled; the other receives two opposing pushes and is not. This is true of all four simultaneous cases.

  • (A) ✓ Correct. It is also a useful self-check: if you have concluded that both are settled

or neither is, you have made an error.

  • (B) ✗ Two shifts give less certainty, not more, about one of the two variables.
  • (C) ✗ One is always determinate. An answer that declares both unknowable has discarded a

markable half.

  • (D) ✗ Opposite directions settle the price only, and the same direction settles the quantity

only. Neither case settles both.

  • (E) ✗ Relative size settles the indeterminate variable. The other one is fixed by direction

alone, which is a general statement.

Question 11 — Opposite directions · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Demand up raises the price; supply down also raises the price — two pushes the same way, so the price definitely rises. Demand up raises the quantity while supply down lowers it — two opposing pushes, so the quantity is indeterminate.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ This is the same-direction result. It has the right structure attached to the wrong case.
  • (C) ✗ Right variable determinate, wrong direction: a demand increase and a supply decrease both

raise the price.

  • (D) ✗ Claims certainty about the quantity that the information does not support.
  • (E) ✗ Unchanged is one of three possible quantity outcomes, and only when the two shifts are

exactly equal. Indeterminate is not the same as unchanged.

Question 12 — Same direction · Analysis

Answer: (B)

Both curves shift left. A demand decrease lowers the quantity and a supply decrease lowers it too, so the quantity definitely falls. But a demand decrease lowers the price while a supply decrease raises it, so the price is indeterminate.

  • (A) ✗ The opposite-direction pattern applied to a same-direction case.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Claims certainty about the price. It could rise, fall or stay the same.
  • (D) ✗ Wrong variable determinate and wrong direction.
  • (E) ✗ Exact cancellation happens only if the two shifts are the same size, which nothing here

states. Reaching a definite answer by assuming it is the error being tested.

Question 13 — "Indeterminate" · Synthesis · the College Board's own sample question

Answer: (B)

The price is indeterminate exactly when the two curves shift in the same direction, because then one shift pushes the price up and the other pushes it down. (B) is the only same-direction option.

The published answer key (CED p. 140) gives B, tagged Learning Objective MKT-2.G, Unit 1, Skill 2.B.

  • (A) ✗ Opposite directions, so the price is determinate — it rises. Here it is the quantity

that is indeterminate.

  • (B) ✓ Correct. Both curves shift right, so the quantity certainly rises and the price could

go either way.

  • (C) ✗ Opposite directions again, so the price is determinate — it falls.
  • (D) ✗ A single shift. Both variables are determinate: price and quantity both fall.
  • (E) ✗ Also a single shift. Price rises and quantity falls, both determinate.

Note the structure of the distractors: two are opposite-direction cases and two are single shifts. Answering this item requires knowing all four simultaneous results and that single shifts are never indeterminate.

Question 14 — Relative magnitude · Synthesis

Answer: (C)

"Indeterminate" means one further fact is needed, and it is always the same fact: which curve shifted further. A demand shift that dominates raises the quantity; a supply shift that dominates lowers it.

  • (A) ✗ The starting price tells you nothing about which shift is larger.
  • (B) ✗ Normal and inferior describe how demand responds to income. Here the demand shift has

already happened; its cause is irrelevant to its size.

  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ The competitive assumption is what allows the model to be used at all. It was already

assumed before the shifts.

  • (E) ✗ It is unknowable only until the relative sizes are given. It is not unknowable in

principle, which is why questions that supply the sizes have definite answers.

Question 15 — Relative magnitude · Analysis

Answer: (B)

Once the relative sizes are given, nothing is indeterminate. A large demand increase against a small supply decrease: the price rises — which was certain anyway — and the demand effect on quantity dominates, so the quantity rises too.

  • (A) ✗ Correct for the general case, but the question has supplied the missing information.

Saying "indeterminate" when the sizes are given throws away a mark.

  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ This would be the answer if the supply shift were the larger one.
  • (D) ✗ The price is determinate in every opposite-direction case; it is never the ambiguous one

here.

  • (E) ✗ Two curves moving does not by itself create indeterminacy — the directions and the

relative sizes settle it, and both are given.

Question 16 — The complete answer · Synthesis

Answer: (C)

Demand down and supply up is an opposite-direction case, so the price definitely falls and only the quantity is indeterminate. An answer that says only "it depends" has not stated the half that can be stated.

The model sentence: "The equilibrium price falls; the change in equilibrium quantity is indeterminate without knowing the relative sizes of the two shifts."

  • (A) ✗ It is half an answer. The price direction is fully determined by the two shift

directions.

  • (B) ✗ Two shifts always leave exactly one variable indeterminate, so the statement is not

simply wrong — it is incomplete.

  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Reverses which variable is which. Opposite directions settle the price, not the quantity.
  • (E) ✗ Relative sizes are exactly what would resolve the quantity. Dismissing them is a second

error on top of the first.

Question 17 — Showing two panels · Synthesis

Answer: (E)

One diagram can only depict one outcome. Drawing a single panel in which the quantity rises demonstrates that the quantity can rise, not that it is indeterminate — and to a reader it looks like a claim that the quantity definitely rises. Two panels, with the relative shift sizes reversed, are what show ambiguity.

  • (A) ✗ Accuracy is not the issue; sufficiency is. The diagram is correct and does not answer the

question asked.

  • (B) ✗ Two panels are enough to establish that the outcome depends on the relative sizes. The

exactly-equal case is a knife-edge and adds nothing.

  • (C) ✗ The quantity rising is a perfectly possible outcome, so the diagram is not wrong.
  • (D) ✗ It can be shown — that is what a second panel is for.
  • (E) ✓ Correct. This is exactly what Figure 7.3 does with its two panels, and what Check

Your Understanding 2 asks for.

Question 18 — Module 6 against Module 7 · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

Scenario I is a price away from an unchanged intersection: a surplus arises, sellers cut prices, and the market returns to the same equilibrium it started from. Scenario II moves a curve: the old intersection no longer exists, and the market settles at a new equilibrium with a higher price and lower quantity.

  • (A) ✓ Correct. It is the single most useful distinction in this part of the course.
  • (B) ✗ True of Scenario II only. In I nothing about buyers or sellers changed.
  • (C) ✗ True of Scenario I only. In II the curve has moved.
  • (D) ✗ Exactly reversed.
  • (E) ✗ Both reach an equilibrium. That is the point of the adjustment process in each case.

Question 19 — Drawing the answer · Application

Answer: (D)

The printed five-mark rubric awards the new equilibrium price and quantity as a separate mark from the new curve. This student has earned marks 1 to 4 and lost mark 5.

  • (A) ✗ Visible is not labelled. The rubric asks for the new price and quantity to be identified

on the axes.

  • (B) ✗ Undercounts badly: the axes, both curves and the original equilibrium are three separate

marks, and the new curve is a fourth.

  • (C) ✗ The shift and the new equilibrium are two distinct marks, not one.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ The change is shown — the new curve is drawn correctly. Only its consequence is

unlabelled.

Question 20 — The whole chain · Analysis

Answer: (D)

Diagnose: beans are an input for a cup of coffee, so a cheaper input changes what sellers offer — supply shifts right. At the old price, quantity supplied now exceeds quantity demanded: a surplus. The surplus pushes the price down, and buyers move down along an unchanged demand curve, buying more. Price falls, quantity rises.

  • (A) ✗ Wrong curve. Beans are bought by sellers of coffee, not by drinkers of it.
  • (B) ✗ Right curve, wrong direction: a cheaper input increases supply.
  • (C) ✗ Right curve and right direction, but a rightward supply shift produces a surplus at

the old price, not a shortage — and the price therefore falls.

  • (D) ✓ Correct. It names all three stages.
  • (E) ✗ Wrong curve, and a demand increase produces a shortage rather than a surplus.

Notes for the teacher

Content validity. All 14 concept entries appear across the 20 items, and there is no background section because the module has no background entries. The weighting is back-loaded on purpose: questions 10 to 17 are all simultaneous-shift items, eight of twenty, against four single-shift items. That is not proportional to the textbook's page count, which spends more space on single shifts. It is proportional to where marks are lost, and to the fact that the only sample item the College Board publishes for MKT-2.G is a double shift.

Question 13 is not mine. It is reproduced verbatim from the CED's sample multiple-choice questions (p. 134), and its solution cites the published answer key (p. 140). A class that can answer it has met the standard the exam board itself chose to illustrate. It is also the only place the word indeterminate appears before the real paper — the phrase occurs twice in the whole Macro CED, both times in the sample exam and never in the required course content.

Bloom levels. Application 5 · Analysis 10 · Synthesis 5, a ratio of 1 : 2 : 1 against the standard's 1 : 3 : 1.75 — the closest of any paper in this unit. The structural reason is worth recording: a module whose entire content is "given a change, determine the outcome" generates analysis items naturally, and its simultaneous cases require two separate results to be combined, which is what synthesis means here. Compare Module 5, which could reach only 1 : 1.67 : 0.67 because its content is largely classification.

Distractor design. Every wrong option encodes a documented error. The recurring families:

  • Wrong curve — reading a demand event as a supply event because the news is bad (2A, 4A, 20A)
  • Right curve, wrong direction (2D, 9D, 20B)
  • The four single-shift results attached to the wrong case — 3B, 3C, 3D, 5A, 5B, 5D are all

correct sentences about a different event

  • Shift and movement confused in vocabulary (6A, 6C, 6E, 9B)
  • The wrong half of the simultaneous rule (11B, 12A, 12D, 16D)
  • "Indeterminate" misread as "unchanged" (11E, 12E)
  • Answering "indeterminate" when the sizes are given (15A) — the mirror error, and the one that

costs marks on a question a student has otherwise understood

Right answer, wrong reasoning. Two options reach a defensible-sounding conclusion by a route that would fail on a written answer, and each is called out in its solution: 6B and 20C.

Answer distribution. A 4 · B 4 · C 4 · D 4 · E 4. The first draft was skewed — B took eleven of the twenty keys — because the correct answer to a directional question so often reads as a short "price rises, quantity falls" clause that lands second in an option list. Ten items were rebalanced by permuting their options, never by rewriting them: questions 1, 6, 19 and 20 moved their key to D; questions 2, 4, 8 and 17 to E; questions 10 and 18 to A. Question 13 was deliberately left alone, because it is reproduced verbatim from the College Board's published sample and its options must appear in the order they were published. The longest run is A at questions 10 and 11.

build/check_m07_test.py recomputes the distribution, the Bloom counts, the longest run and every option letter cited above, so an edit that strands one of them fails the check.

Timing. 35 minutes for 20 items. There is no arithmetic anywhere in this module, so the time is all reading and reasoning; if a class needs longer, the difficulty is the simultaneous cases, and the diagnostic table will show it as a cluster around questions 10 to 17.
