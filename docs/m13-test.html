Section A

1. An experienced accountant lost her job when her firm closed. Accounting jobs are plentiful in her city. She has turned down two offers of clerical work at half her old pay and is applying for accounting positions.

How is her unemployment best classified?

  • (A) Structural, because she is refusing jobs that are available.
  • (B) Cyclical, because she was laid off.
  • (C) She is not unemployed, because she turned down job offers.
  • (D) Frictional, because she is searching for a job that matches her skills.
  • (E) Structural, because her firm closed.

2. In an economy, the number of job openings equals the number of job-seekers in every occupation and every region.

Which of the following is true?

  • (A) There is still some unemployment, because workers take time to find matching jobs and new workers keep entering the labor force.
  • (B) Unemployment is zero, because a job is available for every job-seeker.
  • (C) There is structural unemployment but no frictional unemployment.
  • (D) All the unemployment that remains is cyclical.
  • (E) The natural rate of unemployment is zero.

  • (D) A retired engineer who is not looking for work.
  • (E) Film-camera technicians whose skills are no longer used by any firm in their region, several years into an economic expansion.

5. In the market for unskilled labor, labor demand is Q = 400 − 20W and labor supply is Q = 30W, where Q is thousands of workers and W is the wage in dollars an hour. The government sets a minimum wage of $10 an hour.

Compared with no minimum wage, how much structural unemployment does the floor create, and what happens to the number of people employed?

  • (B) 100 thousand unemployed; employment falls by 40 thousand.
  • (C) 100 thousand unemployed; employment is unchanged.
  • (D) 40 thousand unemployed; employment falls by 40 thousand.

6. In the same market — labor demand Q = 400 − 20W, labor supply Q = 30W — the minimum wage is instead set at $6 an hour.

What is its effect?

  • (A) 100 thousand workers are structurally unemployed.
  • (B) Employment falls to 180 thousand.
  • (C) Structural unemployment rises, because the minimum wage is low.
  • (D) None: the floor is below the equilibrium wage, so it does not bind.
  • (E) Cyclical unemployment of 100 thousand.

7. A country at full employment extends unemployment benefits from 26 to 52 weeks and raises the weekly payment. Assume the economy remains at full employment.

What is the most likely effect?

  • (C) Cyclical unemployment rises, and the natural rate is unchanged.
  • (D) Nothing, because benefits do not affect how long people search.
  • (E) Frictional unemployment rises, but the natural rate is unchanged because it counts only structural unemployment.

Section A — solutions

Question 1 — Job search · Application

Answer: (D)

She is jobless, looking and available, so she is unemployed. Jobs in her occupation are plentiful; what she is doing is job search — Krugman's laid-off programmer (p. 127), who "should take the time to look for a job that takes advantage of her skills." That is frictional unemployment.

  • (A) ✗ Turning down poor matches is part of search. Structural unemployment means the jobs for her skill are not there; here they are.
  • (B) ✗ Being laid off does not make unemployment cyclical. Nothing in the stem is about a slump.
  • (C) ✗ She is still searching, so she is unemployed. Refusing an offer does not move anyone out of the labor force.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ One firm closing is job destruction, not a lasting surplus in her labor market.

Question 2 — Frictional unemployment · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Krugman (p. 128): frictional unemployment "exists even when the number of people seeking jobs is equal to the number of jobs being offered". It is inevitable because jobs are constantly created and destroyed and new workers keep entering (p. 127).

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ A job existing is not the same as a worker having found it. Matching takes time.
  • (C) ✗ The reverse. With no surplus in any market there is no structural unemployment; frictional remains.
  • (D) ✗ Nothing here is caused by a slump in output.
  • (E) ✗ The natural rate includes frictional unemployment, which is never zero.

Question 3 — Frictional unemployment over the cycle · Analysis

Answer: (C)

This is Figure 13.1's argument. When the rate is low, spells are short and most unemployment is frictional; when it is high, more of it is long-term and a "smaller share of unemployment is frictional" (p. 128). The data support a statement about shares.

  • (A) ✗ Shares of different totals. Half of 4% is 2 points of the labor force; 30% of 9% is 2.7 points. If anything, Year Y had more people in short spells.
  • (B) ✗ Frictional unemployment never disappears. Nearly a third of the unemployed in Year Y had short spells.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Nothing in the table separates structural from cyclical unemployment.
  • (E) ✗ Long spells are the mark of the other kinds. The share of frictional unemployment falls in a slump.

Question 4 — Structural unemployment · Analysis

Answer: (E)

A persistent surplus of a particular skill in a particular region, lasting into an expansion: the definition (p. 128) and the test — it survives good times.

  • (A) ✗ A new entrant searching: frictional.
  • (B) ✗ Laid off in a recession with a recall promised: cyclical.
  • (C) ✗ Searching after a move: frictional.
  • (D) ✗ Not looking for work, so not unemployed at all.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 5 — Minimum wages · Synthesis

Answer: (B)

Equilibrium: 400 − 20W = 30W, so W = $8 and Q = 240 thousand. At $10: quantity supplied 30 × 10 = 300, quantity demanded 400 − 200 = 200. Structural unemployment is 300 − 200 = 100 thousand, and employment falls from 240 to 200, by 40 thousand — the two facts Krugman draws from Figure 13.2 (p. 129).

  • (A) ✗ 300 − 240 counts only the new job-seekers the higher wage draws in, and forgets the jobs it destroys.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ The floor raises the cost of labor, so firms hire fewer workers. Employment does not stay at 240.
  • (D) ✗ 240 − 200 is the fall in employment, not the surplus of job-seekers over jobs.
  • (E) ✗ Reads the quantity supplied as employment. Nobody can be employed unless a firm hires them.

Question 6 — Minimum wages · Analysis

Answer: (D)

The equilibrium wage is $8. A floor of $6 is below it, so the market wage stays at $8 and the floor does nothing. Krugman: for most workers "the minimum wage is irrelevant" because the market wage is above it (p. 129). Only a binding floor causes structural unemployment.

  • (A) ✗ At $6, 400 − 120 = 280 would be demanded and 180 supplied — but the wage is never $6, because nothing stops it rising to $8.
  • (B) ✗ Treats the floor as if it held the wage down. A floor stops the wage falling, not rising.
  • (C) ✗ A lower floor binds less, not more.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ A floor is not a cause of cyclical unemployment, and this one does not bind.

Question 7 — Unemployment benefits · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Generous benefits reduce the incentive to find a job quickly and "increase both structural and frictional unemployment" (p. 132) — the natural rate rises. The economy stays at full employment, so the actual rate rises with the natural rate and cyclical unemployment remains zero.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Being able to afford a longer search lengthens it. That raises unemployment.
  • (C) ✗ Benefits change the natural rate, not the gap between the actual and natural rates.
  • (D) ✗ The textbook says the opposite: European benefits are blamed for Eurosclerosis.
  • (E) ✗ The natural rate is frictional plus structural. A rise in frictional raises it.

Question 8 — Natural rate of unemployment · Application

Answer: (C)

Natural = frictional + structural = 2.4 + 1.6 = 4.0%. Cyclical unemployment is not part of it.

  • (A) ✗ Frictional alone.
  • (B) ✗ Structural alone.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ 2.4 + 1.6 + 2.0 is the actual rate, not the natural rate.
  • (E) ✗ 1.6 + 2.0 adds cyclical to structural and drops frictional.

Question 9 — Full employment and the natural rate · Analysis

Answer: (E)

The natural rate is the unemployment rate "when the economy produces full-employment real output" — the framework's definition, which the student guide supplies in entry 10. At full employment cyclical unemployment is zero, and what remains is frictional and structural.

  • (A) ✗ Full employment does not mean zero unemployment.
  • (B) ✗ At full employment there is no cyclical unemployment at all.
  • (C) ✗ Unemployment above zero is normal. A recession means unemployment above the natural rate.
  • (D) ✗ Cyclical unemployment is not always present; at full employment it is zero, so the natural rate here is 4.5%.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 10 — Cyclical unemployment · Application

Answer: (B)

Laid off because output fell across the economy, with the job expected back when it recovers — the definition of cyclical unemployment, and the textbook's Melanie, part (c).

  • (A) ✗ Searching while laid off does not make the cause search.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Nothing in the stem says the industry is shrinking for good. Orders collapsed in a recession and are expected to recover.
  • (D) ✗ The natural rate is not a type, and it excludes unemployment caused by the cycle.
  • (E) ✗ Workers on temporary layoff awaiting recall are counted as unemployed.

Question 11 — Actual = natural + cyclical · Synthesis

Answer: (D)

Actual = 6.4 ÷ 80 × 100 = 8%. Natural = (2.0 + 1.6) ÷ 80 × 100 = 4.5%. Cyclical = 8 − 4.5 = 3.5 points — equivalently 2.8 million people ÷ 80 million.

  • (A) ✗ Uses 3.6 million as if it were 3.6%, then subtracts it from 8.
  • (B) ✗ Reads every number of people in millions as a percentage.
  • (C) ✗ Rates for the first two, but leaves cyclical in millions of people.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ 2.0 ÷ 80 = 2.5% counts frictional unemployment only as the natural rate.

Question 12 — Actual = natural + cyclical · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Natural = actual − cyclical = 7.2 − 2.2 = 5.0%. Structural = natural − frictional = 5.0 − 3.0 = 2.0%.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ 7.2 − 3.0 subtracts frictional from the actual rate and forgets cyclical.
  • (C) ✗ That is the natural rate, one step short.
  • (D) ✗ 3.0 − 2.2 subtracts cyclical from frictional, which has no meaning.
  • (E) ✗ Cyclical unemployment, not structural.

Question 13 — When the actual rate is below the natural rate · Analysis

Answer: (E)

Cyclical = actual − natural = 5 − 6 = −1 point. Unemployment below the natural rate means output above full employment — Krugman, Module 33 (p. 328): "When the output gap is positive (an inflationary gap), the unemployment rate is below the natural rate." These two figures open the CED's sample FRQ 1.

  • (A) ✗ The subtraction run backwards. A recession has the actual rate above the natural rate.
  • (B) ✗ A one-point gap is a gap. Cyclical unemployment is zero only when the rates are equal.
  • (C) ✗ The actual rate can fall below the natural rate, for a while, in a boom.
  • (D) ✗ The sign is right; the output conclusion is backwards.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 14 — When the actual rate is below the natural rate · Synthesis

Answer: (C)

At 5% against 5% cyclical unemployment was zero. At 3.8% against 5% it is 3.8 − 5 = −1.2 points. Spending pushed output above its full-employment level, and unemployment below its natural rate.

  • (A) ✗ The cyclical figure is right; falling unemployment comes with rising output, not falling.
  • (B) ✗ Subtracts the wrong way round, turning a boom into a slump.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ The stem fixes the natural rate at 5%. Spending moves the actual rate.
  • (E) ✗ A deviation runs both ways; negative cyclical unemployment is exactly what a boom produces.

Question 15 — Telling the three types apart · Analysis

Answer: (B)

I lost his job because output fell across the economy: cyclical. II is a new entrant searching: frictional. III are a lasting surplus of a skill no local employer needs: structural — like Melanie's outsourced job in the review's FRQ 2(b).

  • (A) ✗ Swaps I and III.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Swaps II and III.
  • (D) ✗ The steelworker is not searching after a quit or a move; a recession took his job.
  • (E) ✗ The graduate's unemployment has nothing to do with the cycle.

Question 16 — Telling the three types apart · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

Structural and cyclical unemployment are both a surplus of job-seekers. What separates them is whether the surplus survives full employment: cyclical unemployment disappears when output recovers; structural unemployment is part of the natural rate and does not.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Points towards cyclical, if anything.
  • (C) ✗ All unemployed people are searching; it distinguishes nothing.
  • (D) ✗ Benefits affect how long people search, not which type their unemployment is.
  • (E) ✗ A local rate above the national one fits a local slump as well as a dying industry.

Question 17 — Changes in labor force characteristics · Analysis

Answer: (D)

Experienced workers stay in jobs longer, so they have less frictional unemployment (p. 131). An older, more experienced labor force is one likely reason the U.S. natural rate fell after the 1970s.

  • (A) ✗ A change in the labor force's make-up moves the natural rate. It is not a slump.
  • (B) ✗ The textbook's evidence runs the other way: 8.6% for ages 25–54 against 26.4% for teenagers in January 2010.
  • (C) ✗ The framework's own example of what moves the natural rate is labor force characteristics.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Not the mechanism the textbook gives, and the direction is wrong.

Question 18 — Changes in institutions and policies · Analysis

Answer: (E)

Faster matching shortens job search. Krugman (p. 131): websites "may have reduced frictional unemployment". Frictional unemployment is part of the natural rate, so the natural rate falls.

  • (A) ✗ Matching has nothing to do with the business cycle.
  • (B) ✗ Search is frictional by definition. The website shortens it.
  • (C) ✗ The question is about the natural rate; shorter searches lower it.
  • (D) ✗ It does not create jobs, but it fills existing ones faster, which is the point.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 19 — Changes in institutions and policies · Application

Answer: (C)

Job training "widen[s] the range of jobs" workers can perform (p. 132) and so reduces structural unemployment — one of Krugman's two examples of policies that lower the natural rate.

  • (A) ✗ A higher binding minimum wage raises structural unemployment.
  • (B) ✗ More generous benefits raise frictional and structural unemployment.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ That works on cyclical unemployment, not the natural rate.
  • (E) ✗ Wages bargained above equilibrium raise structural unemployment.

Question 20 — Cyclical against natural · Synthesis

Answer: (B)

Cyclical = actual − natural = 5.8 − 5.8 = 0, as before. The labor force got younger and less experienced, which raises frictional unemployment and so the natural rate (p. 131). The actual rate rose with it.

  • (A) ✗ Cyclical unemployment is measured from the natural rate, which also rose.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ The actual rate is still equal to the natural rate. There is no slump.
  • (D) ✗ Young, first-time workers change jobs often: the rise is frictional.
  • (E) ✗ The actual rate rose by the same amount, so the gap is unchanged.

Question 21 — Two changes at once · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

Cyclical = actual − natural = 8 − 5.5 = 2.5 points. The recession raised the actual rate by 3 points; half a point of that rise is the higher natural rate.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ 8 − 5 measures from the old natural rate.
  • (C) ✗ Adds the change in the natural rate instead of subtracting it.
  • (D) ✗ That is the actual rate.
  • (E) ✗ The change in the natural rate, not cyclical unemployment.

Section B — solutions

Question 22 — Job creation and job destruction · Analysis

Answer: (D)

The textbook's own case (p. 126): in July 2007 the rate was 4.7% and there were 4.5 million job separations. "This constant churning of the workforce is an inevitable feature of the modern economy."

  • (A) ✗ The rate counts people out of work at a moment; those who quickly find new jobs barely touch it.
  • (B) ✗ Separations on that scale happen in good times too.
  • (C) ✗ Most people who quit or are fired move to new jobs; some search for a while, frictionally.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Job separations count both, "either fired or quit voluntarily."

Question 23 — Labor unions and efficiency wages · Analysis

Answer: (E)

Efficiency wages are set above equilibrium "as an incentive for better employee performance" (p. 130). "When many firms pay efficiency wages, the result is a pool of workers who want jobs but can't find them" — structural unemployment, as with a minimum wage.

  • (A) ✗ Firms can choose the wage; the effect on the labor market follows anyway.
  • (B) ✗ Nothing here is about the business cycle.
  • (C) ✗ Jobs fill quickly, but more people want them than there are jobs.
  • (D) ✗ A wage above equilibrium creates a surplus of workers, not a shortage.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 24 — Seasonal unemployment · Application

Answer: (C)

The framework names three types, and a predictable, temporary spell of search between jobs behaves like frictional unemployment. Seasonal occurs nowhere in Module 13.

  • (A) ✗ The business cycle is not the seasons. Cyclical unemployment comes from falls in output.
  • (B) ✗ His skill is wanted every winter. There is no lasting surplus.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ He has no job and is looking, so he is counted.
  • (E) ✗ The natural rate is not a type.
