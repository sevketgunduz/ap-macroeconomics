Income, Spending and the Multiplier

Sixteen terms from Module 16 of Krugman’s Economics for AP: the marginal propensities to consume and save, how one change in spending becomes several rounds of income, and what moves consumer and investment spending — plus the tax multiplier and the other multiplier calculations your exam asks for, which your textbook leaves out.

The 16 terms

01

Disposable income

★ Exam
Definition

Income after taxes are paid and government transfers are received. It is what households can actually spend or save.

Textbook page 161.

Start from what people earn. Subtract the taxes they pay. Add the transfers they receive — unemployment benefits, Social Security checks. What is left is disposable income.

It matters here because it is what consumer spending depends on. Anything that changes it — more output and wages, a tax cut, a bigger benefit check — changes how much people spend.

Watch out. Taxes and transfers move disposable income before anyone spends a cent. That is exactly how a tax cut reaches spending (entry 8).

Textbook

Figure 16.1, point A. The middle fifth of American households in 2008 had average disposable income of $46,936 and spent $42,659 of it.

Numeric

One household. Earnings $50,000, taxes $8,000, transfers $2,000: disposable income = 50,000 − 8,000 + 2,000 = $44,000.

Illustrative

A tax rebate. The government sends you a $1,000 rebate. Your disposable income is $1,000 higher today, though you produced nothing more.

How it connects. Spending out of it is measured by the MPC (entry 2). Taxes change it directly, which is where the tax multiplier (entry 8) starts.

Check yourself

1.1 You earn $60,000, pay $12,000 in taxes and receive no transfers. What is your disposable income?

Show answer

60,000 − 12,000 = $48,000.

1.2 The government raises a family’s benefits by $500 a year. What happens to its disposable income?

Show answer

It rises by $500, before the family spends any of it.

02

Marginal propensity to consume (MPC)

★ Exam
Definition

The change in consumer spending divided by the change in disposable income: how much of an extra dollar of disposable income is spent.

Textbook page 159, margin and equation 16-1.

People usually spend part, but not all, of an extra dollar. So the MPC is a number between 0 and 1. An MPC of 0.6 means 60 cents of each extra dollar is spent.

It is a ratio of changes. Divide the extra spending by the extra income — never total spending by total income.

Watch out. Total spending ÷ total income is a different number. At point A it is 42,659 ÷ 46,936 = 0.91, but the MPC the data give is about 0.53.

Textbook

Page 159. Spending rises by $6 billion when disposable income rises by $10 billion: 6 ÷ 10 = 0.6.

Numeric

A raise. Your take-home pay rises by $1,000 and you spend $750 more: MPC = 750 ÷ 1,000 = 0.75.

Illustrative

American households, 2008. The line fitted to the data in Figure 16.3 has an MPC of 267/500, about 0.53.

How it connects. It is spending out of disposable income (entry 1); what is not spent is the MPS (entry 3). It sets the size of each round of the multiplier process (entry 5), and it is the slope of the consumption function (entry 12).

Check yourself

2.1 Disposable income rises by $2,000 and consumer spending by $1,600. What is the MPC?

Show answer

1,600 ÷ 2,000 = 0.8.

2.2 Why is the MPC normally between 0 and 1?

Show answer

Because people spend some of an extra dollar and save the rest.

03

Marginal propensity to save (MPS)

★ Exam
Definition

The fraction of an extra dollar of disposable income that is saved. MPC + MPS = 1.

Textbook page 159, margin.

Every extra dollar is either spent or saved, so the two fractions add up to one. If you know one, you know the other: MPS = 1 − MPC.

Saving is the leak in the multiplier. The money saved in each round is not spent, so it does not become anyone else’s income.

Watch out. The MPS is about the extra dollar. It is not the share of all income that is saved.

Textbook

Page 164. An MPC of about 0.53 means an MPS of about 1 − 0.53 = 0.47.

Numeric

From the MPC. MPC 0.75, so MPS = 1 − 0.75 = 0.25.

Illustrative

A $1,000 raise. You spend $800 and save $200: MPC 0.8, MPS 0.2, and together they make 1.

How it connects. It is the other half of the MPC (entry 2), and the expenditure multiplier (entry 6) is one divided by it.

Check yourself

3.1 The MPC is 0.9. What is the MPS?

Show answer

1 − 0.9 = 0.1.

3.2 Disposable income rises by $500 and saving by $100. What are the MPS and the MPC?

Show answer

MPS = 100 ÷ 500 = 0.2, so MPC = 0.8.

04

Autonomous change in spending

★ Exam
Definition

An initial rise or fall in spending at a given level of real GDP. It is the cause of the chain reaction, not its result.

Textbook page 160, margin.

Something has to start the multiplier: firms decide to build more, the government buys more, foreigners buy more exports, or households decide to spend more at every income. That first change is autonomous — “self-governing”.

The extra consumer spending in later rounds is different. It happens because incomes rose. It is induced spending, and the multiplier already counts it.

Watch out. Do not count the later rounds as a second autonomous change. Only the first change goes into the formula.

Textbook

Home builders. Builders decide to spend an extra $100 billion on homes. That $100 billion is the autonomous change.

Numeric

Feeling richer. A housing boom makes households spend $20 billion more at every level of income. The autonomous change in consumer spending is $20 billion.

Illustrative

Four places to start. Consumer spending, investment, government purchases or net exports — an autonomous change in any of them starts a multiplier.

How it connects. It starts the multiplier process (entry 5). It can come from a shift in consumer spending (entry 13) or in planned investment (entry 14).

Check yourself

4.1 Consumer spending rises because incomes rose in the last round. Is that an autonomous change?

Show answer

No. It is induced by the rise in income, and the multiplier already counts it.

4.2 Name the four kinds of spending whose autonomous change can start a multiplier.

Show answer

Consumer spending, investment spending, government purchases and net exports.

05

The multiplier process

★ Exam
Definition

One person’s spending is another’s income. An autonomous change in spending raises income, which raises spending, which raises income again — in rounds that shrink by the MPC each time.

Textbook pages 158–160.

Round 1 is the autonomous change itself. Round 2 is the MPC times that, because the people who earned it spend that share. Round 3 is the MPC times round 2, and so on.

The rounds get smaller because some income leaks into saving each time. So the total is finite: it adds up to a multiple of the first change. It works in reverse too — a fall in spending shrinks income round after round.

Watch out. The rounds shrink by the MPC, not by the MPS. With an MPC of 0.6, each round is 60% of the last.

Textbook

Page 160. MPC 0.6 and $100 billion of new investment: rounds of $100 billion, $60 billion, $36 billion… adding up to $250 billion.

Numeric

The diagram above. MPC 0.8 and $10 billion: rounds of $10, $8 and $6.4 billion, and the total is $50 billion.

Illustrative

The Great Depression. From 1929 to 1933 investment fell by $82.8 billion and real GDP by $260.6 billion — about 3.1 times as much (2005 dollars).

How it connects. Each round’s size is set by the MPC (entry 2); it starts with an autonomous change (entry 4); its total is the expenditure multiplier (entry 6).

Check yourself

5.1 MPC 0.75 and $40 billion of new investment. What are the first three rounds?

Show answer

$40 billion, $30 billion, $22.5 billion.

5.2 Why does the total stop growing even though the rounds go on?

Show answer

Because part of each round is saved, so every round is smaller than the last.

06

Expenditure multiplier

★ Exam

⚑ The name is not in your textbook — learn it from here. Your textbook calls this just the multiplier. Your exam calls it the expenditure multiplier or the spending multiplier, to tell it apart from the tax multiplier.

Definition

Expenditure multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) = 1 ÷ MPS. Change in real GDP = multiplier × the autonomous change in spending.

Textbook page 160, equation 16-4, called “the multiplier”.

The rounds 1 + MPC + MPC² + … add up to 1 ÷ (1 − MPC). So you never need to add rounds: divide one by the MPS and multiply.

The bigger the MPC, the bigger the multiplier, because less leaks out at each round. It applies to an autonomous change in any kind of spending — consumer, investment, government or net exports.

Watch out. Divide by 1 − MPC, not by the MPC. With an MPC of 0.8 the multiplier is 5, not 1.25.

Textbook

Your AP Review, question 2. MPC 0.5 gives a multiplier of 1 ÷ 0.5 = 2; MPC 0.8 gives 1 ÷ 0.2 = 5.

Numeric

Page 160. MPC 0.6: 1 ÷ 0.4 = 2.5, so $100 billion of investment raises real GDP by $250 billion.

Illustrative

American households. Your textbook’s MPC of about 0.53 gives 1 ÷ 0.47, a multiplier of about 2.13.

How it connects. It is one divided by the MPS (entry 3), and the total of the rounds (entry 5). It sets the shift in aggregate demand (entry 7); compare it with the tax multiplier (entry 9); and run it backwards in entry 11.

Check yourself

6.1 The MPC is 0.9. What is the expenditure multiplier?

Show answer

1 ÷ 0.1 = 10.

6.2 The MPS is 0.25 and government purchases rise by $20 billion. By how much can real GDP rise?

Show answer

Multiplier 1 ÷ 0.25 = 4, so 4 × 20 = $80 billion.

07

The multiplier shifts aggregate demand

★ Exam

⚑ Not in this module — learn it from here. Module 16 keeps all prices fixed and never mentions aggregate demand. Your exam describes the multiplier as the size of the shift in aggregate demand, and asks for the change in real GDP “at most”.

Definition

The multiplier gives how far aggregate demand shifts. Real GDP rises by that full amount only if the price level does not rise — so it is the maximum change in real GDP.

Textbook page 158, assumption 1: prices are fixed.

Your textbook assumes firms will produce any extra output at the same prices. Then every dollar of extra spending becomes a dollar of extra real GDP, and the multiplier gives the whole change.

In the aggregate demand and supply model you meet next, the multiplier tells you how far the AD curve shifts left or right. If prices rise as output grows, part of the shift becomes higher prices, not more output. That is why exam answers say real GDP rises “by a maximum of” the amount.

Watch out. The multiplier is the size of the shift, measured along the real GDP axis. It is not a promise that output rises that much.

Textbook

Assumption 1. Producers supply extra output at a fixed price, so changes in spending become changes in real GDP. Your textbook says this needs changing for the long run.

Numeric

A fall in investment. Investment falls by $30 billion and the MPC is 0.8: aggregate demand shifts left by 5 × 30 = $150 billion.

Illustrative

Near full capacity. An economy already producing flat out gets $100 billion of new spending. Aggregate demand shifts right by the full multiplier, but much of it shows up as higher prices.

How it connects. Its size is the expenditure multiplier (entry 6) times the change in spending.

Check yourself

7.1 The MPC is 0.75 and government purchases rise by $10 billion. How far does aggregate demand shift?

Show answer

4 × 10 = $40 billion to the right.

7.2 Why do answers say real GDP rises “by a maximum of” the multiplier amount?

Show answer

Because if the price level rises, part of the shift in demand becomes higher prices instead of more output.

08

Tax multiplier

★ Exam

⚑ Not in your textbook — learn it from here. Module 21 shows a transfer working through the rounds in a table, but your textbook never names the tax multiplier or writes its formula. Your exam does both.

Definition

Tax multiplier = −MPC ÷ (1 − MPC) = −MPC ÷ MPS. A tax cut raises real GDP; a tax increase lowers it.

Not in your textbook. Module 21 (pages 210–211) works a transfer through the rounds.

A tax cut is not spending. It raises disposable income, and households spend only the MPC share of it. So round 1 is MPC × the tax cut, not the whole amount — and every round after is one step behind the spending case.

The sign is negative because taxes and output move in opposite directions. A transfer payment works like a tax cut: the same size of multiplier, with a plus sign.

Watch out. Round 1 of a tax cut is MPC × the cut. Households save part of it straight away.

Textbook

Module 21, Table 21.1. A $50 billion transfer with an MPC of 0.5: households spend $25 billion in round 1, and real GDP rises by $50 billion in the end.

Numeric

A tax cut. MPC 0.8 and a $10 billion tax cut: tax multiplier −0.8 ÷ 0.2 = −4, so real GDP can rise by $40 billion.

Illustrative

A tax increase. MPC 0.75 and a $20 billion tax increase: −3 × 20 = −$60 billion.

How it connects. It works through disposable income (entry 1). Compare it with the spending multiplier in entry 9; combine the two in entry 10; run it backwards in entry 11.

Check yourself

8.1 The MPC is 0.9. What is the tax multiplier?

Show answer

−0.9 ÷ 0.1 = −9.

8.2 The MPC is 0.6 and taxes rise by $10 billion. What is the largest effect on real GDP?

Show answer

Tax multiplier −0.6 ÷ 0.4 = −1.5, so −$15 billion.

09

The spending multiplier is bigger than the tax multiplier

★ Exam

⚑ Not in this module — learn it from here. Module 21 compares the two in a table. Your exam states it as a rule and expects you to explain it.

Definition

For the same MPC, a change in government purchases moves real GDP more than an equal change in taxes. In size, the spending multiplier is exactly 1 bigger.

Module 21, page 210, Table 21.1.

Government purchases are spending in round 1, all of it. A tax cut reaches spending only after households save part of it. So the tax cut’s chain starts smaller and stays smaller.

The difference is the first round. 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) minus MPC ÷ (1 − MPC) is (1 − MPC) ÷ (1 − MPC) = 1.

Watch out. Compare the sizes. The tax multiplier is negative, so “−4 is smaller than 5” is true but not the point: 4 is smaller than 5.

Textbook

Module 21, Table 21.1. MPC 0.5: $50 billion of purchases raises real GDP by $100 billion; $50 billion of transfers raises it by $50 billion.

Numeric

The diagram above. MPC 0.8: spending multiplier 5, tax multiplier −4. Switch between the first two buttons and watch round 1.

Illustrative

Building a bridge or sending checks. Spending $10 billion on a bridge pays $10 billion of wages at once. Sending $10 billion in checks does not, if some of it is saved.

How it connects. It compares the expenditure multiplier (entry 6) with the tax multiplier (entry 8). The gap of exactly 1 is why entry 10 works.

Check yourself

9.1 The MPC is 0.75. Give both multipliers and the gap between their sizes.

Show answer

Spending 4, tax −3; in size they differ by 1.

9.2 Why does a tax cut raise real GDP by less than the same increase in government purchases?

Show answer

Because households save part of the tax cut before anything is spent, so the first round is smaller.

10

Changing spending and taxes together

★ Exam

⚑ Not in your textbook — learn it from here. Raising spending and taxes by the same amount is a classic exam question. Your textbook never works it.

Definition

Raise government purchases and taxes by the same amount and real GDP rises by that amount, at most. The combined multiplier is 1, whatever the MPC.

Not in your textbook.

Do the two parts separately and add. The spending raises real GDP by the spending multiplier times the amount. The tax lowers it by the tax multiplier times the amount. Because the two multipliers differ by exactly 1 in size, what is left is the amount itself.

So a budget that stays balanced is not neutral. More spending paid for by equal taxes still expands the economy.

Watch out. The answer is not zero. The two changes do not cancel, because the tax’s first round is smaller.

Textbook

Not worked in your textbook. Module 21’s table has both halves: with an MPC of 0.5, $50 billion of purchases adds $100 billion and $50 billion of transfers adds $50 billion. A $50 billion tax rise would take away $50 billion, leaving $50 billion.

Numeric

MPC 0.9. Purchases and taxes both rise by $50 million: 10 × 50 − 9 × 50 = 500 − 450 = $50 million.

Illustrative

The diagram above. Press the third button at every MPC. The rounds change shape, but the total is always $100 billion.

How it connects. It adds the tax multiplier (entry 8) to the spending multiplier, using the gap of 1 from entry 9.

Check yourself

10.1 The MPC is 0.75. Government purchases and taxes both fall by $20 billion. What happens to real GDP?

Show answer

−4 × 20 + 3 × 20 = −80 + 60 = −$20 billion.

10.2 Does the answer to a balanced change depend on the MPC?

Show answer

No. It always equals the change in spending.

11

Working backwards: how big a change is needed?

★ Exam

⚑ Not in your textbook — learn it from here. Your textbook always goes from a change in spending to a change in real GDP. Your exam also asks the other way round: how big a change is needed?

Definition

To move real GDP by a target amount, divide the target by the multiplier: change in purchases = target ÷ spending multiplier; change in taxes = target ÷ tax multiplier.

Not in your textbook.

Policymakers start from the gap they want to close and ask how much to spend or how much to cut taxes. Divide the target by the right multiplier.

A tax cut has to be bigger than the equivalent spending increase, because its multiplier is smaller. Its sign comes out negative: taxes must be cut to raise output.

Watch out. Divide; do not multiply. A $100 billion target with a multiplier of 4 needs $25 billion, not $400 billion.

Textbook

American households. With your textbook’s estimated multiplier of about 2.13, raising real GDP by $100 billion takes about 100 ÷ 2.13 = $46.9 billion of new spending.

Numeric

MPC 0.75. To raise real GDP by $100 billion: purchases up by 100 ÷ 4 = $25 billion, or taxes cut by 100 ÷ 3 = $33.3 billion.

Illustrative

Cooling an economy. Real GDP is $50 billion above where policymakers want it and the MPC is 0.8. Cut purchases by 50 ÷ 5 = $10 billion, or raise taxes by 50 ÷ 4 = $12.5 billion.

How it connects. It runs the expenditure multiplier (entry 6) and the tax multiplier (entry 8) backwards.

Check yourself

11.1 The MPC is 0.9. How much must government purchases rise to raise real GDP by $50 billion?

Show answer

50 ÷ 10 = $5 billion.

11.2 The MPC is 0.8. How big a tax cut raises real GDP by $40 billion?

Show answer

40 ÷ 4 = $10 billion.

12

The consumption function

○ Background
Definition

c = a + MPC × yd: a household’s spending equals autonomous consumer spending a, plus the MPC times its disposable income.

Textbook pages 162–164, equations 16-5 and 16-9, Figures 16.2 and 16.3.

Drawn with disposable income across and spending up, it is a straight line. Its vertical intercept is autonomous consumer spending — what the household would spend with no income, by borrowing or using savings. Its slope is the MPC.

Your exam does not use the equation by name. It is a good way to see the MPC as a slope, and your textbook’s review uses it.

Watch out. A change in current disposable income is a movement along the line. Only other things — entry 13 — shift it.

Textbook

Your AP Review, question 3. c = $16,000 + 0.5 × $20,000 = $26,000.

Numeric

Your AP Review, free response 1. c = $15,000 + 0.8 × $40,000 = $47,000.

Illustrative

The fitted line, Figure 16.3. c = $17,484 + 0.534 × yd. A household with $50,000 of disposable income is predicted to spend about $44,184.

How it connects. Its slope is the MPC (entry 2); entry 13 shifts it; entry 16 explains why current income is not the whole story.

Check yourself

12.1 A household’s consumption function is c = $10,000 + 0.6 yd. What does it spend at a disposable income of $30,000?

Show answer

10,000 + 18,000 = $28,000.

12.2 What does the vertical intercept of the consumption function show?

Show answer

Autonomous consumer spending: what the household would spend with no disposable income.

13

What shifts consumer spending

★ Exam
Definition

Expected future disposable income and wealth. A rise in either makes households spend more at every level of current income — an autonomous rise in consumer spending.

Textbook pages 165–166, Figure 16.4.

A student with a well-paid job starting in six months spends more now. A worker who fears a layoff spends less now. Neither one’s current income has changed: their expectations have.

Wealth works the same way. Two people with the same income spend differently if one owns a house and savings. A stock market boom raises wealth across the economy; a fall in house prices lowers it. Each is an autonomous change in spending, so each starts a multiplier.

Watch out. A change in wealth from booming stocks or houses shifts spending. A fall in the price level that raises the real value of money is a different idea — the real wealth effect — and it is a movement along aggregate demand, not a shift.

Textbook

Maria and Mark. Both expect to earn $30,000. Maria owns a home and has $200,000 in the bank; Mark has little. Other things equal, Maria spends more.

Numeric

A stock boom. Households spend $10 billion more at every income and the MPC is 0.75: real GDP can rise by 4 × 10 = $40 billion.

Illustrative

2008. House prices fell, household wealth fell, and consumer spending fell at every level of income.

How it connects. Each is an autonomous change (entry 4); it shifts the consumption function (entry 12); entry 16 explains why expectations matter.

Check yourself

13.1 Workers learn that big layoffs are coming next year. What happens to consumer spending now?

Show answer

It falls at every level of current income — an autonomous fall that starts a multiplier downward.

13.2 Your current income rises. Is that a shift in your consumer spending?

Show answer

No. It is a movement along your consumption function.

14

Planned investment spending and what moves it

★ Exam
Definition

The investment firms intend to undertake. It falls when the interest rate rises, rises when firms expect faster growth in real GDP, and falls when they already have spare production capacity.

Textbook pages 166–168, margin.

Interest rate: a firm goes ahead with a project only if its expected return beats the cost of the funds. A higher rate means fewer projects pass. That holds even if the firm uses its own past profits, because it could have lent them out and earned interest — an opportunity cost.

Expected sales and capacity: firms invest to meet future sales. Expecting fast growth means more investment; idle machines mean less. Swings in investment are far bigger than swings in consumer spending, and most recessions start with a fall in investment — which is why investment is watched as a leading indicator of where the economy is heading.

Watch out. Using retained earnings does not make a project free. The interest the firm gives up is the cost.

Textbook

A $150,000 mortgage. Monthly payments are $1,048 at 7.5% interest and $851 at 5.5% — $197 less, which helped set off the housing boom of 2003.

Numeric

A new machine. It is expected to return 6% a year. At an interest rate of 5% the firm buys it; at 7% it does not — even if it has the cash.

Illustrative

A car maker with idle lines. Sales are expected to grow, but a third of its factories stand idle. It will not build new ones until the spare capacity is used up.

How it connects. A change in it is an autonomous change (entry 4). The difference between planned and actual investment is entry 15.

Check yourself

14.1 The interest rate rises from 4% to 8%. What happens to planned investment, and why?

Show answer

It falls: fewer projects earn more than the cost of funds.

14.2 Firms expect real GDP to grow much faster next year. What happens to planned investment?

Show answer

It rises, to build capacity for higher sales.

15

Inventories and unplanned investment

○ Background
Definition

Actual investment = planned investment + unplanned inventory investment. Goods made but not sold go into inventories, and that counts as investment.

Textbook pages 168–170, margin and equation 16-10.

Firms decide output before they know sales. If sales fall short, unsold goods pile up: positive unplanned inventory investment. If sales beat expectations, shelves empty: negative unplanned inventory investment.

Rising inventories usually signal a slowing economy, and firms respond by cutting output. Falling inventories signal growth.

Watch out. Background here. Unplanned inventory investment happens to firms; planned investment is what they choose.

Textbook

Cars. The industry makes 800,000 cars a month expecting to sell them all, but sells 700,000: 100,000 cars of unplanned inventory investment.

Numeric

Strong sales. Planned investment $50 billion; sales beat forecasts and inventories fall by $5 billion. Actual investment = 50 − 5 = $45 billion.

Illustrative

A supermarket. The manager stocks for normal sales. A heatwave empties the drinks aisle: negative unplanned inventory investment.

How it connects. It is the gap between what firms plan (entry 14) and what they end up investing.

Check yourself

15.1 Sales are lower than firms expected. Is unplanned inventory investment positive or negative?

Show answer

Positive: unsold goods pile up.

15.2 Planned investment is $80 billion and unplanned inventory investment is $6 billion. What is actual investment?

Show answer

80 + 6 = $86 billion.

16

Why current income is not the whole story

○ Background
Definition

The permanent income hypothesis: spending depends mainly on the income people expect over the long term. The life-cycle hypothesis: people plan spending over their lifetime, saving in peak years and living off wealth later.

Textbook pages 165–166.

Both explain why expected future income and wealth shift spending. People smooth their spending: they do not raise it one-for-one with a good year or cut it with a bad one.

You will not be asked to name either hypothesis. Use them to understand entry 13.

Watch out. Background only. Your exam asks what shifts spending, not which theory explains it.

Textbook

Milton Friedman, 1956. People with an unusually good year save most of the windfall; people with an unusually bad year save little or borrow.

Numeric

Two workers. Both earn $40,000 this year. One expects $40,000 next year, the other $60,000. The second spends more today.

Illustrative

Retirement. A couple in their fifties saves hard; in their seventies they spend more than their income, drawing on what they saved.

How it connects. It explains why expectations and wealth (entry 13) shift the consumption function (entry 12).

Check yourself

16.1 A worker gets a one-time $5,000 bonus. Why might spending rise only a little?

Show answer

Because spending depends mainly on long-term income, so most of a one-time windfall is saved.

16.2 When in life does the life-cycle hypothesis say people save most?

Show answer

In their peak earning years, typically their 40s and 50s.

Current production capacity — spare capacity means firms can meet sales without new investment, so planned investment falls.

MPC = change in consumer spending ÷ change in disposable income. MPC + MPS = 1. Expenditure multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) = 1 ÷ MPS. Tax multiplier = −MPC ÷ (1 − MPC). Change in real GDP = multiplier × the change, at most. Working backwards: needed change = target ÷ multiplier. The last three columns are the largest possible changes in real GDP.

The five things to get right from Module 16

  1. MPC + MPS = 1, and both are about the extra dollar of disposable income.
  2. Expenditure multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC). Divide by the MPS, not by the MPC.
  3. Tax multiplier = −MPC ÷ (1 − MPC) — one smaller in size, because part of a tax change is saved before it is spent.
  4. Equal changes in spending and taxes move real GDP by that amount, whatever the MPC.
  5. The multiplier gives the shift in aggregate demand — the most real GDP can change. To size a policy, divide the target by the multiplier.
