Teaching Notes — Krugman Module 3: The Production Possibilities Curve Model

Teacher's reference. Krugman Economics for AP, Module 3, book pp. 16–22, mapped to the AP Macroeconomics framework. Student version: student_module_03.html. Test: test_module_03.md.

Read this first if you have taught the Micro version of this module. The correction you made there is reversed here. In AP Microeconomics, Krugman's unemployment-and-recession framing of points inside the curve was off-syllabus and had to be retrained to underutilised resources. In AP Macroeconomics it is not merely allowed — it is the framework's own definition of what the curve means. See section 5.

The Enduring Understanding is the whole reframing, quoted verbatim (CED p. 35):

MOD-1 — The production possibilities curve (PPC) model is used to demonstrate the full employment level of output and to illustrate changes in full employment.

Compare the Micro framing of the identical five essential-knowledge statements, which sit under MKT-1 there: "Most resources are scarce, and in most cases the use of resources involves constraints and trade-offs." Same diagram, same five EKs, a different reason for teaching it. In Micro the PPC is a model of allocation. In Macro it is a model of capacity and employment — which is why it reappears in Unit 3 as the vertical long-run aggregate supply curve and in Unit 5 as the thing economic growth moves.

Krugman's own text states the Macro reading explicitly, on p. 18:

"The production possibilities curve shows the amount that can possibly be produced if all resources are fully employed. In other words, changes in unemployment move the economy closer to, or further away from, the production possibilities curve (PPC). But the curve itself is determined by what would be possible if there were full employment in the economy."

That paragraph is the single most useful thing in the module for this course. Do not skip it.

The EK statements, in the CED's own words

  • MOD-1.B.1 — The PPC is a model used to show the tradeoffs associated with allocating resources.
  • MOD-1.B.2 — The PPC can be used to illustrate the concepts of scarcity, opportunity cost,

efficiency, underutilized resources, and economic growth or contraction.

  • MOD-1.B.3 — The shape of the PPC depends on whether opportunity costs are constant,

increasing, or decreasing.

  • MOD-1.B.4 — The PPC can shift because of changes in factors of production as well as changes

in productivity/technology.

  • MOD-1.B.5 — Economic growth results in an outward shift of the PPC.

The learning objective MOD-1.B has three verbs, and part (c) is the one that gets skipped:

a. Define (using graphs as appropriate) the PPC and related terms. b. Explain (using graphs as appropriate) how the PPC illustrates opportunity costs, tradeoffs, inefficiency, efficiency, and economic growth or contraction under various conditions. c. Calculate (using data from PPCs or tables as appropriate) opportunity cost.

3. Required elements this module does not contain

Two of the five essential knowledge statements are not fully served by the text, and both gaps are verified by full-text search over all 927 pages.

MOD-1.B.3 — decreasing opportunity cost. The phrase "decreasing opportunity cost" appears 0 times in the book. No bowed-inward curve appears in the module, in its figures, or in its AP Review. Krugman argues on p. 20 that increasing costs are the realistic case and stops there. The framework requires all three shapes. Supply the third.

MOD-1.B.2 — contraction. The word "contraction" appears 0 times in Module 3 (44 sheets book-wide, all of them later). The idea gets exactly one sentence on p. 21 — "If an economy's PPC shifts inward, the economy has become smaller" — and no figure, against a full section plus Figure 3.3 for growth. MOD-1.B.2 names growth or contraction in the same breath. This gap matters more in Macro than it did in Micro, because Unit 5 uses inward shifts for real.

One thing to mark down rather than up. Krugman's efficient in allocation (p. 18) is good economics and is not in the AP Macroeconomics framework at all — it belongs to Micro Topic 3.7. In the Micro version of these notes it was a useful preview. Here it is background. Teach the idea if you have the minutes; do not assess it.

5. Corrections to make explicitly in class

The Micro correction is reversed here

If you or your students have come from AP Microeconomics, the instruction there was: a point inside the curve means underutilised resources, never unemployment, and never recession. That instruction was right — for that exam.

In AP Macroeconomics:

  • EU MOD-1 defines the curve by the full employment level of output.
  • MOD-1.B.2 still says underutilized resources, so that phrase remains correct.
  • But unemployment is Topic 2.3 and recessions are Topic 2.7. Both are examinable content in

this course, and Krugman's p. 18 passage is the framework's own reasoning.

Say this out loud, in one sentence: "Inside the curve means resources are idle. In this course we are allowed to call that unemployment, and the curve itself shows what the economy could produce at full employment."

Still worth correcting

"Bowed outward" is not "shifted outward." Bowed is a shape — the pattern of opportunity costs. Shifted is a movement — the economy's capacity changed. Both phrases contain the word outward and they are unrelated. This is the most common wording error in the module.

The slope is the opportunity cost, and it needs units. Krugman states it once, almost in passing (p. 19): the slope of a straight-line PPC equals the opportunity cost of the horizontal good in terms of the vertical good. Do not let it pass as an aside, and insist on "one fish costs ¾ of a coconut" rather than a bare 0.75. Always compute the reciprocal too.

A point outside is not impossible forever. It is unattainable now. Growth is precisely the event that makes it attainable — a connection the module makes and students miss.

Teach the three shapes together, in one sitting. Constant, increasing and decreasing are one comparison, not three lessons, and the whole skill reduces to a single question: moving right along the curve, does it get steeper, stay straight, or get flatter?

Teach as a pair, never singly. Each of these is defined by its contrast:

  • Inside / outside the curve — wasteful but possible against impossible; the most-missed pair
  • Bowed outward / shifted outward — a shape against a movement
  • Growth / contraction — the same mechanism in opposite directions
  • Movement along / shift of — a choice about the mix against a change in capacity
  • Efficient in production / efficient in allocation — the second is background in this course
