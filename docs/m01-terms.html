1. Economics — Core

Definition. "Economics is the study of scarcity and choice." (Krugman, p. 2)

CED anchor. No statement defines the discipline. This is the framing for MOD-1.A.1 — "Individuals and societies are forced to make choices because most resources are scarce." (Topic 1.1, CED p. 34)

Where taught. p. 2.

Explanation

Five words containing the subject. There is never enough of what people want, so somebody must choose, and every choice gives something up. Everything in the following forty modules is a version of that sentence.

Krugman draws the boundary sharply: "you might say that it isn't economics if it isn't about choice." If a question contains no trade-off it is not an economic question, however much money is involved.

Scale is not part of the definition, which is why the same reasoning covers one person on an island and a national economy. In this course the scale is always the second one, and Module 2 starts there.

Common error. "Economics is the study of money." Money is one scarce thing among many, and the scarcest resource in this module is time, which carries no price at all.

Examples

1. From the text. A Walmart or Target holds thousands of products and nobody could afford all of them. Even with unlimited money there is limited room at home. Both constraints bind, so the shopper chooses what to leave on the shelf. (p. 2)

2. Numeric. A government allocates a fixed $500 billion between health and transport. Every billion to one is a billion not available to the other. Wanting both does not remove the constraint; it only decides which side of it you take.

3. Illustrative. A coffee shop with one espresso machine and two staff in the morning rush. Making one complicated drink properly means three simpler customers wait. The binding resource is machine-minutes, not money.

Figure. None. The idea is prior to any diagram.

2. Scarcity — Core

Definition. "A resource is scarce when there is not enough of it available to satisfy the various ways a society wants to use it." (Krugman, p. 3)

CED anchor. MOD-1.A.1 — "Individuals and societies are forced to make choices because most resources are scarce." (Topic 1.1, CED p. 34). Learning objective MOD-1.A(a): "Define scarcity."

Where taught. pp. 3–4.

Explanation

Scarcity is about competing uses. It does not mean rare and it does not mean running out. A resource is scarce when using it one way means it cannot be used another way.

Two consequences surprise students. Something can be plentiful and still scarce — there is a great deal of fresh water on Earth and it is very scarce in Arizona. And something can be rare without being scarce, if nobody wants it.

The most scarce resource in this module is time, and Krugman makes the point deliberately: money is not the only limit, because "there are only 24 hours in a day." Time never sends an invoice, which is exactly why students forget to count it in entry 5.

Note the framework's word: most. MOD-1.A.1 says most resources are scarce, not all. The Micro framework spells the exception out — established knowledge is non-rival, so it is not scarce — and the Macro framework compresses it into one word. Krugman never makes the point at all. Say it explicitly; it is the half-sentence a non-rival-resource question turns on.

Common error. Writing "scarce" when the intended word is "expensive" or "nearly gone". A high price is a result of scarcity in a market, not the same thing as scarcity.

Examples

1. From the text. Convenience stores charge more than supermarkets and stay in business. Some customers would rather pay extra than spend the time driving further and queueing longer — trading money for time, which only makes sense if time is scarce. (p. 3)

2. Numeric. A student has 4 hours before an exam and 6 hours of revision they would like to do. The 4 hours are scarce: every hour on one subject is an hour not on another. Had they 10 hours free, the constraint would not bind and there would be no economic problem.

3. Illustrative — the exception. The Pythagorean theorem. Using it does not use it up, and everyone can use it simultaneously. Economists call this non-rival. Land, labour and capital are scarce; ideas need not be, which is why MOD-1.A.1 says most.

Figure. The PPC in Module 3 is the standard picture of scarcity — every point beyond the curve is unattainable precisely because resources are scarce.

3. Resources (factors of production) — Core

Definition. "A resource is anything that can be used to produce something else." The economy's resources, "sometimes called factors of production", fall into four categories: land (including timber, water, minerals, and all other resources that come from nature), labor (the effort of workers), capital (machinery, buildings and other manufactured goods used to make other goods and services), and entrepreneurship (risk taking, innovation, and the organisation of resources for production). (Krugman, p. 3)

CED anchor. Learning objective MOD-1.A(b) — "Define… economic resources." (Topic 1.1, CED p. 34). Factors of production also appear in MOD-1.B.4: "The PPC can shift because of changes in factors of production as well as changes in productivity/technology." (Topic 1.2, CED p. 35)

Where taught. p. 3.

Explanation

Resources are inputs, not outputs. A cheeseburger is not a resource; the cook's labour, the grill and the land the restaurant sits on are.

Capital is the category students get wrong, because in ordinary English "capital" means money. In economics capital is manufactured goods used to make other goods — machines, buildings, tools. Money is not capital; money is what you use to buy capital.

The Macro framework never lists the four categories. MOD-1.A(b) requires students to define economic resources and stops there. Entrepreneurship appears 0 times in the Macro CED — against 2 in the Micro CED, where it also fails to make MKT-1.A.2's list. Teach the four-way division because it is standard and every prep book uses it, and do not let a student believe a fourth item scores a mark.

Common error. Calling money capital. A firm that says "we need capital to expand" usually means money; the capital is the machinery the money would buy.

Examples

1. From the text. Krugman's own classification exercise (AP Review, CYU 2): time spent flipping hamburgers is labour, a bulldozer is capital, a river is land. (p. 8)

2. Numeric. A bakery employs 6 staff, owns 2 ovens and rents 200 m² of floor space. Labour 6, capital 2, land 200 m². The recipe it uses is not a scarce resource at all — see entry 2.

3. Illustrative. A delivery van is capital; a farmer's field is land; an accountant's afternoon is labour; deciding to launch a new product line is entrepreneurship.

Figure. Module 3's PPC shifts outward when the quantity or quality of these inputs rises — which is MOD-1.B.4, and the reason this entry is worth teaching properly even though Topic 1.1 barely mentions it.

4. Individual choice — Core

Definition. "Individual choice is decisions by individuals about what to do, which necessarily involve decisions about what not to do." (Krugman, p. 2)

CED anchor. MOD-1.A.1 — "Individuals and societies are forced to make choices because most resources are scarce." (Topic 1.1, CED p. 34)

Where taught. p. 2.

Explanation

The definition contains its own second half: choosing to do one thing is choosing not to do the others. That is not word-play — it is what makes every choice carry a cost, and entry 5 is the name of that cost.

MOD-1.A.1 covers both halves of the module's opening: individuals and societies are forced to choose. Krugman makes the same move on p. 3, moving from one person's decision to a society's, and in this course the second half is the one that matters. Every macroeconomic policy question is a society choosing.

Common error. Believing that not deciding avoids the choice. Declining to act is a choice with the same forgone alternatives; delay does not remove the decision, it makes it by default.

Examples

1. From the text. Krugman's framing: an individual choice is "what to do, which necessarily involves decisions about what not to do". The second clause is the content. (p. 2)

2. Numeric. A student with 4 free hours signs up for a 3-hour club. The choice is not just "join"; it is simultaneously "do not spend those 3 hours on the alternatives" — a job shift, revision, rest.

3. Illustrative — society choosing. A government with a fixed budget that funds a new hospital has chosen not to fund the roads that money would have built. MOD-1.A.1 names exactly this case.

Figure. None. Module 3's PPC turns it into a picture.

5. Opportunity cost — Core

Definition. "The real cost of an item is its opportunity cost: what you must give up in order to get it." (Krugman, p. 4)

CED anchor. MOD-1.B.2 — "The PPC can be used to illustrate the concepts of scarcity, opportunity cost, efficiency, underutilized resources, and economic growth or contraction." Learning objective MOD-1.B(c) — "Calculate (using data from PPCs or tables as appropriate) opportunity cost." (Topic 1.2, CED p. 35)

Where taught. pp. 4–5. Calculated from a graph in Module 3 (p. 19) and from a table in Module 4.

Explanation

Opportunity cost is the single best alternative forgone, not the sum of everything forgone. A student choosing between three uses of an afternoon gives up two of them and the opportunity cost is the better of those two, not both added together.

It has two components and students reliably count only one. Explicit cost is money actually paid. Implicit cost is the value of what the time or the asset would otherwise have produced. Krugman's college example is built on this: tuition is explicit, forgone earnings are implicit, and the second is usually the larger.

This is the only concept in Module 1 the Macro framework asks students to calculate, which is why it is worth more class time than anything else here. The calculation arrives properly in Module 3 with the PPC; the reasoning has to be in place first.

Common error. Counting only money. A "free" event is not free if attending costs three hours that would otherwise have earned or produced something. Conversely, a cost that would be paid whichever option is chosen is not part of the opportunity cost of either.

Examples

1. From the text. The decision to attend college. Tuition and books are explicit; the salary forgone by not working for four years is implicit, and for most students it is the bigger number. Krugman's LeBron James case is the extreme version — the forgone earnings were so large that college was not worth it. (pp. 4–5)

2. Numeric. A free concert ticket, three hours long, and the alternative is a shift paying $20 an hour. Explicit cost $0, implicit cost 3 × $20 = $60. The opportunity cost of the "free" ticket is $60.

3. Illustrative — the AP Review item. Moving from a $45,000 job to a $50,000 job: the $45,000 old salary is part of the opportunity cost, the extra commuting time is, and the more spacious office is not — it is a benefit of the new job, not something given up. (p. 8)

Figure. The PPC in Module 3: the slope of the curve is the opportunity cost, which is what MOD-1.B(c) asks students to calculate.

6. Marginal benefit and marginal cost — Context

Definition. Krugman gives these in running text rather than the margin (p. 3): the marginal benefit is the gain from doing something one more time; the marginal cost is what that one additional time costs.

CED anchor. None. Marginal benefit and marginal cost have no essential knowledge statement in the AP Macroeconomics framework. The word marginal appears 12 times in the Macro CED and every occurrence is the marginal propensity to consume or save (MOD-2.B.2, Unit 3), against 186 occurrences in the Micro CED, where marginal analysis is core content.

Where taught. p. 3.

Explanation

"Marginal" means one more. Marginal benefit is the extra gain from one more unit; marginal cost the extra cost. Neither is the total, and neither is the average — the distinction students lose most often.

This is genuine economics and it is not assessed in this course. Teach it as a reasoning tool, spend five minutes, and do not build a test on it.

Then flag the vocabulary. In Unit 3 students meet the marginal propensity to consume: the change in consumer spending divided by the change in disposable income. Same word, much narrower job. A student who has drilled MB and MC in AP Micro will read "marginal" and reach for the wrong idea.

Common error. Including costs that do not change when one more unit is produced. A theatre's building is already paid for and does not vary with one extra ticket sold, so it is no part of the marginal cost of that ticket.

Examples

1. From the text. Krugman introduces marginal analysis as "the study of the costs and benefits of doing a little bit more of an activity versus a little bit less". (p. 3)

2. Numeric. Three slices of pizza in, the fourth gives less extra satisfaction than the third — marginal benefit falls as quantity rises. If the fourth slice costs 45 and is worth 40, it reduces net benefit even though pizza in general is a good thing.

3. Illustrative — the Macro trap. In Unit 3, a marginal propensity to consume of 0.8 means that of one extra dollar of disposable income, 80 cents is spent. That is "marginal" in the same one-more-unit sense, applied to one specific ratio — and it is the only sense this course examines.

Figure. None here. The MB = MC intersection belongs to the Micro course.

7. Marginal analysis — Context

Definition. "The study of the costs and benefits of doing a little bit more of an activity versus a little bit less." (Krugman, p. 3)

CED anchor. None. See entry 6.

Where taught. p. 3.

Explanation

The decision rule: keep going while marginal benefit exceeds marginal cost, stop when it no longer does. The optimum sits where MB = MC.

The reason it matters even unassessed is that it is how economists think about any quantity decision, and students who own it find Unit 3's multiplier arithmetic easier to accept. But no Macro question will ask for it, and a test item built on MB = MC is testing the wrong course.

Common error. "Do it while the benefit is positive." The first unit almost always has a positive benefit, so that rule never stops. The comparison is against the cost of the same unit.

Examples

1. From the text. Krugman's framing of the method as comparing "a little bit more" with "a little bit less" — the comparison is always at the margin, never of the totals. (p. 3)

2. Numeric. Marginal benefit 40, marginal cost 45. Do not do that unit: it reduces net benefit by 5, even though the activity as a whole may be worthwhile.

3. Illustrative. A school deciding how many extra revision sessions to run. The first adds a lot; the eighth adds little and still costs a teacher's evening. The right number is where the two meet.

Figure. None.

8. Economy — Context

Definition. "An economy is a system for coordinating a society's productive and consumptive activities." (Krugman, p. 2)

CED anchor. None. No Macro essential knowledge statement defines an economy or names the three basic questions of resource allocation. In the Micro framework this is MKT-1.B.2, Topic 1.2.

Where taught. p. 2.

Explanation

Note what the word is not. Economics is the study; an economy is the thing studied. Japan has an economy; a student studies economics. The quickest test is the article — "a market economy" and "Japan's economy" both take one, "economics" never does.

An economy answers three questions: what to produce, how to produce it, and for whom — who consumes what is produced. In AP Microeconomics that trio is examinable (MKT-1.B.1). Here it is vocabulary.

Common error. Using economics and economy interchangeably in written answers. It reads as imprecision even when the substance is right.

Examples

1. From the text. Krugman's definition frames an economy as a coordination system — the emphasis is on coordinating, not on the goods themselves. (p. 2)

2. Numeric. The U.S. economy coordinated roughly $20 trillion of production in a recent year across millions of independent decisions, with no one directing it. That coordination is the thing the word names.

3. Illustrative. A household is arguably a small economy: it decides what gets cooked, who does it, and who eats. It is also too small to be a society and depends on the wider economy for almost everything it uses — which is why the analogy helps and should not be pressed.

Figure. The circular-flow diagram in Module 10 is the formal picture, and it is examinable (Topic 2.1).

9. Market economy — Context

Definition. "In a market economy, the decisions of individual producers and consumers largely determine what, how, and for whom to produce, with little government involvement in the decisions." (Krugman, p. 2)

CED anchor. None. The phrase market economy appears 0 times in the AP Macroeconomics framework, against 3 in the Micro framework.

Where taught. p. 2.

Explanation

Nobody is in charge, and that is the point. Coordination happens through prices: a high price signals that something is wanted and rewards whoever supplies it, so producers move toward it without instruction.

Krugman's pollution example on p. 3 is the memorable version — an appeal to reduce pollution voluntarily is unlikely to work; a financial incentive is more likely to succeed, because it changes what people gain.

Common error. Reading "market economy" as "no government". The definition says little government involvement in the decisions about what, how and for whom. Taxes and regulation do not disqualify an economy from the label.

Examples

1. From the text. High prices and profits lead producers to make more of the most-needed goods, because whoever produces them keeps the profits. (p. 2)

2. Numeric. A shortage pushes a price from $2 to $5. No authority ordered the change and no authority ordered the extra production that follows it; the $3 is the entire message.

3. Illustrative. Deciding how a bakery produces bread: the owner chooses the cheapest method that works, because whatever is saved is kept. Nobody had to tell them to look.

Figure. None in this module.

10. Command economy — Context

Definition. "In a command economy, industry is publicly owned and a central authority makes production and consumption decisions." (Krugman, p. 2)

CED anchor. None. The phrase appears 0 times in the Macro framework, against 2 in Micro.

Where taught. pp. 2–3.

Explanation

Two conditions, and both are required: public ownership and central decision-making. Either alone is not a command economy.

Krugman's account of why they work badly is an incentive argument, which is the useful part: producers in the Soviet Union "had little incentive to produce what consumers wanted", so shortages persisted and queues formed. Nobody gained anything by noticing a shortage and fixing it.

Common error. Treating any state involvement as command. A country with public healthcare and private industry is not a command economy; industry is not publicly owned and firms still decide what to make.

Examples

1. From the text. The Soviet Union between 1917 and 1991 — command economies "are famous for long lines at shops". (p. 2)

2. Numeric. A central plan sets shoe output at 10 million pairs. Demand turns out to be 14 million. In a market the price rises and output follows; under the plan the gap persists until the next plan is written.

3. Illustrative. A school canteen where a single administrator fixes the menu, the quantities and the price for the year. Queues and waste both follow from the same cause: no signal, and no one who gains by responding to one.

Figure. None.

11. Mixed economy — Context

Definition. Not in Krugman. An economy in which both markets and government make resource allocation decisions — what every real economy is.

CED anchor. None. Mixed economy appears 0 times in the AP Macroeconomics framework.

Where taught. Nowhere. The phrase does not occur in Krugman either.

This entry reverses the Microeconomics finding

In AP Microeconomics this is the starred entry of Module 1: MKT-1.B.2 (Micro CED p. 35) explicitly names "command economy, market economy, or mixed economy", Krugman never supplies the third, and the Micro documents tell you to write the definition from scratch because it is examinable.

In AP Macroeconomics none of the three is in the framework at all. The term is still worth a sentence, because pure market and pure command economies are limiting cases and every economy this course models is a mixed one. But it is not a gap to be filled, and it must not appear on a Macro test. If you teach both courses, this is the single easiest mistake to carry across.

Explanation

Pure market and pure command economies are analytical extremes. Every real economy sits between them, which is why comparing a country to a pure market is informative: the interesting part is which decisions are not being left to the market.

For this course the practical relevance is Units 3 to 5. Every fiscal and monetary policy question assumes a government acting inside a market economy — which is what a mixed economy is. The idea is load-bearing; the term is not examined.

Common error. Treating "mixed" as a fourth kind of economy alongside the other two. It is the region between them, and effectively everything real is in it.

Examples

1. From the framework's silence. No Macro statement mentions any economic system by name — yet every policy topic from Unit 3 onward presumes a government operating within a market. The concept is assumed rather than taught.

2. Numeric. Government spending is roughly 35–45% of GDP across most developed economies. Neither extreme describes that; the label for it is mixed.

3. Illustrative. A country where firms set prices and choose products, while the state runs schools, roads and courts and taxes to pay for them. Markets make most decisions, government makes some.

Figure. None.

12. Incentives — Preview

Definition. "Incentives are rewards or punishments that motivate particular choices." (Krugman, p. 2)

CED anchor. POL-4.A.3 — "Supply-side fiscal policies affect aggregate demand, aggregate supply, and potential output in the short run and long run by influencing incentives that affect household and business economic behavior." (Topic 5.7 Public Policy and Economic Growth, CED p. 100). This is the only occurrence of the word in the Macro framework; no statement defines incentives in general.

Where taught. pp. 2–3.

Explanation

An incentive changes what someone gains from an action, which is why it changes behaviour where information and appeals do not. Krugman's example is exact: an appeal to reduce pollution voluntarily probably will not work, while a financial incentive is more likely to succeed.

In this course the idea surfaces once, in Unit 5, and in one direction: supply-side fiscal policy — tax changes, deregulation — is said to work by altering the incentives households and firms face. A student who has met incentives in Module 1 will find that sentence obvious rather than mysterious, which is the whole reason to teach it here.

Common error. Confusing an incentive with information. Telling people that recycling helps changes what they know; charging for landfill waste changes what they gain. Only the second is an incentive.

Examples

1. From the text. Voluntary appeals to cut pollution against a financial incentive to cut it. Economists are "skeptical of any attempt to change behaviour that doesn't change their incentives". (p. 3)

2. Numeric — the Unit 5 version. A cut in the marginal tax rate on wages from 40% to 30% raises the take-home value of an extra hour worked from 60 cents to 70 cents in the dollar. POL-4.A.3 is the claim that this change in payoff changes behaviour, and thereby potential output.

3. Illustrative. A class that gets a free period when the room is left tidy will tidy the room. A poster asking them to tidy the room changes information, not payoff.

Figure. None here; in Unit 5 the effect is drawn as a shift in long-run aggregate supply.

13. Property rights — Context

Definition. "Property rights… establish ownership and grant individuals the right to trade goods and services with each other." (Krugman, p. 3)

CED anchor. None. Property rights appears 0 times in the AP Macroeconomics framework, against 8 in the Micro framework, where MKT-3.A.1 (Micro CED p. 45) makes it a requirement: "A well-defined system of property rights is necessary for the market system to function well."

Where taught. p. 3.

Explanation

Ownership decides who bears the consequences of a decision, and that is what makes incentives work. An owner who will sell the timber later loses money by clear-cutting now, so they replant. Nobody owns the litter in a public park, so nobody bears the cost of the mess.

The Micro framework makes this a Unit 2 requirement. The Macro framework never mentions it. Teach it in a sentence alongside incentives — it explains why incentives exist — and do not assess it.

Common error. Reading property rights as being only about physical possession. The right to trade is half the definition, and it is the half that makes a market possible.

Examples

1. From the text. Krugman pairs property rights with incentives as the two things that "create many of the incentives" in a market economy. (p. 3)

2. Numeric. A forest owner who can sell timber in 20 years harvests gradually and replants. An open-access forest with no owner is cut to nothing, because whoever leaves a tree standing gains nothing by it.

3. Illustrative. Litter in a public park against litter in your own garden. The mess is the same; who pays for it is not.

Figure. None.

14. Microeconomics and macroeconomics — Context

Definition. Microeconomics is "the study of how people make decisions and how those decisions interact". Macroeconomics is "concerned with the overall ups and downs in the economy". (Krugman, p. 5)

CED anchor. None in either framework. No essential knowledge statement defines the two branches.

Where taught. p. 5, with Table 1.1.

Explanation

No anchor, and the most useful ten minutes in the module for this course — because it says what the next forty modules are about.

Macroeconomics is not "economics but bigger". It asks different questions and its answers sometimes contradict the micro intuition. Table 1.1 (p. 5) sets the pairs out directly: how many people are employed in this firm against how many are employed in the economy; whether this price rose against whether the overall price level rose.

The AP Review's MCQ 5 is the same idea as an exam item, and its three wrong answers are macro questions — government employment policy, the economy's employment level, the overall price level. Used as an opener it tells a class exactly what it has signed up for. The Microeconomics version of these notes rejects that item as off-syllabus; here it is the best orientation question available.

Common error. Assuming what is true of one household or firm is true of the whole economy. Everyone saving more sounds prudent and can reduce total spending, income and saving. Unit 3 is largely about cases like this.

Examples

1. From the text. Table 1.1 pairs a micro question with its macro counterpart, line by line. (p. 5)

2. Numeric — the AP Review item. MCQ 5 asks which question is studied in microeconomics. "Should I go to college or get a job?" is the micro one; employment policy, total employment and the overall price level are all macro. (p. 8)

3. Illustrative. One person standing up at a concert sees better. Everyone standing up sees the same as before and is less comfortable. What works individually need not work in aggregate.

Figure. Table 1.1 (p. 5).

15. Economic aggregates — Context

Definition. "Economic measures that summarize data across many different markets." (Krugman, p. 5)

CED anchor. None as a defined term. The idea is everywhere in Unit 2 under other names — aggregate output (MEA-2.A.1), the price level, the unemployment rate — and aggregate is one of the most frequent words in the Macro framework.

Where taught. p. 5.

Explanation

An aggregate is many markets added into one number. Krugman introduces the word here and then uses it constantly from Module 2 onward: aggregate output, aggregate demand, aggregate supply, the aggregate price level.

What makes it worth a minute now is what aggregation hides. A single unemployment rate says nothing about which industries or regions carry it. A single price level can be steady while some prices double and others halve. Every macro measurement is a summary, and every summary discards something — which is the honest framing for Unit 2's measurement topics.

Common error. Reading an aggregate as a description of a typical person. Real GDP per capita is an arithmetic average and may describe nobody, a point that returns in Module 2.

Examples

1. From the text. Krugman's definition is itself the point: measures that summarize data across many different markets. (p. 5)

2. Numeric. Two regions, unemployment 3% and 15%, equal populations. The aggregate is 9%, which describes neither region and hides the problem entirely.

3. Illustrative. A school reporting one average grade across every subject. Useful for a trend, useless for deciding which department needs help.

Figure. None here. Figure 2.1 in Module 2 is the first real aggregate plotted over time.

16. Positive and normative economics — Context

Definition. Positive economics is "the branch of economic analysis that describes the way the economy actually works". Normative economics "makes prescriptions about the way the economy should work". (Krugman, p. 6)

CED anchor. None. Normative appears 0 times in either framework.

Where taught. pp. 6–8.

Explanation

Positive statements have right answers, at least in principle; normative statements turn on values. "The price of gasoline is rising" is positive. "The price of gasoline is too high" is normative, and no amount of data settles it.

The distinction is worth ten minutes for intellectual honesty about what economists do and do not claim. It is worth no more than that in this course: no question will ask for it, and the module's own CYU 4 and MCQ 4 both test it.

Common error. Assuming a forecast is normative because it is uncertain. "Gas prices are expected to fall" is positive — a claim about what will happen, which will turn out right or wrong.

Examples

1. From the text. Krugman's own contrast between describing how the economy works and prescribing how it should. (p. 6)

2. Numeric — the AP Review item. MCQ 4 offers three statements: prices are rising (positive), prices are too high (normative), prices are expected to fall (positive). Only the middle one is normative. (p. 8)

3. Illustrative. "Raising the minimum wage reduces employment among young workers by x%" is positive and testable. "The minimum wage should be raised" is normative, and two economists who agree on the first can disagree on the second.

Figure. None.

17. Economic model — Context

Definition. "Simplified representations of reality such as graphs or equations." (Krugman, p. 6)

CED anchor. None as a defined term — but Big Idea 3 is Macroeconomic Models (MOD): "Macroeconomic models are simplified representations that depict basic economic relationships and can be used to predict and explain how those relationships are affected by economic shocks." (CED p. 17). The Enduring Understanding for this entire unit, MOD-1, is about a model.

Where taught. pp. 6–8.

Explanation

No essential knowledge statement defines a model, and models are the method of the whole course. Units 3 to 6 are almost entirely models: AD–AS, the money market, loanable funds, the Phillips curve, the foreign exchange market. Skill category 4 — Graphing and Visuals — exists to assess them, and Topic 1.2's suggested skill is 4.A, "Draw an accurately labeled graph or visual to represent an economic model or market."

A model deliberately leaves things out. That is not a flaw, it is the method. The question to ask is never "is this realistic?" but "does it keep the things that matter for the question I am asking?"

Krugman's point about disagreement is worth keeping: two economists "can legitimately disagree about which simplifications are appropriate — and therefore arrive at different conclusions." Neither has made a mistake; they modelled different things.

Common error. Rejecting a model because one assumption is unrealistic. Every assumption is unrealistic — that is what makes it an assumption. Ask whether it changes the answer.

Examples

1. From the text. "How much would revenue increase if the toll were raised from $1.00 to $1.50?" is a "what if" question, and models are especially good at those. (p. 6)

2. Numeric — the next module but one. Module 3's PPC assumes an economy producing exactly two goods. No economy does, and that assumption is what turns a trade-off into a curve with a measurable slope.

3. Illustrative — this course. The AD–AS diagram represents an entire national economy with two lines. It is absurd as a description and it is how every Unit 3 question is answered.

Figure. Every figure from Module 3 onward.
