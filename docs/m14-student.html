Inflation: Who Wins, Who Loses

Seventeen terms from Module 14 of Krugman’s Economics for AP: what inflation, deflation and disinflation are, how to turn nominal values into real ones, and who gains and who loses when inflation is not what people expected. That last idea is what your exam means by the costs of inflation.

One word carries this module: unexpected. Inflation that everyone expected is built into loans and wages, and moves nothing. Inflation that surprises people moves purchasing power from anyone owed fixed dollars to anyone who owes them.

The 17 terms

01

Inflation and the price level

★ Exam
Definition

Inflation is a rise in the overall level of prices.

Textbook pages 134–135.

Your textbook’s surprising point: the level of prices does not matter. Replace every dollar with one-seventh of a “new dollar” — prices, wages, debts, everything — and nobody is richer or poorer. Prices are lower, but so is everyone’s income.

What matters is the rate of change of prices, and above all whether it was expected. That is where the costs come from.

Watch out. “Prices are high” is not inflation. Inflation is prices rising.

Textbook

The “new dollar”. At 7 to 1, a $140,000 mortgage becomes 20,000 new dollars and a $14 wage becomes 2 new dollars an hour. The real wage is unchanged.

Numeric

Figure 14.1. The U.S. price level rose in every year from 1969 to 2009, while the inflation rate rose above 13% around 1980 and fell below zero in 2009.

Illustrative

A country’s new currency. France swapped francs for euros at 6.55957 to 1 in 2002. Every price and every wage was divided by the same number. Nobody got poorer.

How it connects. The rate of change is entry 4; dividing by the price level is entry 2; a fall in the level is entry 5.

Check yourself

1.1 All prices and all wages double overnight. Is anyone worse off?

Show answer

No. Real wages and real incomes are unchanged. (Anyone owed a fixed number of dollars is worse off — entry 15.)

1.2 What is the difference between a high price level and a high inflation rate?

Show answer

The first is where prices are; the second is how fast they are rising.

02

Real wage and real income

★ Exam
Definition

The real wage is the wage rate divided by the price level. Real income is income divided by the price level. They measure purchasing power.

Textbook page 135, margin.

A nominal wage is the number of dollars you are paid. A real wage is what those dollars buy. If your pay and every price rise by the same percentage, your nominal wage rises and your real wage does not.

This is the general rule for any real variable: take the nominal value and deflate it by the price level.

Watch out. A raise is not a real raise unless it beats inflation.

Textbook

Your AP Review, question 2. Your nominal wage doubles and prices double: your real wage does not change.

Numeric

A wage that only keeps up. $20 an hour becomes $22 while the price index goes from 100 to 110. The real wage is $20 both years.

Illustrative

A pay freeze. Your pay is frozen for a year while prices rise 3%. Your nominal wage is unchanged; your real wage has fallen about 3%.

How it connects. It deflates by the price level (entry 1). Calculating it is entry 3.

Check yourself

2.1 Your wage rises 4% and prices rise 4%. What happens to your real wage?

Show answer

Nothing. It is unchanged.

2.2 Your wage rises 2% and prices rise 5%. Did you get a real raise?

Show answer

No. Your real wage fell, by about 3%.

03

Calculating changes in real variables

★ Exam

⚑ Not in your textbook — learn it from here. Your exam asks you to calculate changes in real variables. Module 14 defines the real wage and real income but never works one out with a price index.

Definition

Real value = nominal value ÷ (price index ÷ 100). For a quick answer: % change in the real value ≈ % change in the nominal value − the inflation rate.

Not worked in your textbook. The definitions are on page 135.

Dividing by the index over 100 turns this year’s dollars into base-year dollars. Then you can compare across years honestly.

The shortcut is close when the numbers are small, and it is what most questions expect. If the question gives you index numbers, divide; if it gives you percentage changes, subtract.

Watch out. Divide by the index over 100. Dividing $22 by 110 gives 20 cents, not $20.

Textbook

Your textbook, Module 15. Income doubles from $40,000 to $80,000 while the price index goes from 100 to 200: real income is unchanged.

Numeric

An income that beats inflation. $40,000 becomes $42,000 while the index goes from 100 to 102. Real income = 42,000 ÷ 1 = $41,176.47, up 2.9%. The shortcut: 5% − 2% = 3%.

Illustrative

A wage that only keeps up. $20 becomes $22 while the index goes from 100 to 110. Real wage = 22 ÷ 1.1 = $20: no change.

How it connects. It applies entry 2, using the inflation rate (entry 4).

Check yourself

3.1 Your wage rises from $15 to $16.50 while the price index rises from 100 to 110. What happened to your real wage?

Show answer

16.50 ÷ 1.10 = $15. It is unchanged.

3.2 Nominal income rises 7% and the inflation rate is 3%. About how much did real income change?

Show answer

About +4%.

04

The inflation rate

★ Exam
Definition

The percentage change per year in a price index: (price level in year 2 − price level in year 1) ÷ price level in year 1 × 100.

Textbook page 135, margin and formula.

The price level is measured by an index such as the consumer price index (next module) or the GDP deflator (Module 11). The inflation rate is how fast that index is rising.

Any price index will do, as long as you use the same one for both years.

Watch out. Divide by the first year’s level, not the second’s.

Textbook

The U.S. in peacetime. Inflation has never been above 15% a year.

Numeric

Your exam board’s sample question. A price index rises from 100 to 125: (125 − 100) ÷ 100 × 100 = 25%.

Illustrative

Not starting from 100. An index rises from 200 to 210: 10 ÷ 200 × 100 = 5%, not 10%.

How it connects. It is the rate of change of the price level (entry 1). Below zero it is deflation (entry 5); falling, it is disinflation (entry 6). Real variables subtract it (entry 3), and so do real interest rates (entry 11).

Check yourself

4.1 A price index goes from 240 to 252. What is the inflation rate?

Show answer

12 ÷ 240 × 100 = 5%.

4.2 A price index falls from 150 to 147. What is the inflation rate?

Show answer

−3 ÷ 150 × 100 = −2% — deflation.

05

Deflation

★ Exam
Definition

A fall in the overall price level — an inflation rate below zero.

Textbook pages 134 and 139.

Deflation is rare but not harmless. Between 1929 and 1933 the U.S. price level fell by 35%. Debts were fixed in dollars, so their real value rose: farmers and homeowners owed more purchasing power than they had borrowed.

That led to bankruptcies, and lenders whose customers could not repay got into trouble too.

Watch out. Deflation is prices falling, not inflation slowing — that is entry 6.

Textbook

1929–33. The price level fell 35% as the economy plunged into the Great Depression.

Numeric

An index that falls. From 100 to 98: an inflation rate of −2%.

Illustrative

Cheaper next year. If you expect a TV to cost less next year, you wait. When many people wait, spending falls.

How it connects. It is a negative inflation rate (entry 4) and a fall in the price level (entry 1). Compare disinflation (entry 6). Its winners and losers are entry 14.

Check yourself

5.1 Inflation goes from 2% to −1%. Is that disinflation or deflation?

Show answer

Deflation: the price level is now falling.

5.2 In deflation, does a fixed-dollar debt get easier or harder to repay?

Show answer

Harder. Each dollar repaid is worth more.

06

Disinflation

★ Exam
Definition

Bringing the inflation rate down. Prices are still rising — just more slowly.

Textbook page 139, margin.

The U.S. inflation rate peaked around 1980 and fell to about 4% by the end of the decade. That was disinflation: the price level kept rising every year, but by less.

Disinflation and deflation are different things. Disinflation is the rate falling. Deflation is the level falling.

Watch out. From 8% to 3% is disinflation. From 3% to −1% is deflation.

Textbook

The 1980s. Inflation fell from its 1980 peak to about 4% by 1988.

Numeric

Two years. Inflation is 6% one year and 2% the next. Prices rose both years; the rate fell by 4 points. Disinflation.

Illustrative

Slower, not lower. A coffee that rose 30 cents last year rises only 10 cents this year. Still more expensive — just rising more slowly.

How it connects. It is a falling inflation rate (entry 4), not deflation (entry 5). What it cost the U.S. is entry 7.

Check yourself

6.1 Inflation goes from 9% to 4%. Did prices fall?

Show answer

No. They rose 4%. The rate fell: disinflation.

6.2 Name the difference between deflation and disinflation in one sentence.

Show answer

Deflation is a falling price level; disinflation is a falling inflation rate.

07

Why disinflation is costly

○ Background
Definition

Once high inflation is built into expectations, bringing it down usually takes a period of high unemployment.

Textbook page 139, Figure 14.2.

Policy makers try to stop inflation creeping above 2–3%. The reason is not that 5% is a disaster, but that getting back down from an established high rate is painful.

In 1981–82 the U.S. brought inflation down with a severe recession. You meet the reason in Unit 5, on the Phillips curve.

Watch out. Background here. The idea returns, with a graph, in Unit 5.

Textbook

1981–82. The recession that brought inflation down drove unemployment to 10.8%, its highest since the Great Depression.

Numeric

Figure 14.2. Inflation peaked above 13% in 1980; by the late 1980s it averaged about 4%.

Illustrative

Prevention. A central bank raises interest rates when inflation reaches 3%, to avoid having to fight 10% later.

How it connects. It is the price of disinflation (entry 6).

Check yourself

7.1 Why do policy makers act when inflation is only a little above 2–3%?

Show answer

Because bringing it down once it is established is much more costly.

7.2 What did the U.S. disinflation of the early 1980s cost?

Show answer

A deep recession and unemployment of 10.8%.

08

Shoe-leather costs

○ Background
Definition

The increased costs of transactions caused by inflation — the time and effort people spend avoiding holding money.

Textbook pages 136–137, margin.

When prices rise fast, cash loses value while you hold it, so people go to the bank more often and keep less money. That uses real resources: time, staff, bank branches.

At U.S. inflation rates these costs are small. In hyperinflations they are huge.

Watch out. Your exam does not name this cost. Know it as background.

Textbook

Germany, 1921–23. Employment at German banks rose from about 100,000 in 1913 to 375,000 in 1923.

Numeric

Brazil, early 1990s. The banking sector grew to 15% of GDP — more than twice the U.S. share.

Illustrative

Payday shopping. Workers paid daily rush to spend their wages before prices rise again.

How it connects. It is one of three named costs, with menu costs (entry 9) and unit-of-account costs (entry 10); it is worst in hyperinflation (entry 17).

Check yourself

8.1 Why do people hold less money when inflation is high?

Show answer

Because money loses purchasing power the longer they hold it.

8.2 Banking apps make it easier to move money. Are shoe-leather costs higher or lower?

Show answer

Lower: avoiding holding money costs less effort.

09

Menu costs

○ Background
Definition

The real costs of changing listed prices.

Textbook page 137, margin.

Every listed price — shelf labels, menus, catalogues, websites — costs something to change. Inflation forces firms to change them more often.

Small at low inflation, especially now that many prices change electronically. Large at very high inflation.

Watch out. Your exam does not name this cost. Know it as background.

Textbook

Brazil, early 1990s. Supermarket workers reportedly spent half their time replacing price stickers.

Numeric

Zimbabwe, 2008. Official estimates put inflation at 1,694,000%. Prices were quoted in U.S. dollars instead.

Illustrative

A rental brochure. A holiday company reprints its colour brochures because the prices in them are out of date.

How it connects. It is one of three named costs, with entry 8 and its extreme form is hyperinflation (entry 17).

Check yourself

9.1 Why are menu costs smaller today than fifty years ago?

Show answer

Prices can be changed electronically.

9.2 Name one menu cost a restaurant faces in high inflation.

Show answer

Reprinting its menus.

10

Unit-of-account costs

○ Background
Definition

Costs that arise because inflation makes money a less reliable unit of measurement.

Textbook pages 137–138, margin.

Contracts, budgets and taxes are all measured in dollars. When the dollar keeps changing in value, decisions made in dollars get worse.

Taxes are the clearest case: tax law counts nominal gains, so people pay tax on gains that are only inflation — phantom gains.

Watch out. Your exam does not name this cost. Know it as background.

Textbook

Land bought and sold. Bought for $100,000, sold a year later for $110,000 with 10% inflation: no real profit, but tax on a $10,000 phantom gain.

Numeric

The 1970s. With inflation high, some firms were discouraged from productive investment by taxes on phantom gains.

Illustrative

A household budget. A family cannot tell whether it is saving enough when prices change unpredictably month to month.

How it connects. It is the third named cost with entry 8. Its uncertainty is entry 16.

Check yourself

10.1 What is a phantom gain?

Show answer

A nominal gain that is only inflation, with no real gain, yet still taxed.

10.2 Why is money a worse unit of account in high inflation?

Show answer

Because a dollar is worth a different amount each time you measure with it.

11

Nominal and real interest rates

★ Exam
Definition

The nominal interest rate is the rate actually paid on a loan, unadjusted for inflation. The real interest rate is the nominal rate minus the inflation rate.

Textbook page 138, margin.

Every rate you see advertised is nominal. The real rate tells you what the lender actually gains in purchasing power.

Measured afterwards, the real rate uses the actual inflation rate. That is how you check who came out ahead.

Watch out. The real rate can be negative. Inflation above the nominal rate means the lender lost purchasing power.

Textbook

Your textbook’s example. Nominal 8%, inflation 5%: real rate = 8 − 5 = 3%.

Numeric

Your AP Review, question 2. $1,000 at 5%, with 5% inflation: real rate 0%.

Illustrative

The diagram above. Press Much higher: nominal 5%, inflation 8%, real rate −3%.

How it connects. It subtracts the inflation rate (entry 4). Loans are priced on expected inflation (entry 12), and the gap decides entry 13.

Check yourself

11.1 Nominal rate 7%, inflation 3%. Real rate?

Show answer

4%.

11.2 Nominal rate 4%, inflation 6%. Real rate, and who lost?

Show answer

−2%. The lender.

12

Expected inflation and the real interest rate

★ Exam
Definition

A loan’s nominal rate is agreed in advance, so both sides work from the inflation they expect. Nominal rate ≈ the real rate they want + the inflation they expect.

Textbook page 138.

A lender who wants a 3% real return and expects 2% inflation asks for 5%. If she expected 6% inflation she would ask 9%.

So there are two real rates: the one planned (nominal minus expected inflation) and the one that happened (nominal minus actual inflation). Everything in entry 13 is the gap between them.

Watch out. If inflation turns out exactly as expected, nobody gains or loses, however high it is.

Textbook

Your textbook, page 138. Each party has an expectation about future inflation, and so an expectation about the real interest rate.

Numeric

The diagram above. Nominal 5%, expected inflation 2%: a planned real rate of 3%.

Illustrative

High but expected. Press High, but expected: everyone expects 6%, the lender asks 9%, inflation is 6%. Real rate 3%, as planned.

How it connects. It applies entry 11 before the fact. The surprise is entry 13; the risk of not knowing is entry 16.

Check yourself

12.1 A lender wants a 4% real return and expects 3% inflation. What nominal rate does she ask?

Show answer

About 7%.

12.2 Inflation is 10% and everyone expected 10%. Who gains?

Show answer

Nobody. The nominal rate already allowed for it.

13

Unexpected inflation: borrowers gain, lenders lose

★ Exam
Definition

If inflation turns out higher than expected, borrowers gain at the expense of lenders. If it is lower than expected, lenders gain at the expense of borrowers.

Textbook page 138.

The borrower repays a fixed number of dollars. If prices have risen more than expected, those dollars buy less than either side planned: the real rate is lower, and the borrower has won.

Your exam calls this redistribution: unexpected inflation moves wealth from one group to another. It is not a loss to the economy as a whole — one side’s loss is the other’s gain.

Watch out. Always say unexpected. Expected inflation redistributes nothing.

Textbook

Mortgages, 1973–83. By 1983 a dollar bought only 45% of what it had in 1973, so people with early-1970s mortgages repaid in much cheaper dollars.

Numeric

The diagram above. Press Inflation higher than expected: the real rate falls from 3% to 0%, and about $30 of purchasing power moves to the borrower.

Illustrative

Mortgages, 1993–2003. Inflation came in lower than expected: by 2003 a dollar still bought 78% of its 1993 value, and lenders gained.

How it connects. It is the gap between entry 12 and entry 11. Run backwards it is deflation (entry 14); other groups are entry 15.

Check yourself

13.1 You borrowed at a fixed 6% expecting 2% inflation. Inflation is 5%. Did you gain or lose?

Show answer

Gain. Your real rate is 1%, not the 4% you expected.

13.2 Does unexpected inflation make the economy as a whole poorer through this channel?

Show answer

No. It redistributes: the borrower’s gain is the lender’s loss.

14

Unexpected deflation

★ Exam
Definition

A surprise fall in the price level raises the real value of debts: lenders gain, borrowers lose.

Textbook page 139.

It is entry 13 in reverse. The borrower repays fixed dollars that now buy more than when they were borrowed.

If debts become too heavy, borrowers default — and then lenders lose too. That is what happened in the early 1930s.

Watch out. Unexpected disinflation (inflation lower than expected, but positive) works the same way, just less strongly.

Textbook

1929–33. Prices fell 35%; debtors saw the real value of their debts rise sharply, and many went bankrupt.

Numeric

The diagram above. Press Deflation: the real rate is 7% instead of 3%, and about $40 moves to the lender.

Illustrative

A farmer in 1932. She borrowed when wheat sold for $1 a bushel; now it sells for 50 cents. Her debt is the same number of dollars and twice as many bushels.

How it connects. It is entry 13 reversed, caused by deflation (entry 5). People on fixed incomes gain from it — entry 15.

Check yourself

14.1 Unexpected deflation: who gains, the borrower or the lender?

Show answer

The lender.

14.2 Why can deflation end up hurting lenders too?

Show answer

Borrowers who cannot repay default.

15

Fixed incomes, savers and contracts

★ Exam

⚑ Not in your textbook — learn it from here. Your textbook names only borrowers and lenders. Your exam can ask about anyone whose income or savings are fixed in dollars — and people living on fixed incomes are the group it names most often.

Definition

Anyone paid or owed a fixed number of dollars loses from unexpected inflation and gains from unexpected deflation: people on fixed incomes, savers holding cash or fixed-rate deposits, workers on fixed-wage contracts.

Not in your textbook. The borrower–lender reasoning on page 138 is the same.

A pensioner receiving a fixed $2,000 a month is a lender in disguise: she is owed fixed dollars every month. So is a saver with cash in a drawer or a deposit at a fixed rate, and a worker whose wage is fixed for three years.

The other side gains: the firm paying the fixed wage, the bank paying the fixed deposit rate. Payments that are indexed — tied to a price index — are protected, because they rise with prices (next module).

Watch out. “Fixed income” means fixed in dollars. An indexed pension is not fixed in this sense.

Textbook

Your AP Review, question 1(b). Lanwei is reimbursed in dollars, late. When inflation is high, the payment she receives is worth less than what she spent.

Numeric

A fixed pension. $2,000 a month with 4% unexpected inflation buys about $1,920 of today’s goods a year later.

Illustrative

A three-year wage deal. Workers agree to 2% raises expecting 2% inflation. Inflation is 5%: their real wages fall and their employer gains.

How it connects. It extends entry 13 to everyone owed fixed dollars, and reverses in deflation (entry 14).

Check yourself

15.1 A retiree's pension is fixed at $1,500 a month. Inflation is unexpectedly high. Gain or lose?

Show answer

Lose. Her fixed dollars buy less.

15.2 Her pension is indexed to the price index instead. What changes?

Show answer

It rises with prices, so her real income is protected.

16

Uncertainty and long-term contracts

○ Background
Definition

Because unexpected inflation creates winners and losers, unpredictable inflation makes people reluctant to sign long-term contracts.

Textbook pages 138–139.

Nobody wants to lend for thirty years at a fixed rate if inflation might jump. In countries with high and uncertain inflation, long-term loans are rare.

That makes long-term investment harder — a real cost, not just a transfer.

Watch out. Background. The examinable point is the redistribution itself, entry 13.

Textbook

High-inflation countries. Long-term loans are rare, so people struggle to commit to long-term investments.

Numeric

A risk premium. A lender unsure whether inflation will be 2% or 8% asks for a higher nominal rate to be safe.

Illustrative

A house purchase. Without a long fixed-rate mortgage, a family has to save for years longer.

How it connects. It comes from not knowing expected inflation (entry 12), and adds to the costs of entry 10 and hyperinflation (entry 17).

Check yourself

16.1 Why are long-term loans rare where inflation is high and variable?

Show answer

Lenders cannot be sure what real rate they will earn.

16.2 Is this a transfer or a real cost?

Show answer

A real cost: some productive investment does not happen.

17

Hyperinflation

○ Background
Definition

Extremely high inflation — hundreds or thousands of percent a year.

Textbook pages 136–137 and the box on Israel, page 136.

Every cost of inflation becomes severe: people spend hours avoiding money, prices change by the day, and nobody can plan.

Your exam does not use the word. It is the extreme case of everything in entries 8, 9 and 16.

Watch out. Background only.

Textbook

Israel, 1985. Inflation reached 500%; restaurant menus listed numbers to multiply by a figure on a chalkboard that changed every day.

Numeric

Zimbabwe, May 2008. Official estimates of inflation reached 1,694,000%.

Illustrative

Germany, 1923. Merchants sent runners to the bank many times a day to swap cash for something that would hold its value.

How it connects. It magnifies shoe-leather (entry 8), menu (entry 9) and uncertainty (entry 16) costs.

Check yourself

17.1 Why do people in hyperinflation keep almost no cash?

Show answer

It loses value within hours or days.

17.2 Does your exam name hyperinflation?

Show answer

No. Know it as background.

  2. (a) Higher costs of financial transactions — one point
  3. (a) Shoe-leather cost — one point
  4. (b) A net cost — one point
  5. (b) Lanwei’s forgone work is a cost to the economy — one point

Question 2. You borrow $1,000 for one year at 5% interest to buy a couch. You did not expect any inflation, but there is unexpected inflation of 5% over the year. (a) What was the real interest rate on your loan? (b) Explain how you gained. (c) Who lost? Explain.

Show the answer

(a) Real rate = 5% − 5% = 0%.

(b) You repaid $1,050, but prices had risen 5%, so that buys only what $1,000 bought a year earlier. You had the couch for a year at no real cost.

(c) The lender. She expected a 5% real return and got 0%: the inflation she did not expect moved purchasing power from her to you.

Inflation rate = change in the price index ÷ its first value × 100. Real value = nominal ÷ (index ÷ 100). Real interest rate = nominal − inflation. Deflation = falling prices; disinflation = falling inflation. Unexpected inflation helps anyone who owes fixed dollars and hurts anyone owed them; unexpected deflation does the reverse.

The five things to get right from Module 14

  1. Inflation is the price level rising. The level does not matter; the rate — and the surprise — does.
  2. Real = nominal deflated by the price level. Divide by the index over 100, or subtract the inflation rate from the percentage change.
  3. Disinflation is not deflation. One is the rate falling, the other the level.
  4. Real interest rate = nominal − inflation. Planned with expected inflation; measured with actual.
  5. Unexpected inflation redistributes: borrowers and payers of fixed dollars gain; lenders, savers and people on fixed incomes lose. Deflation reverses it.
