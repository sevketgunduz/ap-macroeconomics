Measuring Unemployment

Fifteen terms from Module 12 of Krugman’s Economics for AP: who counts as unemployed, the two rates built from that, and what the unemployment rate misses. Two formulas, three categories of people, and one idea your textbook leaves out — what happens to the rates when people move between the categories.

The 15 terms

01

Employed

★ Exam
Definition

Currently holding a job, either full time or part time.

Textbook page 119, margin.

This is the easy one: you are employed if and only if you have a job. Hours do not matter — ten a week counts exactly as much as fifty — and nor does being away from it for a while, as long as the job is still yours.

Everything else in this module is about the people who are not employed, and the question of which of them count.

Watch out. Part-time workers are employed. Many wrong answers to calculation questions come from leaving them out of the labor force.

Textbook

Anthony, a schoolteacher. He is not working during his three-month summer break, but he has a job to go back to. Employed.

Numeric

Your AP Review’s table. 20,000 people work part time and 70,000 full time: 90,000 employed, all counted the same way.

Illustrative

A nurse on unpaid leave. Two weeks off to care for a relative, with the job held open. Still employed.

How it connects. Employed plus unemployed (entry 2) is the labor force (entry 3). Some employed people want more hours — entry 10. Moving into or out of a job is entry 8.

Check yourself

1.1 A student works eight hours a week in a shop. Employed or not?

Show answer

Employed. Any job, full or part time, counts.

1.2 Why does it matter that part-time workers count as employed?

Show answer

Because the unemployment rate cannot see people who have a part-time job but want a full-time one. They look exactly like everyone else with a job.

02

Unemployed

★ Exam
Definition

No job, actively looking for work in the past four weeks, and available to work.

Textbook page 119, margin and text.

Not working is not enough. Your textbook’s example: millions of retired people and millions of disabled people do not work, and none of them is unemployed. Retirees are not looking; the disabled are not available.

So the test has three parts, and all three must hold: jobless, looking, available. “Looking” means having searched during the past four weeks. That four-week line is what separates an unemployed person from a discouraged one (entry 9). Your exam shortens all this to “out of work”; use the three-part test when you classify a person.

Watch out. “Has no job” and “unemployed” are not the same thing. Always check for the search.

Textbook

Grace, an investment banker. Laid off and currently searching for another position. Jobless, looking, available: unemployed.

Numeric

The survey behind the number. Each month the Current Population Survey asks about 60,000 families whether they work and, if not, whether they have looked for work in the past four weeks.

Illustrative

A graduate sending applications. She finished her course in June and has applied for six jobs this month. Unemployed — even though she has never had a job.

How it connects. Unemployed plus employed (entry 1) is the labor force (entry 3). Someone who stops looking becomes a discouraged worker (entry 9); what that does to the rates is entry 8.

Check yourself

2.1 A laid-off worker stopped searching two months ago because nobody is hiring. Unemployed?

Show answer

No. They have not looked in the past four weeks, so they are not counted as unemployed — they are a discouraged worker, outside the labor force.

2.2 Name the three conditions.

Show answer

No job; actively looked for work in the past four weeks; available to work.

03

Labor force

★ Exam
Definition

The employed plus the unemployed — everyone who is working or looking for work.

Textbook page 119, margin.

The labor force is the people who are taking part in the job market: those with a job and those trying to get one. It is not the whole adult population, and it is not just the people with jobs.

Get this number right and both rates follow. It is the top of one fraction and the bottom of the other.

Watch out. The unemployed are in the labor force. A common slip is to use the number employed as the labor force.

Textbook

Your AP Review, question 1. 5 million employed and 1 million unemployed: a labor force of 6 million.

Numeric

The diagram above. 540 employed + 60 unemployed = a labor force of 600, out of 1,000 adults.

Illustrative

A household of five. A parent working full time, a parent laid off and searching, a retired grandparent, a student not looking, a teenager working ten hours a week. Three are in the labor force.

How it connects. It is entry 1 plus entry 2. Everyone else is not in the labor force (entry 4). It is the denominator of the unemployment rate (entry 6) and the numerator of the participation rate (entry 5).

Check yourself

3.1 An economy has 150 million employed and 10 million unemployed. What is the labor force?

Show answer

150 + 10 = 160 million.

3.2 Is a full-time student who is not looking for work in the labor force?

Show answer

No. They are neither working nor looking.

04

Not in the labor force

★ Exam

⚑ The name is not in your textbook — learn it from here. Your textbook describes these people (retirees, students, the disabled) but never names the group. Every classification question has three possible answers, and this is the third.

Definition

Adults who are neither working nor looking for work: retirees, full-time students who are not job-hunting, people caring for family, the disabled, and discouraged workers.

Not named in the textbook. The people are described on page 119.

Every adult is in exactly one of three groups: employed, unemployed, or not in the labor force. The third group is usually the biggest after the employed, and it is left out of the unemployment rate entirely — it is in neither the top nor the bottom of the fraction.

People move in and out of it all the time: students graduate and start looking; workers retire; job-seekers give up. Those movements are what entry 8 is about.

Watch out. Being outside the labor force says nothing about whether someone wants a job. Discouraged workers want one; they are here because they stopped looking.

Textbook

Retirees and the disabled. In December 2008 the U.S. had 32 million retired workers and 7 million disabled workers receiving benefits. None was counted as unemployed.

Numeric

The diagram above. 400 of the 1,000 adults are not in the labor force: 1,000 − 600.

Illustrative

A parent at home. A father who left his job to look after his children and is not looking for work. Not in the labor force.

How it connects. It is the adults outside the labor force (entry 3), and it is why the participation rate (entry 5) is below 100%. Discouraged workers (entry 9) are here; people moving in and out is entry 8.

Check yourself

4.1 Name the three groups every adult belongs to exactly one of.

Show answer

Employed, unemployed, and not in the labor force.

4.2 A retiree has no job. Why is she not unemployed?

Show answer

Because she is not looking for work. She is not in the labor force.

Drill: which group?

Twelve people. Put each in exactly one of the three groups. Check all three conditions for unemployed every time.

05

Labor force participation rate

★ Exam
Definition

Labor force ÷ adult population × 100. The share of adults who are working or looking.

Textbook page 119, equation 12-1.

“Adult population” means everyone aged 16 or older. The participation rate tells you how much of it is taking part in the job market at all. It falls when people retire or give up, and rises when people start looking.

It is the measure that tells you why the unemployment rate moved. If the unemployment rate fell and the participation rate fell too, people probably gave up rather than found jobs.

Watch out. The denominator is the adult population, not the labor force. The two rates have different denominators — that is the most tested fact in this module.

Textbook

Your AP Review, question 1. 6 million ÷ 12 million × 100 = 50%.

Numeric

Your AP Review’s table. 100,000 ÷ 200,000 × 100 = 50%.

Illustrative

The diagram above. 600 ÷ 1,000 = 60%. Press Retire and it falls to 58%.

How it connects. It divides the labor force (entry 3) by all adults, including those not in the labor force (entry 4). How it moves is entry 8; calculating it is entry 7.

Check yourself

5.1 Adult population 80,000; employed 46,000; unemployed 4,000. What is the participation rate?

Show answer

Labor force 50,000; 50,000 ÷ 80,000 × 100 = 62.5%.

5.2 Does an unemployed person finding a job change the participation rate?

Show answer

No. They were in the labor force before and still are.

06

Unemployment rate

★ Exam
Definition

Number unemployed ÷ labor force × 100. The share of the labor force without a job.

Textbook page 119, equation 12-2.

This is the number in the news. It is a good guide to how easy or hard it is to find a job: at 4% in 2000 employers joked that anyone who could fog a mirror could get hired; in 2009 it passed 10% in seventeen states.

The denominator is the labor force. People not in the labor force are in neither the top nor the bottom, which is why a discouraged worker leaving can lower the rate.

Watch out. Do not divide by the employed, and do not divide by the adult population. Both are classic wrong answers.

Textbook

2008–09. The rate rose from 4.8% in February 2008 to 10.1% in October 2009.

Numeric

Your AP Review’s table. Unemployed = 100,000 − 20,000 − 70,000 = 10,000; the rate is 10,000 ÷ 100,000 × 100 = 10%.

Illustrative

The diagram above. 60 ÷ 600 = 10%.

How it connects. Its denominator is the labor force (entry 3). Calculating it alongside the participation rate is entry 7; its limits are entry 12; how it moves over the business cycle is entry 14; how single movements shift it is entry 8; how it varies between groups is entry 15.

Check yourself

6.1 9.2 million employed, 0.8 million unemployed. What is the unemployment rate?

Show answer

Labor force 10 million; 0.8 ÷ 10 × 100 = 8%.

6.2 Why is the adult population no use for this calculation?

Show answer

Because the unemployment rate is a share of the labor force, not of all adults.

07

Calculating the two rates

★ Exam
Definition

Find the labor force first. Then: participation = labor force ÷ adults; unemployment = unemployed ÷ labor force.

Textbook pages 119 and 125.

Almost every calculation question is these two fractions with different numbers. The trap is always the denominator, so say it out loud before you divide: unemployed over labor force; labor force over adults.

Some questions give you the rates and ask for a number. Work backwards through the same formulas: labor force = participation rate × adults; unemployed = unemployment rate × labor force.

Watch out. If a question gives “population” and “labor force”, the population is only for the participation rate. Your exam board’s own sample question is built on exactly this trap.

Textbook

Your AP Review, question 1. Labor force 6 million; participation 6 ÷ 12 = 50%; unemployment 1 ÷ 6 = 16.7%.

Numeric

Your exam board’s sample question. Population 250,000; labor force 200,000; 175,000 employed. Unemployed = 25,000; the rate is 25,000 ÷ 200,000 = 12.5%.

Illustrative

Working backwards. 50 million adults, participation 64%, unemployment 5%. Labor force = 32 million; unemployed = 5% of 32 million = 1.6 million.

How it connects. It uses entry 5 and entry 6 together.

Check yourself

7.1 In the sample question, which wrong answer do you get by dividing 25,000 by the population?

Show answer

10%. The population is the wrong denominator for the unemployment rate.

7.2 Adults 260 million, employed 160 million, unemployed 8 million. Find both rates.

Show answer

Labor force 168 million. Participation 168 ÷ 260 = 64.6%. Unemployment 8 ÷ 168 = 4.8%.

08

How changes move the two rates

★ Exam

⚑ Not in your textbook — learn it from here. Your exam asks you to explain how changes in the labor market affect both rates. Your textbook defines the rates and never moves anyone between categories. The diagram above does it for you.

Definition

Every change is someone moving between employed, unemployed and not in the labor force. Work out which two groups, then what that does to each fraction.

Not in the textbook. The nearest is Check Your Understanding 1, page 124.

Use the diagram above: every button moves twenty people. Four results surprise people, and they are the four your exam likes.

Giving up lowers the unemployment rate. Discouraged workers leave the unemployed and the labor force. Starting to look raises it — so an improving job market can push the rate up. Retiring raises it, by shrinking the labor force. And being cut to part time changes nothing, because the worker is still employed.

Watch out. A falling unemployment rate is not automatically good news. Check the participation rate: if it fell too, people gave up.

Textbook

Job websites. Faster job-matching lowers the rate. But websites that draw discouraged workers back into the search raise it — your textbook’s own answer to Check Your Understanding 1.

Numeric

The diagram above: twenty give up. Unemployed 60 → 40, labor force 600 → 580. The rate falls from 10% to 6.9%; participation from 60% to 58%. Nobody found a job.

Illustrative

The diagram above: twenty retire. Employed 540 → 520. The same 60 unemployed are now a share of 580, so the rate rises to 10.3% with no one losing a job.

How it connects. It moves people among entries 1, 2 and 4, and reads the result off entry 5 and entry 6. Giving up is entry 9.

Check yourself

8.1 An unemployed person finds a job. What happens to each rate?

Show answer

Unemployment rate falls; participation rate unchanged.

8.2 The unemployment rate fell, and so did the participation rate. What probably happened?

Show answer

Unemployed people stopped looking and left the labor force.

09

Discouraged workers

★ Exam
Definition

People who are capable of working but have given up looking for a job because of the state of the job market.

Textbook page 120, margin.

They want work. They have stopped searching because they believe there is none to find. Having not searched in the past four weeks, they fail the test for unemployment and are counted as not in the labor force.

So the unemployment rate leaves them out. That is the first reason your exam gives for the rate understating joblessness, and it matters most in long slumps, when discouragement grows.

Watch out. A discouraged worker lowers the unemployment rate by leaving. That is a limitation of the measure, not an improvement in the economy.

Textbook

Rosa, an older worker. Laid off, gave up looking months ago. Discouraged — not counted as unemployed.

Numeric

Counting them back in. Labor force 100 million, 5 million unemployed, 2 million discouraged. Counting them: (5 + 2) ÷ (100 + 2) = 6.9%, against 5%.

Illustrative

A miner in a closed-mine town. Would take any job, stopped applying a year ago because nobody is hiring. Discouraged.

How it connects. They are former unemployed (entry 2) now outside the labor force (entry 4), and a main reason the rate understates joblessness (entry 12). They are part of a wider group, entry 11. What their leaving does to the rates is entry 8.

Check yourself

9.1 Why is a discouraged worker not unemployed?

Show answer

They have not looked for work in the past four weeks, which the definition requires.

9.2 A thousand unemployed people become discouraged. Does the unemployment rate rise or fall?

Show answer

It falls — they leave both the unemployed and the labor force.

10

Part-time workers and the underemployed

★ Exam
Definition

Part-time workers are employed. The underemployed are part-timers who want full-time work and cannot find it.

Textbook pages 119–120, margin.

Your exam says the unemployment rate “excludes” part-time workers. It does not leave them out of the count — they are in the labor force as employed. What it leaves out is their underemployment: someone working ten hours who wants forty is scored exactly like someone working forty.

So in a recession, when firms cut hours instead of jobs, a lot of lost work never reaches the unemployment rate at all.

Watch out. Underemployed means part time against your will. An engineer mowing lawns full time is employed; by your textbook’s own definition she is not underemployed, whatever the back of the book says.

Textbook

Sergio, a classically trained musician. Can only find work playing at local parties. Employed — and underemployed.

Numeric

Your AP Review’s table. The 20,000 part-timers are counted in the 90,000 employed. Leave them out and you get the unemployed wrong by 20,000.

Illustrative

The diagram above. Press Cut to part time: twenty people lose most of their hours and neither rate moves.

How it connects. They are employed (entry 1), and the second reason the rate understates joblessness (entry 12).

Check yourself

10.1 A cashier works 15 hours a week and wants 40. How is she counted?

Show answer

As employed. The unemployment rate cannot see her missing hours.

10.2 A firm cuts 500 full-time workers to half time. What happens to the unemployment rate?

Show answer

Nothing. They all still have jobs.

11

Marginally attached workers and broader measures

○ Background
Definition

People who would like a job and have looked recently, but are not looking now. They include the discouraged. Broader measures such as U6 add them, and the underemployed, back in.

Textbook pages 120–121, margin and Figure 12.2.

The government publishes several unemployment measures. The broadest, U6, counts the unemployed, the marginally attached and those working part time for economic reasons. It is always well above the headline rate.

The useful fact is in Figure 12.2: all the measures move together. So the headline rate is still a good guide to changes in the labor market, even if its level is too low.

Watch out. Your exam names discouraged workers, not “marginally attached” or “U6”. Know them as background.

Textbook

Figure 12.2. Four measures from 1994 to 2010, each higher than the last, all rising and falling together.

Numeric

A broad measure. Add 2 million discouraged workers to 5 million unemployed and a labor force of 100 million, and the rate goes from 5% to about 6.9%.

Illustrative

Someone who stopped for now. Looked for work in the spring, would like a job, stopped looking in the summer to care for a parent. Marginally attached, but not discouraged.

How it connects. The discouraged (entry 9) are part of this group.

Check yourself

11.1 Is every marginally attached worker discouraged?

Show answer

No. Discouraged workers are the ones who stopped because of the job market; others stopped for other reasons.

11.2 If the official rate rises sharply, what usually happens to U6?

Show answer

It rises too, staying well above the official rate.

12

Why the rate understates joblessness

★ Exam
Definition

The unemployment rate leaves out discouraged workers and counts the underemployed as fully employed, so it understates how many people want work and cannot get it.

Textbook page 120.

This is the limitation your exam names. Two groups: those who have given up looking (entry 9) and those working part time who want more (entry 10). Neither shows up as unemployed.

Because both groups grow in bad times, the understatement is worst exactly when the rate matters most. Two countries with the same rate can have very different amounts of joblessness.

Watch out. Understating is a limitation of the measure. It does not mean the official rate is useless — it still tracks changes well.

Textbook

Tackle the Test, question 5. The unemployment problem may be understated “due to discouraged workers” — the textbook’s own answer.

Numeric

Counting the discouraged. From 5% to 6.9% when 2 million discouraged workers join a labor force of 100 million.

Illustrative

Two countries at 6%. One has had high unemployment for years and many people have stopped looking; the other has not. Same rate, more hidden joblessness in the first.

How it connects. It comes from entry 9 and entry 10, and it is a limitation of the unemployment rate (entry 6). The opposite argument is entry 13.

Check yourself

12.1 Name the two groups your exam says the rate leaves out.

Show answer

Discouraged workers and (underemployed) part-time workers.

12.2 Why is the understatement usually worst in a long recession?

Show answer

Because that is when more people become discouraged and more are pushed into part-time work.

13

Why the rate can overstate

○ Background
Definition

Someone between jobs who is sure of finding one soon is still counted as unemployed, so the rate never reaches zero, even in a boom.

Textbook page 120.

Finding the right job takes a few weeks even when jobs are plentiful. Everyone in that search counts as unemployed. In 2000, when jobs were easy to find, the rate was still 4%.

Your exam criticises the rate only for understating. This side of the story returns in the next module as frictional unemployment, which is part of why there is a natural rate above zero.

Watch out. Background here, but the idea is examinable in Module 13 under its proper name.

Textbook

2000. The rate was 4% in a labor market so tight employers joked about the mirror test.

Numeric

Two weeks between jobs. A worker who quits on the 1st and starts a new job on the 15th is counted as unemployed if the survey reaches them on the 10th.

Illustrative

A designer with an offer. She has an offer she will accept and is doing a few more interviews. Unemployed, for now.

How it connects. It is the other side of entry 12.

Check yourself

13.1 Why does the unemployment rate never fall to zero?

Show answer

Because people are always between jobs, searching, and they count as unemployed.

13.2 Does your exam list overstating as a limitation?

Show answer

No — only understating. Overstating comes back as frictional unemployment in Module 13.

14

Unemployment over the business cycle

★ Exam
Definition

The unemployment rate rises in every recession and usually falls when the economy grows faster than average.

Textbook pages 122–123, Figures 12.1, 12.4 and 12.5.

Less output needs fewer workers. In U.S. data from 1949 to 2009, every year of falling real GDP was a year of rising unemployment, without exception.

The link is with growth, not just with recessions. Real GDP grew about 3.3% a year on average; in years above that the rate usually fell, and in years below it the rate usually rose — even with no recession, which is sometimes called a growth recession. After the 1990–91 and 2001 recessions the rate kept rising for over a year, because growth was too slow.

Watch out. “The economy is growing” does not guarantee falling unemployment. Slow growth can raise it.

Textbook

1981–82. The most severe recession in Figure 12.4 pushed unemployment to 10.8% in November 1982.

Numeric

2000. Real GDP grew 4.1%, above the 3.3% average, and the rate fell 0.2 points, from 4.2% to 4.0%.

Illustrative

A slow recovery. Output grows 1% a year after a recession ends. The unemployment rate keeps climbing, because 1% is not enough to absorb a growing labor force.

How it connects. It is read off the unemployment rate (entry 6). Why the cycle happens is Unit 3.

Check yourself

14.1 Real GDP falls by 2% this year. What happened to unemployment, most likely?

Show answer

It rose. In the U.S. data it always has.

14.2 Can unemployment rise while real GDP is growing?

Show answer

Yes, when growth is below its average rate — as after the 1990–91 and 2001 recessions.

15

Differences across groups and places

○ Background
Definition

The national rate is an average. Some groups and some regions face far higher rates.

Textbook pages 120–121, Figure 12.3, and the Rocky Mountain Low box, page 124.

Jobs are easier to find for experienced workers aged 25 to 54 and harder for the young and those near retirement. In August 2007, with the national rate low, rates for some groups were several times higher.

States differ too, and the gaps close over time because people move to where the jobs are.

Watch out. A teenager is unemployed only if looking for work. Not having a job at 16 is not enough.

Textbook

August 2007. Overall 4.7%; African-American workers 7.7%; White teenagers 14.4%; African-American teenagers 31.2% — more than six times the national rate.

Numeric

July 2007. Montana 2.7%, with its oil boom; Michigan 7.2%, with auto layoffs.

Illustrative

Seven years earlier. In 2000 Michigan was at 3.7% and Montana at 4.8% — the positions were reversed.

How it connects. It is a caution about reading the national unemployment rate (entry 6).

Check yourself

15.1 The national rate is 4.7%. Can some group have a rate of 30%?

Show answer

Yes. The national rate is a weighted average, and small groups barely move it.

15.2 Why do differences between states tend not to last?

Show answer

People move to where the jobs are.

Tackle the test — free response

Question 1 (6 points). Population age 16 and older = 12 million; employment = 5 million; unemployment = 1 million. Calculate, showing how: (a) the size of the labor force, (b) the labor force participation rate, (c) the unemployment rate.

Show the answer and the rubric
  1. (a) 6 million — one point
  2. (a) working: employment + unemployment = 5 + 1 — one point
  3. (b) 50% — one point
  4. (b) working: labor force ÷ population × 100 = 6 ÷ 12 × 100 — one point
  5. (c) 16.7% (the printed rubric rounds to 17%) — one point
  6. (c) working: unemployment ÷ labor force × 100 = 1 ÷ 6 × 100 — one point

Half the marks are for the working. Write the fraction before you divide.

Question 2. Classify each person as precisely as you can, and explain. (a) Julie has a graduate degree in mechanical engineering and works full time mowing lawns. (b) Jeff was laid off, would take any job, and after a year of looking has stopped. (c) Ian works 25 hours a week in a bookshop and does not want full-time work. (d) Raj is taking a year off to stay home with his daughter.

Show the answer

(a) Employed. The back of your textbook says “underemployed”, but the textbook’s own definition of underemployed is working part time for lack of full-time work. Julie works full time. She is overqualified for her job; in the statistics she is simply employed.

(b) Not in the labor force — a discouraged worker. He stopped searching because of the job market.

(c) Employed, part time by choice. Not underemployed.

(d) Not in the labor force. Not working and not looking.

Question 3 — the one your exam board published. The population of Country X is 250,000, and the labor force is 200,000 people. If 175,000 people are employed, what is the unemployment rate? (A) 10% (B) 12.5% (C) 20% (D) 80% (E) 87.5%

Show the answer

(B) 12.5%. Unemployed = 200,000 − 175,000 = 25,000. Rate = 25,000 ÷ 200,000 × 100.

Every other option is a real mistake. 10% divides by the population. 20% is the share of the population outside the labor force. 80% is the participation rate. 87.5% is the share of the labor force that is employed. The question is a test of one thing: choosing the denominator.

The five things to get right from Module 12

  1. Three conditions for unemployed: no job, looked in the past four weeks, available. Not working is not enough.
  2. Three groups, one each: employed, unemployed, not in the labor force.
  3. Two denominators. Unemployed over the labor force; the labor force over adults.
  4. Giving up lowers the unemployment rate, and retiring raises it. Check the participation rate before you call a falling rate good news.
  5. Part-timers are employed. That is why the rate cannot see underemployment.
