The headline. Module 11 is the first module of original Macro work, and it is well aligned on the idea and short on the arithmetic. It teaches real against nominal GDP completely and the limitations of GDP in substance. But the learning objective it serves says "Calculate real GDP and the GDP deflator", and the phrase GDP deflator occurs 0 times in pp. 112–117. It arrives thirty pages later, in Module 15 (p. 146). Meanwhile the CED's own sample free-response question 2 (pp. 139, 146) — tagged MEA-1.J and MEA-1.I — opens with "calculate Fehran's real GDP in 2012" from a nominal GDP and a deflator. Teach the deflator here. Section 3 says what else has to be supplied, and it is not much.

Krugman's two stated objectives for the module (p. 112):

  1. The difference between real GDP and nominal GDP
  2. Why real GDP is the appropriate measure of real economic activity

Both are delivered. The textbook's topic order is the CED's in reverse — 2.6 first, then 2.2 on a single page (p. 115) — and that order is the right one to teach: a limitation of real GDP per capita only makes sense once real GDP exists.

The essential knowledge statements, verbatim. Topic 2.6 (CED p. 52):

MEA-1.I.1 — Nominal GDP is a measure of how much is spent on output. Real GDP is a measure of how much is produced. MEA-1.I.2 — Nominal GDP measures aggregate output using current prices. Real GDP measures aggregate output using constant prices, thus removing the effect of changes in the overall price level. MEA-1.J.1 — One way of measuring real GDP is to weigh final goods and services by their prices in a base year. Because this can lead to overstatement of real GDP growth, statistical agencies actually use different methods. MEA-1.J.2 — Nominal GDP can be converted to real GDP by using the GDP deflator.

Topic 2.2 (CED p. 47):

MEA-1.B.1 — GDP is a useful indicator of a nation's economic performance, but it has some limitations, such as failing to account for nonmarket transactions.

Three things in that wording carry the module. "How much is spent" against "how much is produced" is the CED's one-line definition and a better one than the textbook's. "Can lead to overstatement" is a directional claim the textbook never makes. And "nonmarket transactions" is the only limitation the framework names — by a word the textbook does not use.

The overstatement case, and why the textbook's example does not show it

Krugman computes the apples-and-oranges growth rate twice (p. 115): 15% at year 1 prices and 15.4% at year 2 prices, and concludes that "neither answer … is more 'correct' than the other." That is true, and it is the right motivation for chain-linking. But in this example the early base year gives the lower growth rate, which is the opposite of the case MEA-1.J.1 warns about.

The reason is in the prices. From year 1 to year 2 oranges rose 40% and apples 20%, and orange output also grew faster (20% against 10%). Weighting by the early prices under-weights the faster-growing good. The standard case — the one behind MEA-1.J.1's overstatement — is the reverse: the good whose output grows fastest is the one whose price is falling. Weighting it at its old, high price inflates its contribution.

The student guide's case, recomputed by the build script:

Year 1Year 2
Computers10 at $2,00020 at $1,000
Bread (loaves)1,000 at $51,000 at $10
Nominal GDP$25,000$30,000
Real GDP, year 1 prices$25,000$45,000 — growth 80%
Real GDP, year 2 prices$20,000$30,000 — growth 50%

Computer output doubled; bread output did not change. The early base year values each extra computer at $2,000, a price nobody is paying any more, and reports 80%. That is the overstatement, and it is exactly the pattern of real economies, where the goods whose output grows fastest are the ones technology is making cheaper.

Say plainly what is not missing. Every learning objective's idea is present. MEA-1.I is delivered completely, MEA-1.B in substance, MEA-1.J.1 as far as base-year dependence goes. The gaps are one procedure, one direction and one word.

5. Corrections to make explicitly in class

Table 11.2 has a misprint in its base year

Table 11.2 (p. 114) prints 2005 nominal GDP as 12,683 and 2005 real GDP as 12,638, in billions of 2005 dollars. The paragraph immediately above says "For 2005 the nominal GDP and the real GDP are the same." They must be: 2005 is the base year. Two digits are transposed in one of the cells. Point it out — it is a good test of whether the class has understood the base-year rule — and tell students not to compute a 2005 deflator from it.

The other two rows are sound, and they make a clean deflator exercise: 2001 = 10,286 ÷ 11,347 × 100 = 90.6; 2009 = 14,259 ÷ 12,989 × 100 = 109.8. Prices lower before the base year, higher after — which is the paragraph's own explanation of why real GDP is above nominal in 2001 and below it in 2009.

Nominal GDP does not always overstate growth

Krugman's framing (pp. 112–114) is that nominal GDP overstates growth because prices rise. True with inflation, which is the usual case. But MEA-1.I.2 is symmetric — real GDP removes "the effect of changes in the overall price level" — and Krugman's own Check Your Understanding 1 is a case where nominal GDP understates a fall. Teach the rule as: nominal moves with output and prices; real moves with output only. Then the direction of the gap follows from the direction of prices.

Use the CED's definitions, not only the textbook's

Krugman defines nominal and real GDP by how they are calculated — current prices, base-year prices. The CED adds the plainer one: nominal GDP is "how much is spent on output", real GDP is "how much is produced" (MEA-1.I.1). Students who have only the calculation definition struggle with the conceptual items. Give both, and drill the CED sentence.

The deflator is a price index as far as the exam is concerned

Krugman says the GDP deflator "isn't exactly a price index, although it serves the same purpose" (p. 146). The CED treats it as one: the inflation rate is "the percentage change in a price index, such as CPI or GDP deflator" (MEA-1.F.3, CED p. 50), and the Fehran scoring computes inflation straight from it. Follow the CED.

"Standard of living" means real GDP per capita on this exam

Krugman spends half of p. 115 explaining why real GDP per capita is not a sufficient measure of welfare. That is correct and it is Topic 2.2. But the CED's sample FRQ asks whether "the standard of living of the average person" rose, and scores exactly one answer: compare real GDP per capita. Students who answer with a paragraph about leisure and pollution lose the point. The rule: when a question asks you to decide what happened to living standards from data, compute real GDP per capita; when it asks what GDP fails to capture, give a limitation.

Percentage change is (new − old) ÷ old

Every calculation in this module ends in a growth rate, and two errors recur: dividing by the new value, and subtracting inflation from nominal growth instead of computing real growth. The second is a fair approximation for small numbers and wrong for the ones this module uses: in Table 11.1 nominal growth is 50% and the deflator rises 30.4%, so subtraction gives 19.6% against a true 15%.
