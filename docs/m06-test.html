Section A — examinable content

1. A supply schedule shows that at $1.50 a pound farmers will sell 11.2 billion pounds of coffee beans a year, and at $1.00 a pound they will sell 10.0 billion.

Which statement uses the terms correctly?

  • (A) Supply at $1.50 is 11.2 billion pounds.
  • (B) Supply is the difference between 11.2 and 10.0 billion pounds.
  • (C) Supply falls from 11.2 to 10.0 billion pounds as the price falls.
  • (D) The quantity supplied is 11.2 billion pounds.
  • (E) The quantity supplied at $1.50 is 11.2 billion pounds.

2. A student is asked to draw the market for wheat. They draw price on the horizontal axis, quantity on the vertical axis, and two correctly shaped curves crossing once.

What is the most serious problem?

  • (A) Nothing — the curves cross, which is what the question asked for.
  • (B) The curves should not cross unless the question mentions equilibrium.
  • (C) Two curves are too many; a supply question needs only one.
  • (D) The axes are the wrong way round, which is a scored convention and makes every later reading of the graph wrong.
  • (E) Wheat is not a competitive market, so the diagram does not apply.

3. Your framework states the law of supply as a positive relationship between price and quantity supplied.

What does "positive" mean here?

  • (A) That producers benefit from higher prices.
  • (B) That the quantity supplied is always greater than zero.
  • (C) That price and quantity supplied move in the same direction, which is why the curve slopes upward.
  • (D) That the relationship holds without any other things being equal condition.
  • (E) That supply always increases over time.

4. The world price of coffee beans rises from $1.00 to $1.50 a pound, and Brazilian farmers begin planting coffee on land they had previously left for other uses.

What has happened in the coffee market?

  • (A) Supply has increased; the curve has shifted right.
  • (B) The quantity supplied has increased; there has been a movement up along the supply curve.
  • (C) Both supply and quantity supplied have increased.
  • (D) Supply has increased because more land is now in use, which is a change in inputs.
  • (E) Nothing, because the land was always available.

5. After a hurricane, construction wages in the affected region rise sharply, and construction workers from other regions move there to take the work.

In the market for construction labour in that region, what has happened?

  • (A) The supply of labour has increased; the curve has shifted right, because wages are an input price.
  • (B) Both curves have shifted, so nothing definite can be said.
  • (C) The demand for labour has increased, which is why the wage rose.
  • (D) The supply of labour has decreased, because workers are leaving their own regions.
  • (E) The quantity of labour supplied has increased; there has been a movement along the supply curve, because the wage is the price of labour.

6. Two supply schedules for the same good are compared. At every price in the table, the second schedule shows a smaller quantity than the first.

Which conclusion is justified?

  • (A) The good's price must have fallen between the two schedules.
  • (B) The law of supply has been violated, because quantity fell.
  • (C) There has been a movement down along the supply curve.
  • (D) There has been a decrease in supply, and the supply curve has shifted left.
  • (E) Demand must have decreased, which pulled supply down with it.

7. A student writes: "New technology lowered production costs, so the supply curve shifted down."

What is the problem, if any?

  • (A) No problem; "down" and "right" describe the same movement for a supply curve, but only "right" is standard and unambiguous, and "down" reads like a decrease.
  • (B) The direction is wrong — better technology shifts supply up.
  • (C) The word should be "movement", not "shift", because technology changes costs rather than the price.
  • (D) Technology affects demand, not supply.
  • (E) No problem at all; "down" is the conventional description.

8. Newsprint prices rose sharply in the mid-1990s. Newsprint is the paper newspapers are printed on.

What happened in the market for newspapers?

  • (A) Supply increased; the curve shifted right.
  • (B) The quantity supplied decreased, along an unchanged curve.
  • (C) Supply decreased; the curve shifted left.
  • (D) Demand decreased, because newspapers became more expensive.
  • (E) Nothing, because the price of newspapers themselves had not changed.

9. A restaurant's landlord doubles the rent. Separately, in the same month, the restaurant raises its own menu prices.

Which pair of effects is correct?

  • (A) Both shift the restaurant's supply curve left.
  • (B) Both are movements along the restaurant's supply curve.
  • (C) The rent rise shifts the supply curve left; the menu price rise is a movement along it.
  • (D) The rent rise is a movement along the curve; the menu price rise shifts it left.
  • (E) The rent rise shifts the supply curve left; the menu price rise shifts the demand curve left.

10. An oil refinery makes both petrol and heating oil from the same crude oil. The price of heating oil rises sharply.

What happens in the market for petrol?

  • (A) Supply increases; the curve shifts right, because the refinery is more profitable.
  • (B) Nothing, because the price of petrol has not changed.
  • (C) Demand decreases, because petrol and heating oil are substitutes for buyers.
  • (D) The quantity supplied falls along an unchanged curve.
  • (E) Supply decreases; the curve shifts left, because the refinery switches production towards heating oil.

11. Natural gas often comes out of the ground as a by-product when oil wells are drilled. The price of natural gas rises substantially.

What happens to the supply of crude oil, and why?

  • (A) It decreases, because drillers switch their effort to gas.
  • (B) It increases, because oil and gas are substitutes in production.
  • (C) It is unchanged, because the oil price has not moved.
  • (D) It increases, because gas and oil are complements in production: a higher gas price makes drilling worthwhile, so more wells are drilled and more oil comes up.
  • (E) It decreases, because gas and oil are substitutes in consumption.

12. Beef and leather come from the same animal. Beef and chicken are alternatives on a shopper's plate.

The price of beef rises. Which pair of curves moves?

  • (A) The supply of leather shifts right, and the demand for chicken shifts right.
  • (B) The supply of leather shifts left, and the supply of chicken shifts right.
  • (C) The demand for leather shifts right, and the demand for chicken shifts left.
  • (D) The supply of leather shifts right, and the supply of chicken shifts left.
  • (E) Only the supply of leather moves; chicken is unaffected because it is a different market.

13. A new printing process cuts the cost of producing textbooks by a third.

What happens in the textbook market?

  • (A) A decrease in textbook supply.
  • (B) An increase in textbook demand.
  • (C) An increase in textbook supply — the supply curve shifts right.
  • (D) A movement along the supply curve for textbooks.
  • (E) An increase in textbook prices, because better technology is expensive.

14. Grain farmers become convinced that wheat will be considerably more expensive after the winter. They leave grain in their silos rather than selling it now.

What happens to the supply of wheat today, and how does this compare with the demand side?

  • (A) Supply today increases, the same direction as demand would move.
  • (B) Supply today is unchanged, because no price has actually changed.
  • (C) Supply today decreases, the same direction as demand would move.
  • (D) Supply today decreases, which is the opposite direction from demand: an expected price rise increases demand today but decreases supply today.
  • (E) The quantity supplied today falls along an unchanged curve.

15. Twenty new coffee-growing countries begin exporting for the first time. No existing grower changes anything about how much they will sell at any price.

What has moved?

  • (A) Every existing grower's individual supply curve has shifted right.
  • (B) The market supply curve has shifted right; no individual supply curve has moved.
  • (C) Nothing, since no existing grower changed their behaviour.
  • (D) The market supply curve has shifted left, because there is more competition.
  • (E) There has been a movement along the market supply curve.

16. At a price of $2 a pound, three producers would supply 3,000, 2,000 and 1,000 pounds respectively.

What is the market quantity supplied at $2, and how do you get it?

  • (A) 6,000 pounds — add the quantities, holding the price fixed.
  • (B) 2,000 pounds — take the average of the three.
  • (C) 6,000 pounds at $6, since the prices add as well.
  • (D) 3,000 pounds — the market is set by the largest producer.
  • (E) It cannot be found without knowing the quantity demanded.

17. A market is in equilibrium at a price of $1.00 and a quantity of 10 billion pounds.

Which statement is true?

  • (A) The demand schedule and the supply schedule give the same quantity at every price.
  • (B) The equilibrium quantity is the sum of quantity demanded and quantity supplied, so 20 billion pounds.
  • (C) Everyone who wants the good has obtained it.
  • (D) Producers could not supply more even if the price were higher.
  • (E) The quantity demanded and the quantity supplied are equal at $1.00.

19. In the coffee market, the price is $1.50 a pound. At that price the quantity demanded is 8.1 billion pounds and the quantity supplied is 11.2 billion pounds.

Calculate the imbalance and name it.

  • (A) A shortage of 3.1 billion pounds.
  • (B) A shortage of 8.1 billion pounds.
  • (C) A surplus of 19.3 billion pounds.
  • (D) A surplus of 3.1 billion pounds.

20. In the same market the price is $0.75 a pound. Quantity demanded is 11.5 billion pounds and quantity supplied is 9.1 billion pounds. The equilibrium price is $1.00.

Which statement is correct?

  • (A) A surplus of 2.4 billion pounds, because supply always exceeds demand at low prices.
  • (B) A shortage of 2.4 billion pounds, because the price is below the equilibrium price.
  • (C) A shortage of 2.4 billion pounds, because the quantity supplied is the smaller number, which always means a shortage.
  • (D) A surplus of 20.6 billion pounds.
  • (E) A shortage of 1.0 billion pounds, the difference between the price and the equilibrium price.

21. A market is trading at a price above its equilibrium level.

What happens next, and who makes it happen?

  • (A) The price rises, as sellers exploit the shortage.
  • (B) The price falls, because sellers who cannot find buyers cut their prices to poach business and attract more buyers.
  • (C) The price stays where it is until a curve shifts.
  • (D) The price falls, because the government intervenes to clear the surplus.
  • (E) The quantity supplied falls until it equals the quantity demanded, with no change in price.

Section B — background content

This question tests two entries with no anchor in the AP Macroeconomics framework. Score it separately.

22. A table pairs four prices with the quantities a single farmer would sell at each. A second table does the same for a different farmer.

Which statement is correct about what these tables are and how a Macro exam would use them?

  • (A) Each is an individual supply schedule. A Macro question that refers to "the supply curve" means the market curve, which is their horizontal sum, so add the quantities at each price.
  • (B) Each is a market supply schedule, since any schedule describes a market.
  • (C) They are individual supply schedules and cannot be combined, because the two farmers face different prices.
  • (D) Each is an individual supply schedule, and a Macro question referring to "the supply curve" means whichever of the two is larger.
  • (E) They are demand schedules, because they show quantities at different prices.

Solutions

Question 1 — Quantity supplied · Application

Answer: (E)

Quantity supplied is a number attached to one specific price. Supply is the whole schedule. The key names the term correctly and ties it to $1.50.

  • (A) ✗ Uses "supply" for a single number at a single price.
  • (B) ✗ Supply is not a difference between two quantities.
  • (C) ✗ Supply does not fall when the price falls — the quantity supplied does. This is the

distinction the module is built on.

  • (D) ✗ The term is right but no price is stated, and without a price the number is undefined.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 2 — Supply curve · Application

Answer: (D)

Price goes on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal, by convention, and the convention is separately scored. Worse, every subsequent reading — which quantity goes with which price, which way a shift moves — is wrong once the axes are swapped.

  • (A) ✗ The curves crossing is necessary but not sufficient. The conventions are marked.
  • (B) ✗ Two curves on one diagram cross at the equilibrium whether or not the word is used.
  • (C) ✗ Drawing a market needs both curves. Nothing is wrong with the number of curves.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Wheat with thousands of sellers of an identical grade is the textbook example of a

competitive market.

Question 3 — Law of supply · Analysis

Answer: (C)

"Positive" is a statement about direction, not about benefit or sign. Price up, quantity supplied up; price down, quantity supplied down. That is what produces the upward slope. The law of demand is stated with the opposite word, inverse.

  • (A) ✗ True but irrelevant. The word describes the relationship between two variables.
  • (B) ✗ Confuses "positive" with "greater than zero".
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ The law of supply carries the same other things being equal condition as the law of

demand.

  • (E) ✗ Says nothing about time. It is about one moment and two variables.

Question 4 — Movement along the supply curve · Application

Answer: (B)

Coffee's own price rose, so the quantity supplied rose along the same curve. The extra land being planted is how farmers supply more, not a separate cause.

  • (A) ✗ Nothing shifted. No input price, technology, expectation or producer count changed.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Only one of the two changed.
  • (D) ✗ Right conclusion by the wrong route, and it would fail on a written answer. The land was

always there; the higher price made it worth using.

  • (E) ✗ Something did happen — more beans are supplied. It is a movement, not nothing.

Question 5 — Movement along the supply curve · Analysis

Answer: (E)

The trap is the word wage. In the market for construction labour, the good on the horizontal axis is labour, and the wage is its price. A higher own price means a movement up along the supply curve of labour. Contrast this with a wage rise faced by a restaurant, where the wage is an input price for meals and the meals supply curve shifts.

  • (A) ✗ Wages are an input price in the market for the product, not in the market for labour

itself. Here labour is the product.

  • (B) ✗ The scenario is a clean movement along one curve. Nothing forces a second shift.
  • (C) ✗ Demand for labour probably did rise, and that is why the wage rose — but the question

asks about the workers' response, which is the supply side.

  • (D) ✗ In the affected region, supply rises. Workers arriving is the whole point.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 6 — Change in supply · Analysis

Answer: (D)

The test is at any given price. Less is supplied at every price, so the curve has moved left — a decrease in supply.

  • (A) ✗ A price change moves you within one schedule; it does not produce a second one.
  • (B) ✗ Each schedule can still slope upward. The law is about price and quantity within a

schedule, not between two of them.

  • (C) ✗ A movement happens within a single schedule. Two schedules is the signature of a shift.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Demand does not drag supply with it. They are separate curves with separate determinants.

Question 7 — Change in supply · Analysis

Answer: (A)

On an upward-sloping curve, a rightward shift also looks like a downward one, so "down" is technically describable but dangerously ambiguous — it reads as a decrease when the economics is an increase. Use right and left, which mean the same thing for both curves.

  • (A) ✓ Correct. The student's economics is right; the description is unsafe.
  • (B) ✗ Better technology increases supply. The direction the student means is correct.
  • (C) ✗ Technology is a determinant, and determinants shift. "Shift" is the right word.
  • (D) ✗ Technology is a determinant of supply, not demand.
  • (E) ✗ The conventional description is right and left, precisely to avoid this ambiguity.

Question 8 — Input prices · Application

Answer: (C)

Newsprint is an input for newspapers. A higher input price makes production more costly, so producers are less willing to supply at any given price and the curve shifts left. Krugman records the consequence: papers closed and new ventures were cancelled.

  • (A) ✗ Backwards. Dearer inputs decrease supply.
  • (B) ✗ A movement requires the newspaper's own price to change, which it did not.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Demand is about buyers. Nothing about readers changed.
  • (E) ✗ An input price is exactly the kind of thing that shifts a curve without the good's own

price moving.

Question 9 — Input prices against own price · Analysis

Answer: (C)

Two events, two different mechanisms. Rent is an input price for the restaurant, so the supply curve shifts left. The menu price is the restaurant's own price, so raising it is a movement along the supply curve.

  • (A) ✗ Only one of the two shifts the curve.
  • (B) ✗ Only one of the two is a movement.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Exactly reversed, and it is the reversal students make when they see the word "price" and

stop reading.

  • (E) ✗ The first half is right, but a firm raising its own price is a movement along its supply

curve, not a shift of anybody's demand curve.

Question 10 — Substitutes in production · Analysis

Answer: (E)

Petrol and heating oil are substitutes in production: the refinery can make either from the same crude. A higher heating oil price makes heating oil more attractive to produce, so less petrol is offered at every price and the petrol supply curve shifts left.

  • (A) ✗ Profitability is not the mechanism. What matters is which product the same inputs are

turned into.

  • (B) ✗ Another good's price is precisely one of the five shift factors.
  • (C) ✗ They are substitutes in production, not in consumption. Drivers do not put heating oil

in their cars.

  • (D) ✗ Petrol's own price did not change, so there is no movement along its curve.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 11 — Complements in production · Synthesis

Answer: (D)

Two steps, and both must be right. First classify: gas comes up with oil, so they are complements in production. Then apply the direction: a higher price for one makes the joint activity more worthwhile, so more wells are drilled and more oil is supplied at every price.

  • (A) ✗ Drillers cannot switch from oil to gas — the gas arrives as a by-product of drilling for

oil. That is what makes them complements rather than substitutes.

  • (B) ✗ Right conclusion, wrong classification. Substitutes in production would give the opposite

direction, so this option is right by accident.

  • (C) ✗ Another good's price shifts the curve without the good's own price moving.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Wrong on both counts: wrong relationship, and consumption rather than production.

Question 12 — Production against consumption · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

The pair has to be classified twice, because the stem gives two relationships of different kinds and they move different curves.

  • Beef and leather come off the same animal: complements in production. A higher beef price

means more cattle slaughtered, so more leather reaches the market at every price — the supply of leather shifts right.

  • Beef and chicken are alternatives on a shopper's plate: substitutes in consumption. A

higher beef price sends buyers towards chicken, so the demand for chicken shifts right.

One supply curve and one demand curve. Reading both relationships as the same kind is the error this item is built to catch.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Puts the chicken effect on the supply curve. The stem describes shoppers choosing, which

is consumption, so demand moves.

  • (C) ✗ Puts the leather effect on the demand curve. Nothing here changes what buyers of leather

want; what changed is how much leather is produced.

  • (D) ✗ Both effects on supply curves, and the chicken direction would need the stem to say

farmers choose between raising beef and chicken. It says shoppers choose between eating them.

  • (E) ✗ Chicken is affected — through demand, as a substitute in consumption.

Question 13 — Technology · Analysis

Answer: (C)

Better technology lowers the cost of turning inputs into output, so producers supply more at every price. A rightward shift of the supply curve.

  • (A) ✗ Backwards.
  • (B) ✗ Technology is a determinant of supply. Buyers are unaffected.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Only the good's own price produces a movement.
  • (E) ✗ An increase in supply pushes the equilibrium price down, not up — and that is Module 7's

question in any case.

Question 14 — Expectations · Analysis

Answer: (D)

Expectations are the one factor that works in opposite directions on the two sides of the market, for an obvious reason: buyers and sellers are on opposite sides of the same bargain. An expected price rise makes buyers want to buy now (demand today up) and sellers want to sell later (supply today down).

  • (A) ✗ Right direction for supply, wrong comparison. Demand would move the other way.
  • (B) ✗ Expectations are a determinant precisely because they change behaviour before any price

changes.

  • (C) ✗ The supply half is right and the comparison is wrong, which is the error of carrying the

demand-side rule across unchanged.

  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ A movement needs the own price to change. It has not.

Question 15 — Number of producers · Analysis

Answer: (B)

More producers means more supplied at every price, so the market curve shifts right. No existing grower's individual curve moves at all — there are simply more curves in the horizontal sum. This is the supply-side mirror of a baby boom shifting market demand.

  • (A) ✗ The stem says explicitly that no existing grower changed anything.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ The market curve is the sum of whoever is in the market, so adding sellers changes it

immediately.

  • (D) ✗ More sellers increases supply. "More competition" is not a direction.
  • (E) ✗ A movement requires a price change, and no price is mentioned.

Question 16 — Market supply curve · Application

Answer: (A)

Horizontal summation: hold the price fixed and add the quantities. 3,000 + 2,000 + 1,000 = 6,000 pounds at $2.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ You add quantities; you never average them.
  • (C) ✗ Prices are never added. Every producer faces the same market price — that is what makes

it a market.

  • (D) ✗ The market is the sum of all producers, not the largest one.
  • (E) ✗ The supply curve is defined without reference to demand. Demand is needed for the

equilibrium, not for the curve.

Question 17 — Equilibrium · Analysis

Answer: (E)

Equilibrium is the price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied — your framework's own words. At every other price the two differ, which is the whole point of the diagram.

  • (A) ✗ They are equal at one price. At $2.00 the coffee schedules give 11.6 and 7.1.
  • (B) ✗ The equilibrium quantity is the quantity traded, which is the common value of the two,

not their sum.

  • (C) ✗ Only those willing and able to pay $1.00 buy. Everyone else goes without, and that is not

a shortage.

  • (D) ✗ The upward slope says they could — at a higher price. They choose not to at this one.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 18 — Equilibrium price and quantity · Synthesis

Answer: (B)

Find the row where the two columns are equal: at $4, quantity demanded is 70 and quantity supplied is

  1. So the equilibrium price is $4 and the equilibrium quantity is 70 units — one number, the

common value, not the sum.

  • (A) ✗ Adds the two quantities. At equilibrium they are the same 70 units changing hands.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Equilibrium has nothing to do with being in the middle of the table. At $6 there is a

surplus of 30 units.

  • (D) ✗ No interpolation is needed; the table contains the answer exactly.
  • (E) ✗ Equilibrium requires equality at one price, not at every row.

Question 19 — Surplus · Application

Answer: (D)

The price is above equilibrium, so the imbalance is a surplus, and

surplus = quantity supplied − quantity demanded = 11.2 − 8.1 = 3.1 billion pounds.
  • (A) ✗ Right number, wrong name. A shortage is what happens below the equilibrium price.
  • (B) ✗ Reports one of the quantities rather than the gap, and names it wrongly as well.
  • (C) ✗ Adds the two quantities instead of subtracting them.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ The market will adjust, but the question asks for the imbalance at the stated price, and

it is a definite number.

Question 20 — Shortage · Analysis

Answer: (B)

The price is below the equilibrium price, so the imbalance is a shortage, and

shortage = quantity demanded − quantity supplied = 11.5 − 9.1 = 2.4 billion pounds.

The reasoning matters as much as the number, which is why two options give the right figure.

  • (A) ✗ Right arithmetic, wrong name, and the reason offered is false — supply does not exceed

demand at low prices.

  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Right answer, wrong reason. "The smaller number is supply, so it is a shortage" happens

to work here but is not the test; the test is whether the price is below equilibrium.

  • (D) ✗ Adds the quantities and names it wrongly.
  • (E) ✗ Subtracts prices rather than quantities. The imbalance is measured in pounds of coffee.

Question 21 — Market forces · Analysis

Answer: (B)

Above equilibrium there is a surplus: some sellers cannot find buyers. Those frustrated sellers cut prices to take business from other sellers and tempt more buyers in, and the price falls until the surplus is gone.

  • (A) ✗ Above equilibrium there is a surplus, not a shortage, and the price falls.
  • (B) ✓ Correct. It also names who acts, which is what the learning objective asks for.
  • (C) ✗ No curve needs to move. The imbalance itself creates the pressure on price.
  • (D) ✗ Right direction, wrong mechanism. No government is involved; this is what the market does

on its own.

  • (E) ✗ Quantity supplied does fall, but because the price falls. Price is what adjusts.

Question 22 — Supply schedules, individual and market · Synthesis · background

Answer: (A)

Each table is an individual supply schedule. The market schedule is their horizontal sum — add the quantities at each price, never the prices. And in AP Macroeconomics an unqualified "supply curve" always means the market one: the framework's required content refers to the market supply curve and never uses the phrase individual supply.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ A schedule for one seller is an individual schedule. The market schedule is the sum of

all of them.

  • (C) ✗ Every seller in a competitive market faces the same price, which is exactly what makes

the horizontal sum possible.

  • (D) ✗ The market is the sum, not the larger of the two.
  • (E) ✗ Read the direction: if quantity rises as price rises these are supply schedules.
