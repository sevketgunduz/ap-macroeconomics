The headline, before anything else. This is the most over-delivered module in Section 1. Krugman gives eleven pages, seventeen defined terms and a five-row table of shift factors to a topic the Macro framework covers in two essential-knowledge statements. Nothing required is missing; the teaching problem is entirely one of proportion. See sections 3 and 7.

Krugman's four stated objectives for the module (p. 48):

  1. What a competitive market is and how it is described by the supply and demand model
  2. What the demand curve is
  3. The difference between movements along the demand curve and changes in demand
  4. The factors that shift the demand curve

All four are on-syllabus. Objectives 2 and 3 are MKT-2.A; objective 4 is MKT-2.B; objective 1 is the enduring understanding itself.

The enduring understanding names the market type, quoted verbatim (CED p. 37):

MKT-2 — In a competitive market, demand for and supply of a good or service determine the equilibrium price.

That sentence is the reason competitive market is worth a definition here even though no essential knowledge statement mentions it: the framework's whole claim about price is conditioned on it.

The skill is graphing, not calculation. Topic 1.3 was Skill 1.C, quantitative. Topic 1.4 returns to 4.A — draw an accurately labeled graph. Both of Krugman's free-response items ask students to draw, and the printed rubric for FRQ 1 awards points for axis labels and curve labels before it awards anything for economics. Teach the drawing conventions here or pay for them in Unit 3.

The knowledge elements, in the CED's own words

Learning objective MKT-2.A (CED p. 37):

a. Define (using graphs as appropriate) the law of demand. b. Explain (using graphs as appropriate) the relationship between the price of a good or service and the quantity demanded.
  • MKT-2.A.1 — The law of demand states there is an

inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded, leading to a downward-sloping demand curve.

Learning objective MKT-2.B (CED p. 37):

Explain (using graphs as appropriate) the determinants of demand.
  • MKT-2.B.1 — Factors that influence consumer demand, such as changes in consumer income,

cause the market demand curve to shift.

That is the entire required content of Topic 1.4. Two sentences. Read MKT-2.B.1 again and note what it does and does not do: it asserts that determinants exist and shift the curve, and it names exactly one of them — income. The learning objective asks students to explain "the determinants of demand" in the plural, so the other four are in scope as the content of that objective, but the framework never lists them.

Two things follow, and they point in opposite directions.

Substitutes and complements are a real Macro–Micro difference. The Micro CED names substitutes twelve times; the Macro CED never. They remain teachable and testable here as instances of MKT-2.B's "determinants of demand" — Krugman's own AP Review tests them twice — but a student cannot be held to the vocabulary by citation to this framework.

Normal and inferior goods are a Krugman–CED difference, not a Macro–Micro one. Neither framework uses either phrase. What the Macro CED does name is income, and normal and inferior goods are precisely the two cases of an income change, so this pair is better anchored than the words suggest: MKT-2.B.1 is about income, and these are what an income change does.

Factors that influence consumer demand, such as changes in consumer income, cause the market demand curve to shift. Factors that shift the demand for money, such as changes in the price level, … change the equilibrium nominal interest rate.

Same sentence, different market. The College Board wrote Topic 4.5 in the grammar of Topic 1.4, and a student who learns shift-versus-movement properly now gets the money market, the loanable funds market and the foreign exchange market at a large discount. Say this out loud in class. It is the strongest argument available for why a Macro course spends a period on the demand for coffee.

The one thing worth adding is not a gap but an emphasis. MKT-2.B.1 says the market demand curve shifts. Krugman introduces the individual demand curve first and the market curve last, on p. 55, and a class that runs out of time loses exactly the word the framework uses. Reorder if you must cut: teach market demand as the default and treat the individual curve as the footnote, which is also how Krugman describes normal usage ("most of the time, when economists refer to the demand curve, they mean the market demand curve", p. 55).

Three of the five multiple-choice items turn on the same distinction — own price versus a determinant. That is not padding on Krugman's part. It is the error this topic actually produces, and the CED's own instructional activity is built around it (section 7).

FRQ 1's rubric is the best drawing checklist in Section 1. Put it on the wall:

  1. A table labelled Price and Quantity
  2. Values showing a negative relationship
  3. Price on the vertical axis, Quantity on the horizontal
  4. A negatively sloped curve labelled Demand or D
  5. The curve actually plotting the table
  6. A second curve, labelled, to the right of the first

Points 3, 4 and 6 are Skill 4.A verbatim, and they recur in every graphing question in Units 3–6.

5. Corrections to make explicitly in class

"Demand" and "quantity demanded" are not synonyms, and the exam knows it

This is the whole of the module's difficulty. Krugman states the rule on p. 52:

"When economists talk about a 'change in demand,' saying 'the demand for X increased' … they mean that the demand curve for X or Y shifted — not that the quantity demanded rose or fell because of a change in the price."

Drill it as a two-question test that students can apply without thinking:

  1. Did the good's own price change? If yes, it is a movement along — a change in *quantity

demanded*. Nothing shifts.

  1. Did anything else change? If yes, the curve shifts — a change in demand.

Everything in Table 5.1 is question 2. The own-price case is the only case of question 1, and it is the distractor in three of Krugman's five multiple-choice items.

The price of a related good is not an own price

The sentence that breaks students is "a rise in the price of tea increases the demand for coffee". They hear price and reach for a movement. The price that moved belongs to a different good, so for coffee it is one of the "anything else" changes. Write both goods' names on the board every time: whose price moved, and whose curve are we drawing?

Expectations run backwards from intuition

"I expect the price to fall, so I will buy more" is the wrong direction. Expecting a fall means waiting, which decreases demand today. Expecting a rise means stocking up, which increases it. Krugman's Starbucks example (p. 54) is the memorable version: shoppers who knew the October 2006 price rise was coming bought beans before it. MCQ 4 tests exactly this.

An inferior good is not a bad good

Krugman's own examples make this easy — bus rides against taxi rides, fast food against casual dining (p. 53). Inferior is a statement about the income relationship, not about quality. Ask what happens to the demand for own-brand groceries in a recession; students who get that get the concept.

Say "all other things being equal" every single time

The law of demand is false without it, and Krugman's own worked example is built on the failure case: coffee prices were higher in 2006 than 2002 and consumption was higher, because the other things were not equal (p. 50). Introduce the reconciliation as a puzzle before you give the answer. It is the best two minutes in the module.

Two notes on what is not in the table.

Nothing in this module is a Micro-only requirement in disguise. The Micro framework's demand topic, 2.1, carries seven essential knowledge statements against Macro's two, and the five extra ones — property rights, incentives and constraints, the income and substitution effects, diminishing marginal utility, and deriving market demand by summing individual demand — are Micro content. Krugman does not teach the income effect, the substitution effect or diminishing marginal utility in this module at all, so there is nothing here to strip out. The horizontal summation on p. 55 is the only one of the five that appears, and in Macro it is background.

Elasticity is not here and is not in this course. Students who have met demand elsewhere will ask. Krugman covers it in Section 9, Modules 46–48, which is Micro material; the Macro CED has no elasticity topic.

The CED's Sample Instructional Activity for Topic 1.4 (Activity 2, CED p. 33) is written for exactly this lesson and is worth a period of its own if you have one:

Teaching order — what depends on what

Demand is the most linear topic in Unit 1. Each row means the left term has to be in place before the right one makes sense.

Teachbeforebecause

Prices of related goodsSubstitutesthis is what "related" resolves into, not a separate idea
SubstitutesComplementssame mechanism, opposite sign; teaching them apart guarantees the confusion
Income as a determinantNormal and inferior goodsthese are the two cases of an income change

Teach as a pair, never singly. Each of these is defined against the other, and a student who meets one without the other will confuse them under exam pressure:

  • Demand / quantity demanded — the curve against a point on it. This is the module
  • Movement along / shift of — own price against everything else
  • Substitutes / complements — a price rise in the other good pushes demand the opposite way in

each case

  • Normal / inferior goods — the same income rise, opposite shifts
  • Individual / market demand curve — one consumer against the horizontal sum of all of them
