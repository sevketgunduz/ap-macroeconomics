The headline. This is the heaviest module in Unit 1 and the only one that serves two topics at once. It delivers all of Topic 1.5 and two of the three learning objectives in Topic 1.6, and it brings back a verb that has been absent since Topic 1.3: MKT-2.F(c) says calculate the surplus or shortage. Give it two periods.

Krugman's five stated objectives for the module (p. 59):

  1. What the supply curve is
  2. The difference between movements along the supply curve and changes in supply
  3. The factors that shift the supply curve
  4. How supply and demand curves determine a market's equilibrium price and equilibrium quantity
  5. In the case of a shortage or surplus, how price moves the market back to equilibrium

Objectives 1–3 are Topic 1.5 exactly. Objectives 4–5 are MKT-2.E and MKT-2.F. Nothing is off-syllabus and nothing required by these two learning objectives is missing.

The skill changes mid-module, and it is worth noticing. Topic 1.5 is Skill 4.A, draw a labelled graph — the same as demand. Topic 1.6 is Skill 4.C, demonstrate the effect of a change in an economic situation on an accurately labelled graph. 4.C is the skill that carries the whole of Units 3 to 6: every AD–AS question, every money-market question and every foreign-exchange question is a 4.C question. This module is where students first meet it.

The knowledge elements, in the CED's own words

Topic 1.5, learning objective MKT-2.C (CED p. 38):

a. Define (using graphs as appropriate) the law of supply. b. Explain (using graphs as appropriate) the relationship between the price of a good or service and the quantity supplied.
  • MKT-2.C.1 — The law of supply states there is a

positive relationship between price and quantity supplied, leading to an upward-sloping supply curve.

Learning objective MKT-2.D (CED p. 38): Explain (using graphs as appropriate) the determinants of supply.

  • MKT-2.D.1 — Factors that influence producer supply, such as changes in input prices,

cause the market supply curve to shift.

Topic 1.6, learning objective MKT-2.E (CED p. 39): Define (using graphs as appropriate) market equilibrium.

  • MKT-2.E.1 — Equilibrium is achieved at the price at which

quantities demanded and supplied are equal.

Learning objective MKT-2.F (CED p. 39):

a. Define a surplus and shortage. b. Explain (using graphs as appropriate) how prices adjust to restore equilibrium in markets that are experiencing imbalances. c. Calculate (using graphs as appropriate) the surplus or shortage in the market experiencing an imbalance.
  • MKT-2.F.1 — Whenever markets experience imbalances — creating disequilibrium prices,

surpluses, and shortages — market forces drive prices toward equilibrium.

MKT-2.F(c) is the first calculation since Topic 1.3. Topics 1.4 and 1.5 are pure Skill 4.A. This one asks for a number read off a graph, and it is trivially easy provided students know which quantity to subtract from which. Drill it as a subtraction with a name attached, not as a concept.

2. What this module previews

The demand apparatus came back three times; the equilibrium apparatus comes back in every remaining unit of the course. Topic 1.6 is the last time students will see it in a goods market.

Later topicCEDWhat the diagram becomes

4.5 The Money Marketp. 81Money demand and money supply, nominal interest rate on the vertical axis

In every one of them the logic is identical: the curves cross at equilibrium, a price above it creates a surplus that pushes the price down, a price below it creates a shortage that pushes it up. Say this explicitly. The single most common Unit 4 error is a student who can shift a money-demand curve but cannot say why the interest rate moves — and the answer is the one they learned here.

3. Required elements this module does not contain

One, and it is a matter of sequencing rather than a gap in the book.

MKT-2.G.1 — "Changes in the determinants of supply and/or demand result in a new equilibrium price and quantity" (CED p. 39), under LO MKT-2.G. Module 6 establishes equilibrium and shows how price returns to it after a price disturbance. It does not work through what happens when a curve moves. That is Module 7, Supply and Demand: Changes in Equilibrium, pp. 71–76.

This matters for planning, because Topic 1.6 is a single CED topic with three learning objectives split across two Krugman modules. If you assign Module 6 and stop, students have MKT-2.E and MKT-2.F and none of MKT-2.G — which is the half the exam is most likely to ask about, because it is the Skill 4.C half.

Note also that Krugman's own AP Review for this module already tests MKT-2.G material: Check Your Understanding 2 asks what a bumper grape crop does to the equilibrium price, and free-response 2 asks for the effect of a hurricane on the orange market. Both are Module 7 content appearing in Module 6's review. Either hold them back or teach the two modules as one block.

5. Corrections to make explicitly in class

"Substitutes in production" is not "substitutes in consumption"

This is the module's hardest idea and the exam's favourite trap, and MCQ 2 is built on it.

  • In consumption (Module 5): two goods a buyer chooses between. Tea and coffee. A rise in the

price of one increases demand for the other.

  • In production (Module 6): two goods a producer can make from the same resources. Gasoline and

heating oil from the same barrel of crude. A rise in the price of one decreases supply of the other, because the producer switches.

  • Complements in production: goods that come out together. Oil and natural gas from the same

well; beef and leather from the same animal. A rise in the price of one increases supply of the other.

Write the four cases in a two-by-two on the board and leave it up. Students who have only met substitutes in consumption will answer MCQ 2 with option (c), chicken, every time.

Higher wages are an input price, not a movement

Check Your Understanding 1(c) and 1(d) are deliberately adjacent and point in opposite directions:

  • 1(c): fast-food chains must raise wages to attract workers. Wages are an input price for

fast food, so the supply curve for fast food shifts left.

  • 1(d): construction workers move to hurricane-damaged areas, lured by higher wages. Here wages

are the price of the good — the good is labour — so this is a movement along the supply curve of construction labour.

Same word, two roles. The question to ask is always: what good is on the horizontal axis?

Surplus and shortage are subtractions, and the order matters

MKT-2.F(c) asks students to calculate. Give them the two sentences and nothing else:

  • Surplus = quantity supplied − quantity demanded, and it happens when price is above

equilibrium

  • Shortage = quantity demanded − quantity supplied, and it happens when price is below

equilibrium

Then do both from Figure 6.7 and Figure 6.8 so the numbers are real: at $1.50, 11.2 − 8.1 = 3.1 billion pounds surplus; at $0.75, 11.5 − 9.1 = 2.4 billion pounds shortage. Students who memorise "the gap" without the order will report a negative surplus and lose the mark.

The price moves, not the curves

In this module the disturbance is always a price away from equilibrium, and the curves never move. Surpluses and shortages are what a wrong price produces. Module 7 is the opposite case: a curve moves and a new equilibrium price follows. Students who meet both in one week and are not told which is which will merge them.

"Equilibrium" does not mean "good"

Krugman's definition is behavioural: no individual would be better off doing something different. The supermarket queue is the right image — all the lines equalise, and nobody can gain by switching. It says nothing about whether the outcome is fair or efficient. AP Macroeconomics never asks about the welfare of a goods-market equilibrium; consumer surplus and producer surplus have 0 occurrences in the Macro CED.

Teach as a pair, never singly.

  • Demand / supply — the same machinery with the slope reversed. Teach the second against the first
  • Movement along / shift of — own price against everything else, for the second time
  • Substitutes in production / substitutes in consumption — the module's hardest distinction
  • Substitutes in production / complements in production — same mechanism, opposite sign
  • Surplus / shortage — above and below equilibrium, and two subtractions in opposite orders
  • A price away from equilibrium (this module) / a curve that moves (Module 7) — flag the second
