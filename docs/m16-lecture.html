Lecture — Module 16: Income, Spending and the Multiplier

Teacher's lecture script for Krugman's Economics for AP, Module 16 (pp. 158–171), AP Macroeconomics Unit 3, Topic 3.2. Written for high school students meeting the multiplier for the first time.

  • Companions: module_16_teaching_notes.md ·

student_module_16.html · test_module_16.md

  • Length: about 75 minutes — one and a half periods. Natural break after Part 3.
  • Materials: whiteboard, a calculator for each student, 100 paper slips of "play money" for the activity in Part 2.

How this lecture is built

Every idea in the module answers a question raised by the idea before it. The lecture follows that chain, so students never meet a term before they need it:

Part 1  What do people do with an extra dollar?        -> MPC, MPS
          |  "So what happens to the dollar they spend?"
Part 2  One person's spending is another's income       -> the rounds, the multiplier
          |  "What can start the chain?"
Part 3  The starting push: autonomous spending          -> C, I, G, NX
          |  "What if the government cuts taxes instead of spending?"
Part 4  Taxes: the weaker push                          -> tax multiplier, balanced change, working backwards
          |  "What makes households and firms change their spending?"
Part 5  What moves consumers and firms                  -> expectations, wealth, interest rates
          |  "Does real GDP always rise by the full amount?"
Part 6  The limit: prices                               -> "a maximum of", the bridge to aggregate demand

Keep that diagram on the side of the board all lesson, and tick off each part as you finish it. Students should always be able to see where they are and why the next part comes next.

By the end, students can:

  1. Calculate the MPC and MPS from data, and explain why MPC + MPS = 1.
  2. Explain, in words, why $100 of new spending raises total income by more than $100.
  3. Calculate the expenditure multiplier and the tax multiplier from the MPC.
  4. Explain why a tax cut raises real GDP by less than the same amount of government spending.
  5. Work out the effect of raising spending and taxes together, and the change needed to reach a target.
  6. Name what shifts consumer spending and investment spending.

What students need already: GDP = C + I + G + NX (Module 10), and that real GDP measures output (Module 11). Spend one minute recalling both before you start.

Opening hook — 5 minutes

Say:

"In the early 2000s, Fort Myers, Florida had a housing boom. Builders spent millions on new homes. But it wasn't only builders who got richer: restaurants, car dealers and hair salons all did better too — even though nobody built anything for them. Then the boom ended, and they suffered too. Today's question: how can money spent in one place raise incomes in places it was never spent?"

Ask the class: "A company spends $100 billion building houses. By how much does the country's income rise? Write a number down." Collect a few answers — most will say $100 billion. Say you will come back to their numbers at the end of Part 2.

Why start here: the whole module is the answer to this one question. Every later part either explains the answer or asks "what if...?" about it.

Part 1 — What do people do with an extra dollar? (10 minutes)

Step 1: Disposable income

Say: "Before we can follow money around the economy, we need to know what households can actually spend. That is not their whole income."

Board:

Disposable income = income − taxes + transfers
Example: earn $50,000, pay $8,000 tax, receive $2,000 benefits  ->  $44,000

Transfers are payments like unemployment benefits or Social Security — money from the government that is not payment for work. Disposable means "available to dispose of": to spend or save.

Step 2: Spend or save — the MPC and MPS

Activity (2 minutes): "Imagine you get a $100 raise in your weekly pay — after tax. How much of it would you spend, and how much would you save?" Take four or five answers and write them on the board.

Say: "Whatever you said, the two parts add up to $100. Economists measure the share you spend."

Board:

MPC (marginal propensity to consume) = change in spending ÷ change in disposable income
MPS (marginal propensity to save)    = change in saving   ÷ change in disposable income

Spend $80 of an extra $100:  MPC = 80 ÷ 100 = 0.8    MPS = 20 ÷ 100 = 0.2
                              MPC + MPS = 1

Point to the word marginal: it means "the extra one". The MPC is about the next dollar, not about all your income.

Textbook example (p. 159): disposable income rises by $10 billion and spending by $6 billion: MPC = 6 ÷ 10 = 0.6, so MPS = 0.4.

Watch out — the commonest error. Students divide total spending by total income. A family that spends $42,659 of $46,936 has spent 91% of its income, but that is not the MPC. The MPC asks what happens to the next dollar. (Textbook Figure 16.1, point A; the MPC the data actually give is about 0.53.)

Quick check (ask, wait, then answer):

  • "Disposable income rises by $2,000 and spending by $1,600. MPC?" → 0.8
  • "MPC is 0.9. MPS?" → 0.1

Bridge to Part 2: "So you spend $80 of your extra $100. But that $80 doesn't disappear — someone receives it. What do they do with it?"

Part 2 — One person's spending is another person's income (20 minutes)

Step 3: The chain, told as a story

Say, writing each line as you go:

"Maya gets $100 more. With an MPC of 0.8 she spends $80 at a café. The café owner now has $80 of extra income. She spends 0.8 of it, $64, at a bookshop. The bookshop owner has $64 extra. He spends 0.8 of it, $51.20, on a haircut. The barber spends 0.8 of that..."

Board:

RoundWho receives the incomeExtra incomeSpent (× 0.8)Saved (× 0.2)
1Maya$100$80$20
2Café owner$80$64$16
3Bookshop owner$64$51.20$12.80
4Barber$51.20$40.96$10.24
……smaller and smaller

Ask: "Does this go on for ever? Does it add up to infinity?"

Let students argue for a moment, then: "Each round is smaller, because every time, 20% leaks out into saving. The rounds shrink towards zero, and the total stops growing."

Activity: pass the dollar (5 minutes)

Line up five volunteers. Give the first one 100 slips of "play money" worth $1 each. Each volunteer keeps one slip in five (saves 20%, rounding to whole slips) and passes the rest on: 100, 80, 64, 51, 41. The class keeps a running total of all the money received. After five people the slips are nearly gone. Ask: "Where did the money go?" — into the savings of each person. Ask: "How much money was received in total?" — far more than the $100 we started with.

Step 4: The total — the expenditure multiplier

Say: "We can't add infinitely many rounds by hand, but mathematicians can. The rounds 1 + 0.8 + 0.8² + 0.8³ + … always add up to 1 ÷ (1 − 0.8)."

Board:

Expenditure multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) = 1 ÷ MPS

MPC 0.8:  1 ÷ 0.2 = 5    ->  $100 of new spending raises total income by 5 × $100 = $500

Return to the hook. "A builder spends $100 billion. With your textbook's MPC of 0.6, the multiplier is 1 ÷ 0.4 = 2.5, so income rises by $250 billion — not $100 billion. Look at the numbers you wrote down at the start." (Textbook p. 160: rounds of $100 billion, $60 billion, $36 billion... totalling $250 billion.)

The "aha" to point out: in Maya's chain, total saving = 20% of $500 = $100 — exactly the amount that started the chain. The chain stops when all of the original $100 has leaked into savings.

Build the table with the class — ask them to compute each row:

MPCMPSExpenditure multiplier$100 of new spending becomes
0.50.52$200
0.750.254$400
0.80.25$500
0.90.110$1,000

Ask: "What pattern do you see?" → The more people spend of each extra dollar, the bigger the multiplier, because less leaks out each round.

Watch out. Divide by 1 − MPC, not by the MPC. With MPC 0.8 the multiplier is 5, not 1 ÷ 0.8 = 1.25.
Name it. Your textbook just says "the multiplier". The AP exam says expenditure multiplier or spending multiplier — teach the exam's name now, because Part 4 adds a second multiplier.

It works in reverse. If the builders cut $100 billion, incomes fall round after round by the same amounts. That is how a slump in one industry spreads — the Fort Myers bust.

Quick check:

  • "MPC 0.75; firms spend $20 billion more on equipment. Largest rise in real GDP?" → multiplier 4,

$80 billion

  • "MPS 0.1. Multiplier?" → 10

Part 3 — What starts the chain? (5 minutes)

Say: "In Maya's story, the first $100 came from nowhere. In a real economy, something has to start the chain."

Board:

AUTONOMOUS change  = the first push, the CAUSE      (counted once, then multiplied)
INDUCED spending   = the later rounds, the RESULT   (already inside the multiplier)

The first push can come from any part of GDP:
   C  households decide to spend more at every income
   I  firms decide to build or buy more
   G  the government buys more
   NX foreigners buy more of our exports

Autonomous means "self-governing" — it happens for its own reason, not because incomes rose.

Watch out. Don't count the later rounds again. If firms invest $50 billion and households then spend $30 billion more because their incomes rose, the autonomous change is only $50 billion.

Bridge to Part 4: "One of those four starters is the government. But the government has a second tool: instead of spending, it can cut taxes. Is that the same push?"

Natural break. If you split the lecture over two periods, stop here. Open the next period by re-drawing Maya's table and the multiplier formula.

Part 4 — Taxes: the weaker push (20 minutes)

Step 5: Why a tax cut is weaker than spending

Ask first: "Option A: the government spends $100 billion on roads. Option B: it cuts taxes by $100 billion. Which raises real GDP more?" Take a vote. Many students say "the same".

Say: "Follow the first round. Road spending: $100 billion is paid to builders straight away — all of it is round 1. Tax cut: households get $100 billion more disposable income. What do they do with it?" → Spend the MPC share and save the rest. "So round 1 is only 0.8 × $100 billion = $80 billion."

Board — the two chains side by side (MPC 0.8):

RoundSpend $100bnCut taxes $100bn
1$100bn$80bn
2$80bn$64bn
3$64bn$51.2bn
………
Total$500bn$400bn

"The tax chain is the spending chain with its first step missing."

Step 6: The tax multiplier

Board:

Tax multiplier = − MPC ÷ (1 − MPC)

MPC 0.8:  − 0.8 ÷ 0.2 = − 4
   Tax CUT of $100bn   ->  real GDP rises by 4 × $100bn = $400bn
   Tax RISE of $100bn  ->  real GDP falls by $400bn

Why the minus sign? Taxes and output move in opposite directions: higher taxes, lower spending. Transfers (benefit payments) work like a tax cut — same size, plus sign.

Ask: "Compare 5 and 4. What's the gap?" → exactly 1. It is always 1, whatever the MPC, because the only difference is the missing first round.

MPCExpenditure multiplierTax multiplier
0.52−1
0.754−3
0.85−4
0.910−9
Tell students directly: "This is not in your textbook. The textbook never writes the tax multiplier. Your exam does. Learn it from your guide, entry 8."

Step 7: Spending and taxes together

Ask the exam's own question (AP Macroeconomics CED, sample question 5): "The government raises both taxes and spending by $100 million. MPC 0.8. What happens to real GDP?"

Most students say "nothing — they cancel". Work it on the board:

Spending: + 5 × 100 = + $500 million
Taxes:    − 4 × 100 = − $400 million
Total:                  + $100 million    -> real GDP rises by exactly the amount, whatever the MPC

Why? The two multipliers differ by exactly 1 — so what's left is 1 × the amount. A balanced budget change is not neutral: spending paid for by equal taxes still expands the economy.

Step 8: Working backwards — the policymaker's question

Say: "Policymakers don't start with the spending. They start with a target: 'we need real GDP $200 billion higher. How much should we spend?'"

Board:

Needed change = target ÷ multiplier

MPC 0.8, target + $200bn:
   Government spending:  200 ÷ 5 = $40bn more
   Tax cut:              200 ÷ 4 = $50bn cut      (bigger, because part of it is saved)
Watch out. Divide, don't multiply. A $200 billion target with a multiplier of 5 needs $40 billion, not $1,000 billion.

Quick check:

  • "MPC 0.75; tax cut of $40 billion. Effect?" → tax multiplier −3, real GDP + $120 billion
  • "MPC 0.75; target + $120 billion. Spending needed? Tax cut needed?" → $30 billion; $40 billion

Bridge to Part 5: "We've seen the government's push. But most recessions don't start with the government — they start with households and especially firms changing their minds. Why would they?"

Part 5 — What makes households and firms change their spending? (10 minutes)

Step 9: Consumers — expectations and wealth

Say: "Your spending depends on your income today. But two other things change how much you spend at every income."

Board:

Consumer spending shifts UP when:            Consumer spending shifts DOWN when:
 - people expect higher future income         - people fear layoffs / lower future income
 - wealth rises (stock or house prices up)    - wealth falls (house prices fall, as in 2008)

Examples to tell:

  • A student lands a great job that starts in six months — and buys new clothes now.
  • A worker hears the factory may close — and starts saving now, though their pay hasn't changed.
  • Maria and Mark both earn $30,000, but Maria owns a house and has $200,000 in the bank: she spends more

(textbook p. 166).

Connect back: "Each of these is an autonomous change in C — so it starts a multiplier. A stock boom that makes households spend $10 billion more, with MPC 0.75, can raise real GDP by 4 × $10 billion = $40 billion."

Watch out — keep for Module 17. A change in current income is a move along the spending relationship, not a shift. And a fall in the price level that raises the real value of money is a different idea — the real wealth effect — which you'll meet next module.

Step 10: Firms — three things that move investment

Board:

Planned investment FALLS when interest rates RISE          (borrowing costs more; fewer projects pay)
Planned investment RISES when firms expect FASTER GROWTH   (they need more capacity for future sales)
Planned investment FALLS when firms have SPARE CAPACITY    (idle machines: no need to buy more)

Example to tell (p. 167): a $150,000 mortgage costs $1,048 a month at 7.5% interest but $851 at 5.5% — $197 a month less. Lower rates made houses affordable and set off the 2000s housing boom: an autonomous rise in investment, multiplied — the Fort Myers story from the opening.

One subtle point: "What if the firm uses its own saved profits instead of borrowing?" → It still pays a cost: it could have lent that money out and earned interest. The interest rate matters either way.

Why it matters: investment swings far more than consumer spending, and most recessions start with a fall in investment — which the multiplier then spreads to everyone else.

Bridge to Part 6: "So, all these pushes get multiplied. But does real GDP always rise by the full multiplied amount?"

Part 6 — The limit: prices (5 minutes)

Say: "Your textbook makes one big assumption: firms will produce any extra output at the same prices. If that's true, every extra dollar of spending is an extra dollar of real GDP."

"But suppose the economy is already producing nearly flat out. Extra spending then pushes prices up, and some of the multiplied spending becomes higher prices, not more goods."

Board:

The multiplier tells you how far total spending (aggregate demand) shifts.
Real GDP rises by that amount AT MOST.

Exam wording: "Real GDP will increase by a MAXIMUM of $100."

Bridge to the next module: "Next lesson we draw this. The multiplier tells us how far the aggregate demand curve moves — and expectations, wealth and interest rates from Part 5 become the things that shift it."

Wrap-up — 5 minutes

Return to the diagram from the start of the lesson and summarise each part in one sentence, asking students to supply each one:

  1. Of every extra dollar, people spend the MPC share and save the MPS share; MPC + MPS = 1.
  2. Spending becomes someone else's income, so the first push multiplies: × 1 ÷ (1 − MPC).
  3. The push can start in C, I, G or NX; later rounds are induced and already counted.
  4. A tax change is weaker — × −MPC ÷ (1 − MPC) — because part of it is saved first; equal changes in

spending and taxes move real GDP by their own amount; to hit a target, divide.

  1. Expectations and wealth shift consumer spending;

interest rates, expected growth and spare capacity move investment.

  1. Rising prices can absorb part of it, so the multiplied amount is the maximum.

Exit ticket (answers below)

  1. Disposable income rises by $500 and spending by $400. What are the MPC and the MPS?
  2. MPC 0.9. Firms invest $10 billion more. What is the largest possible rise in real GDP?
  3. MPC 0.75. The government cuts taxes by $20 billion. What is the largest possible change in real GDP?
  4. MPC 0.6. The government raises both spending and taxes by $50 billion. What happens to real GDP?
  5. In one sentence: why does a tax cut raise real GDP by less than the same amount of government

spending?

Answers:

  1. MPC = 400 ÷ 500 = 0.8; MPS = 0.2.
  2. Multiplier 1 ÷ 0.1 = 10; 10 × $10 billion = $100 billion.
  3. Tax multiplier −0.75 ÷ 0.25 = −3; a cut of $20 billion raises real GDP by $60 billion.
  4. It rises by $50 billion (2.5 × 50 − 1.5 × 50 = 125 − 75 = 50).
  5. Households save part of a tax cut before spending any of it, so the first round of spending is

smaller.

Teacher's notes

Misconceptions to listen for, in the order they usually appear:

Students sayCorrect it with
"MPC = what I spend ÷ what I earn"The MPC is about the extra dollar — the change, not the total
"Multiplier = 1 ÷ MPC"Re-draw Maya's table: with MPC 0.8 the total is 5 × $100
"The rounds go on for ever, so the total is infinite"Each round leaks into saving; total saving ends up equal to the first push
"A tax cut is the same as spending"Compare the first rounds: $100bn against $80bn
"Raising taxes and spending equally cancels out"Work sample question 5 on the board: +500 − 400 = +100
"To raise GDP by $200bn, spend $200bn × 5"Policymakers divide the target by the multiplier
"Any rise in wealth is the wealth effect"A stock boom shifts spending; a fall in the price level moves along (Module 17)

What is left out on purpose. The textbook's consumption-function equation (c = a + MPC × yd), the inventory and unplanned-investment section, and the permanent-income and life-cycle hypotheses are background for this exam — the teaching notes, section 1, give the reasons. Students meet them in the guide (entries 12, 15, 16) and the test's Section B. If you have spare minutes, the consumption function is the most useful of the three: it shows the MPC as the slope of a line.

What the textbook does not supply. Parts 4 and 6 — the tax multiplier, the comparison of the two multipliers, the balanced change, working backwards and the "maximum" wording — are not in Module 16. Tell students so, and send them to the guide (entries 6–11), so they do not hunt for it in the book.
