The Circular Flow and GDP

Seventeen terms from Module 10 of Krugman’s Economics for AP, the first module of the course that is purely macroeconomics. It answers one question — how do you measure everything an economy produces? — and gives you three ways to do it that always give the same answer.

Textbook pages 102–111·17 terms· Topic 2.1·2 periods

How to use this guide

Read Module 10 in your textbook first, then work through the entries here in order. Entries 1 to 5 are the circular flow; entries 6 to 17 are GDP and how to measure it.

  • ★ Exam means the AP Macroeconomics framework can test it.
  • ★ ⚑ means it is examinable and your textbook never names it. Four entries carry the flag. The ideas are all in Module 10; the names are not.
  • ○ Background means it helps you understand the topic but will not be asked about on its own.
  • Every entry ends with two Check yourself questions. Answer them before opening the answer, then tick Got it.
  • The in-or-out sorter is the part to spend time on. It is the most examined idea in the module.

The one idea. GDP counts final output once. Every rule in this module — leave out intermediate goods, leave out used goods, leave out stocks and transfers, add inventories, subtract imports — is there to make sure each piece of this year’s domestic production is counted exactly one time.

⚑ Four names your textbook leaves out. Your framework says there are three ways of measuring GDP — the expenditures approach, the income approach and the value-added approach — and asks you to calculate nominal GDP. Module 10 teaches all four ideas and names none of them. Entries 8, 13, 14 and 17 give you the names.

This is your textbook’s Figure 10.1. Follow one dollar round it: spent in a product market, received by a firm, paid out in a factor market, received by a household, spent again.

The 17 terms

01

National income and product accounts

○ Background
Definition

The official record of the flows of money between the different sectors of the economy — consumer spending, sales of producers, business investment, government purchases and more. Usually called the national accounts.

Textbook page 102.

Every number in this unit comes from here. In the United States the accounts are kept by the Bureau of Economic Analysis, and GDP is their headline figure.

It is marked background because your exam asks about GDP, not about the accounts as an institution. Read it once, then move on.

Watch out. The national accounts measure flows over a period — spending and income in a year — not how much wealth a country has at a moment. GDP is a flow in the same sense.

Textbook

A sign of development. Your textbook notes that the more reliable a country’s accounts, the more economically advanced it tends to be, and that aid agencies often start by improving them.

Numeric

The headline number. U.S. GDP in 2009 was $14,259 billion, about $46,372 per person.

Illustrative

A household version. A record of everything your household earned and spent this month is a tiny set of accounts. Add up every household, firm and government and you have the national ones.

How it connects. GDP (entry 7) is the accounts’ headline figure, and the circular-flow diagram (entry 2) is the picture of what they record.

Check yourself

1.1 Is the national accounts a stock of wealth or a record of flows?

Show answer

A record of flows — money moving between sectors over a period of time.

1.2 Why is this entry marked background?

Show answer

Because the exam tests GDP and how it is measured, not the accounts as an institution.

02

The circular-flow diagram

★ Exam
Definition

A simplified picture of the economy showing flows of money in one direction and flows of goods, services and factors of production in the other.

Textbook pages 102–103, Figure 10.1.

The idea to take from it is one sentence: every dollar spent is a dollar of someone’s income. When households buy goods, that money becomes firms’ revenue; firms pay it out as wages, rent, interest and profit, which is households’ income; and round it goes.

That is why your framework describes GDP as a total flow of income and expenditure. The diagram is the proof that the two are the same total.

Watch out. Money and real things flow in opposite directions. If you draw goods and money going the same way round the circle, the diagram is wrong.

Textbook

Figure 10.1. Households buy goods and services from firms in product markets; firms buy labor and other factors from households in factor markets.

Numeric

In must equal out. If households receive $500 of wages, rent, interest and profit and there is no government or saving, they spend $500 — which is $500 of revenue for firms.

Illustrative

Your part-time job. The wage the café pays you is income to you and a cost to the café. When you spend it at the cinema, it becomes the cinema’s revenue.

How it connects. It links households and firms (entry 3) through product and factor markets (entry 4), the expanded version (entry 5) adds the other sectors, and it is why the three approaches agree (entry 15). The national accounts (entry 1) record its flows.

Check yourself

2.1 In the simple circular flow, which way does money flow through factor markets?

Show answer

From firms to households — as wages, rent, interest and profit. The factors themselves go the other way, from households to firms.

2.2 Why can GDP be measured either as spending or as income?

Show answer

Because every dollar spent on output is received by someone as income. The circular flow shows the two totals are the same money seen from each side.

03

Households and firms

★ Exam
Definition

A household is a person or group of people who share their income. A firm is an organization that produces goods and services for sale.

Textbook page 103.

In the simple diagram these are the only two sectors. Households own the factors of production — their labor, and the land and capital they own directly or through shares — and sell them to firms. Firms use those factors to produce goods and services, which households buy.

Watch out. Households are sellers too. In factor markets it is the household that sells and the firm that buys. Students who think of households only as shoppers get the factor-market arrows backwards.

Textbook

Who owns what. Your textbook: households own the factors of production — land, labor and capital — and sell their use to firms for rent, wages and interest.

Numeric

One household’s income. Wages $40,000 + interest $500 + rent from a room $3,000 + a dividend of $200 = $43,700, all of it from firms.

Illustrative

Two roles in one day. In the morning you sell an hour of labor to a shop; in the afternoon you buy lunch from another firm. Seller in one market, buyer in the other.

How it connects. Their transactions take place in product and factor markets (entry 4), and together they make up the simple circular flow (entry 2).

Check yourself

3.1 In factor markets, who is the buyer and who is the seller?

Show answer

Firms buy; households sell. The thing sold is the use of a factor of production — usually labor.

3.2 A family earns $52,000 in wages and $1,000 in interest. What kind of market did each come through?

Show answer

Both came through factor markets: wages for labor, interest for lending capital. Together they are $53,000 of factor income.

04

Product markets and factor markets

★ Exam
Definition

Product markets are where goods and services are bought and sold. Factor markets are where resources, especially capital and labor, are bought and sold.

Textbook page 103.

These are the two halves of the circle, and they line up exactly with the two halves of your framework’s sentence. Product markets are the expenditure side: money flows in as spending. Factor markets are the income side: money flows out as wages, rent, interest and profit.

Watch out. The labor market is a factor market, not a product market. A wage is the price of a factor; the price of a haircut is the price of a product.

Textbook

The labor market. Your textbook calls it the best-known factor market: workers are paid for their time.

Numeric

Spending in, income out. Firms sell $800 of goods in product markets and pay $800 in wages, rent, interest and profit in factor markets. Same money, two markets.

Illustrative

Where is the transaction? A bakery buying an oven: product market. A bakery hiring a baker: factor market. A family buying bread: product market.

How it connects. They connect the two sectors of entry 3. The product-market side is where the expenditures approach (entry 13) measures GDP; the factor-market side is where the income approach (entry 14) does. Together they are the two halves of the circular flow (entry 2).

Check yourself

4.1 A software company hires a programmer. Product market or factor market?

Show answer

Factor market. The company is buying labor, a factor of production.

4.2 Which market does the expenditures approach measure, and which does the income approach measure?

Show answer

The expenditures approach adds up spending in product markets; the income approach adds up payments in factor markets.

05

The expanded circular flow

★ Exam
Definition

The circular flow with the government, the rest of the world and financial markets added. Households pay taxes and receive transfers; what is left, disposable income, is spent or saved; private savings flow through financial markets to firms, the government and borrowers abroad.

Textbook pages 103–105, Figure 10.2.

The same rule still holds for every box: what flows in must flow out. For households, money in is wages, profit, interest, rent and government transfers; money out is taxes, consumer spending and private savings. The two totals are equal.

Several of the flows in this diagram come back later in the course with their own units: the financial-markets box becomes the market for loanable funds, government borrowing becomes the deficit, and exports and imports become the whole of Unit 6. Stocks (shares of ownership) and bonds (loans that pay interest) are how households hold claims on firms.

Watch out. Savings are not spending on output, so they are not part of GDP directly — but they are not lost either. They flow through financial markets and come back as investment or government borrowing.

Textbook

Figure 10.2. Four sectors — households, firms, government, rest of the world — linked by three kinds of market: product, factor and financial.

Numeric

The household box balances. In: wages, profit, interest and rent $1,000 + transfers $100 = $1,100. Out: taxes $200 + consumer spending $750 + private savings $150 = $1,100. Disposable income is $1,000 + $100 − $200 = $900.

Illustrative

Where your savings go. Money you put in a bank is lent to a firm building a warehouse. It left the household box as savings and reached a product market as investment spending.

How it connects. It extends the simple circular flow (entry 2), and it is where government purchases and transfers (entry 11) and exports and imports (entry 12) enter. Consumer spending (entry 9) is the largest flow out of the household box.

Check yourself

5.1 A household earns $2,000, pays $400 in taxes and receives $150 in transfers. What is its disposable income?

Show answer

$2,000 + $150 − $400 = $1,750.

5.2 That household spends $1,500. What are its private savings, and where do they go?

Show answer

$1,750 − $1,500 = $250. They go into financial markets, which channel them to firms, the government or borrowers abroad.

06

Final and intermediate goods

★ Exam
Definition

Final goods and services are sold to the final, or end, user. Intermediate goods and services are bought by one firm from another to be used as inputs into final goods and services.

Textbook page 106.

This distinction is the whole of how GDP avoids double counting. The steel in a car is paid for twice — once when the steel mill sells it to the car maker, and again inside the price of the car. Count only final goods, and the steel is counted once.

Your framework’s phrase is final output: it means exactly what your textbook calls final goods and services.

Watch out. Whether a good is final depends on who buys it and why, not on what it is. Flour bought by a bakery is intermediate; the same flour bought by a family is final.

Textbook

Steel and cars. An automaker’s purchase of steel is intermediate; a consumer’s purchase of the finished car is final.

Numeric

Total sales is not GDP. In your textbook’s three-firm economy total sales are $34,700, but GDP is $21,500. The $13,200 difference is ore and steel counted again.

Illustrative

Wheat to bread. A farmer sells wheat to a mill for $200, the mill sells flour to a baker for $300, the baker sells bread for $1,000. Only the $1,000 of bread is final.

How it connects. GDP (entry 7) counts only final goods. Value added (entry 8) is the other way to avoid counting intermediate goods twice, and intermediate goods head the “out” list in entry 16.

Check yourself

6.1 A restaurant buys $80 of fish and a family buys $80 of fish. Which purchase is final?

Show answer

The family’s. The restaurant’s fish is an input into meals, so it is intermediate — its value will be counted in the price of the meals.

6.2 Why would adding every firm’s sales overstate GDP?

Show answer

Because the value of intermediate goods would be counted once when they are sold and again inside the final good.

07

Gross domestic product (GDP)

★ Exam
Definition

The total value of all final goods and services produced in an economy during a given period, usually a year.

Textbook page 106.

Take the definition apart word by word, because an exam question will usually hinge on one of them. Final: no intermediate goods. Produced: new output, so no used goods and no financial assets. In an economy: within the country’s borders, whoever owns the firm. During a period: this year’s production, not this year’s sales.

Your framework states it in five words: GDP is a measure of final output.

Watch out. GDP is about production, not sales. A book printed this year and left unsold is in this year’s GDP, through inventories. A house built in 1990 and sold this year is not.

Textbook

The United States, 2009. GDP was $14,259 billion, about $46,372 per person.

Numeric

The car economy. Three firms, one car a year: GDP is $21,500, the value of the car — not the $34,700 of total sales.

Illustrative

Domestic means where. A Japanese-owned car plant in Tennessee adds to U.S. GDP. An American-owned factory in Mexico does not.

How it connects. It counts final goods (entry 6), it can be measured three ways (entries 8, 13 and 14), and every GDP in this module is nominal (entry 17). The national accounts (entry 1) are where it is recorded, and entry 16 applies the definition case by case.

Check yourself

7.1 A German firm’s factory in South Carolina produces $2 million of cars. Is that in U.S. GDP?

Show answer

Yes. GDP counts production within the country’s borders, whoever owns the firm.

7.2 Which word in the definition excludes a sale of used furniture?

Show answer

Produced — the furniture was produced, and counted, when it was new. Selling it again is not new production.

08

Value added — the value-added approach

★ ⚑
Definition

A producer’s value added is the value of its sales minus the value of its purchases of inputs. The value-added approach measures GDP as the sum of every producer’s value added.

Textbook pages 106–107, Figure 10.3. Your textbook calls this “measuring GDP as the value of production of final goods and services”.

⚑ Learn the name from here. Your framework calls this the value-added approach. The phrase does not appear anywhere in your textbook, which describes the method without naming it. Use the framework’s name in answers.

At each stage of production you subtract what the firm paid other firms for inputs. What is left is what that firm itself added. Add those amounts across every firm and each piece of value is counted exactly once.

In your textbook’s car economy: the ore mine adds $4,200 (it buys no inputs), the steel mill adds $9,000 − $4,200 = $4,800, and the car maker adds $21,500 − $9,000 = $12,500. The total is $21,500 — the value of the car.

Watch out. Subtract inputs bought from other firms, not wages. Wages are part of value added: they are how the value a firm adds is paid out.

Textbook

Figure 10.3. $4,200 + $4,800 + $12,500 = $21,500, equal to GDP.

Numeric

Wheat to bread. Farmer $200, mill $300 − $200 = $100, baker $1,000 − $300 = $700. The value added sums to $1,000 — the price of the bread.

Illustrative

Why the name. A tailor who buys $30 of cloth and sells a $90 jacket has added $60 of value. That $60 is the tailor’s contribution to GDP.

How it connects. It is one of the three approaches, with the expenditures approach (entry 13) and the income approach (entry 14); it is the arithmetic that keeps intermediate goods (entry 6) from being counted twice; and it is why the three agree (entry 15). Its total is GDP (entry 7) — nominal GDP (entry 17).

Check yourself

8.1 A printer buys $3,000 of paper and ink and sells $10,000 of books. What is its value added?

Show answer

$10,000 − $3,000 = $7,000.

8.2 Why would $30,500 — the price of a car plus a car’s worth of steel — be the wrong value added?

Show answer

It counts the steel twice: once when the steel mill sells it and again inside the car. The car maker’s value added is $21,500 − $9,000 = $12,500.

09

Consumer spending (C)

★ Exam
Definition

Household spending on goods and services.

Textbook pages 103 and 108.

The first letter of the spending equation, and by far the largest. In 2009 it was 70.8% of U.S. GDP. When this letter moves, GDP moves with it — which is why the next unit spends so much time on what makes consumers spend.

Watch out. Buying a newly built house is not consumer spending: it is counted as investment spending (entry 10). Rent paid on a home is consumer spending on housing services.

Textbook

2009. C = $10,093 billion out of GDP of $14,259 billion.

Numeric

Its share. $10,093 ÷ $14,259 = 70.8%. More than two-thirds of all spending on final output.

Illustrative

Everyday C. A haircut, a phone contract, groceries, a concert ticket: all consumer spending on goods and services.

How it connects. It is the largest term in the expenditures approach (entry 13), and it is what households do with most of their disposable income in the expanded circular flow (entry 5).

Check yourself

9.1 Which component of spending is the largest part of U.S. GDP?

Show answer

Consumer spending — about 70.8% in 2009. This is your textbook’s multiple-choice question 5.

9.2 A family buys a newly built house. Is that C?

Show answer

No. New residential construction is counted as investment spending, because a house produces housing services for many years.

10

Investment spending (I) and inventories

★ Exam
Definition

Spending on new productive physical capital, such as machinery and structures, and on changes in inventories. Inventories are stocks of goods and raw materials that firms hold to facilitate their operations.

Textbook pages 105–106 and 110.

“Investment” here does not mean buying shares. It means adding to the economy’s stock of physical capital: a new factory, a machine, a new house — and unsold goods added to inventories.

Inventories are counted because GDP measures production. A car made this year and not yet sold was still produced this year, so it goes into GDP as an addition to the firm’s inventory. When it sells next year, the fall in inventories offsets the sale.

Watch out. Buying a share of stock is not investment spending in the national accounts — it transfers ownership of a company and produces nothing. Building the company a new plant is.

Textbook

New structures and unsold books. Your textbook’s free-response answers: a new bottling plant built in the U.S. is in GDP, and a publisher’s unsold books added to inventory are counted as investment.

Numeric

2009. I = $1,623 billion, 11.4% of GDP.

Illustrative

Inventory both ways. A toy firm makes 1,000 toys and sells 800: the other 200 are an addition to inventory, counted this year. Next year it makes none and sells those 200: inventories fall by 200 and GDP from the toys is zero.

How it connects. It is the I in the expenditures approach (entry 13); the rule that changes in inventories count is part of what is in GDP (entry 16), and it is why the three approaches agree (entry 15).

Check yourself

10.1 Which of these is investment spending in GDP: buying shares in a car company, or the car company buying a new robot?

Show answer

The robot. It is new physical capital. Buying shares only changes who owns the company.

10.2 A firm produces $500 of goods this year and sells $400 of them. How much of the $500 is in this year’s GDP?

Show answer

All $500: $400 as sales to final users and $100 as an addition to inventories, which counts as investment spending.

11

Government purchases and government transfers

★ Exam
Definition

Government purchases of goods and services (G) are total spending on goods and services by federal, state and local governments. Government transfers are payments the government makes to individuals without expecting a good or service in return.

Textbook page 105.

Only the first is in GDP. When a city pays a teacher or buys chalk, it is buying a service or a good that was produced. When the government sends a Social Security or unemployment payment, nothing is produced in exchange: money moves from taxpayers to recipients, and it counts in GDP only later, if the recipient spends it.

Watch out. “Government spending” in a news story usually includes transfers. G in the GDP equation does not. Check which one a question means.

Textbook

Chalk and ammunition. Your textbook: government purchases include everything from military spending on ammunition to a local school’s chalk and teacher salaries. Unemployment insurance is its example of a transfer.

Numeric

2009. G = $2,933 billion, 20.6% of GDP — transfers not included.

Illustrative

Two payments, one budget. A state spends $50 million building a bridge and $50 million on welfare payments. Only the $50 million bridge is G.

How it connects. It is the G in the expenditures approach (entry 13); transfers are one of the flows in the expanded circular flow (entry 5), and they are on the “out” list in entry 16.

Check yourself

11.1 A government pays $1 billion in pensions and $1 billion for new roads. How much is G?

Show answer

$1 billion — the roads. The pensions are transfers.

11.2 Why is a transfer payment not part of GDP?

Show answer

Because no good or service is produced in exchange for it. GDP measures production.

12

Exports, imports and net exports

★ Exam
Definition

Exports (X) are goods and services sold to other countries; imports (IM) are goods and services bought from other countries. Net exports are X − IM.

Textbook pages 105 and 108.

Exports are added because they are produced here and bought by foreigners, so they are in none of C, I or G. Imports are subtracted because they are in C, I and G — a U.S. family buying a French perfume records it as consumer spending — but they were produced abroad. Subtracting IM takes out production that happened elsewhere.

Watch out. Subtracting imports does not mean imports make GDP smaller. It removes spending that was already counted in C, I or G but was not on domestic production. The subtraction corrects the total; it does not penalise it.

Textbook

Net importer. In 2009 U.S. net exports were −$390 billion, −2.7% of GDP.

Numeric

A small country. X = $50 billion, IM = $70 billion. Net exports = 50 − 70 = −$20 billion.

Illustrative

Wine two ways. A California winery selling to Montreal: an export, in U.S. GDP. A Tulsa shopper buying French perfume: an import, not in U.S. GDP.

How it connects. They complete the expenditures approach (entry 13), they enter the expanded circular flow through the rest of the world (entry 5), and foreign-produced goods are on the “out” list in entry 16.

Check yourself

12.1 An American buys a $900 Italian laptop. What does it add to U.S. GDP?

Show answer

Nothing. It is counted in C (+$900) and subtracted in IM (−$900), because it was produced in Italy.

12.2 A country’s exports are $400 billion and imports $350 billion. What are net exports?

Show answer

$400 − $350 = $50 billion, positive: a net exporter.

13

The expenditures approach: C + I + G + (X − IM)

★ ⚑
Definition

Measuring GDP as aggregate spending on domestically produced final goods and services: GDP = C + I + G + (X − IM).

Textbook pages 106 and 108, Equation 10-1 and Figure 10.4.

⚑ Learn the name from here. Your framework calls this the expenditures approach. Your textbook never uses the phrase; it says “aggregate spending on domestically produced final goods and services”. Learn the framework’s name.

Four letters and one subtraction. Your textbook calls the sum aggregate spending; your framework calls the method the expenditures approach. This is the approach you will use most, because in the next unit the same four letters become the components of aggregate demand.

Watch out. Only spending on final goods goes in. A car maker buying steel is not I — the steel is an intermediate good and its value is already in the car. A car maker buying a new stamping machine is I.

Textbook

2009. $10,093 + $1,623 + $2,933 − $390 = $14,259 billion.

Numeric

A made-up economy. C = 600, I = 150, G = 200, X = 80, IM = 100. GDP = 600 + 150 + 200 + 80 − 100 = 930.

Illustrative

Why subtract after adding. C + I + G = $14,649 billion in 2009 — more than GDP, because $390 billion of it was spent on imports.

How it connects. It adds up C, I, G and net exports (entries 9–12); it measures the product-market side of entry 4; and it gives the same answer as the value-added and income approaches (entries 8 and 14) — entry 15 says why. The result is GDP (entry 7), and it is nominal (entry 17).

Check yourself

13.1 C = 700, I = 200, G = 250, X = 100, IM = 150. What is GDP?

Show answer

700 + 200 + 250 + 100 − 150 = 1,100.

13.2 Why is “GDP = C + I + G + IM” wrong?

Show answer

Imports are subtracted, not added: they are spending on goods produced abroad. This is your textbook’s multiple-choice question 2, option (b).

14

The income approach

★ ⚑
Definition

Measuring GDP as the total factor income earned by households from firms: wages + interest + rent + profit.

Textbook pages 106 and 108, the right-hand column of Figure 10.3.

⚑ Learn the name from here. Your framework calls this the income approach. Your textbook describes it — “sum the total factor income earned by households from firms” — but never names it, and says it will not emphasise it. Your exam can.

Every dollar a firm receives for final goods must go somewhere. Whatever is not paid out as wages, interest or rent is profit. So adding up all four gives back the value of what was sold.

In your textbook’s car economy: wages $15,700 + interest $2,600 + rent $1,000 + profit $2,200 = $21,500. Same number again.

Watch out. Transfers are not factor income. A pension or unemployment payment is not earned by supplying a factor, so it is not in the income approach — for the same reason it is not in G.

Textbook

Figure 10.3. $15,700 + $2,600 + $1,000 + $2,200 = $21,500.

Numeric

A small economy. Wages 500 + interest 60 + rent 40 + profit 100 = 700.

Illustrative

Profit is the balance. A café sells $1,000 of coffee, pays $300 for beans, $400 in wages, $100 rent and $50 interest. Profit is $150, and its factor income is $400 + $100 + $50 + $150 = $700 — exactly its value added.

How it connects. It measures the factor-market side of entry 4, it is the third of the approaches with the value-added and expenditures approaches (entries 8 and 13), and entry 15 explains why all three agree. Its total is GDP (entry 7), measured in nominal terms (entry 17).

Check yourself

14.1 Wages are $800, interest $90, rent $60 and profit $150. What is GDP by the income approach?

Show answer

800 + 90 + 60 + 150 = $1,100.

14.2 Why is profit included?

Show answer

Because it is the part of a firm’s revenue not paid as wages, interest or rent — the income of the firm’s owners. Leaving it out would make income fall short of spending.

15

Why the three approaches agree

★ Exam
Definition

Value added, spending on final goods and factor income are the same total, because they are the same flow of money in the circular flow measured at three points.

Textbook page 108; your textbook’s Check Your Understanding 1.

Two links make it work. Spending on final goods equals total value added, because every final good is either bought or added to inventories — and additions to inventories are counted as spending by firms. And spending equals factor income, because firms’ revenue is all paid out as wages, interest, rent or profit.

Watch out. If your three totals disagree in a question, you have counted an intermediate good, left out an inventory change or included a transfer. The approaches cannot disagree when done correctly.

Textbook

All three at $21,500. Value added $4,200 + $4,800 + $12,500; spending on the one car $21,500; factor income $15,700 + $2,600 + $1,000 + $2,200. All $21,500.

Numeric

2009, two ways. By value added: $10,669 + $1,760 + $1,830 = $14,259 billion. By spending: $10,093 + $1,623 + $2,933 − $390 = $14,259 billion.

Illustrative

The café again. Its value added and its factor income were both $700. Scale that up to every firm and the totals must match.

How it connects. It is the circular flow (entry 2) read three ways: value added, expenditures and income (entries 8, 13 and 14), with inventories (entry 10) closing the gap between production and sales.

Check yourself

15.1 A firm makes goods worth $600 and sells only $500. How do the approaches still agree?

Show answer

The unsold $100 goes into inventories, which the national accounts count as investment spending. Spending is $500 + $100 = $600, matching the value produced.

15.2 In a question, spending comes to $1,000 and factor income to $1,050. What is the likeliest error?

Show answer

Something that is not factor income — such as a transfer payment — has been included on the income side, or an intermediate purchase counted on one side.

16

What is in GDP and what is out

★ Exam
Definition

In: domestically produced final goods and services, including capital goods, new structures and changes in inventories. Out: intermediate goods and inputs, used goods, financial assets such as stocks and bonds, and foreign-produced goods and services.

Textbook page 110, “What’s In and What’s Out”.

Every item on the “out” list is out for one of two reasons: counting it would count the same production twice (intermediate goods, used goods), or it is not production at all (stocks, bonds, transfers). Foreign-produced goods are out because GDP is domestic.

This is the most examined idea in the module. The sorter below has twelve cases; do it until you can give the reason as well as the answer.

Watch out. The service of selling a used good is new production. The used car is out; the dealer’s commission for selling it is in.

Textbook

Six transactions. Your textbook’s free-response question: a new plant (in), a used airplane (out), an existing share (out), an export (in), an import (out), unsold books into inventory (in).

Numeric

A second-hand sale. A used car sells for $8,000 and the dealer’s commission is $500. GDP rises by $500, not $8,000.

Illustrative

The College Board’s own question. Of stocks and bonds, changes in inventories, product quality, the underground economy and nonmarket activities, only changes in inventories are counted. The last three are the next topic: things GDP misses.

How it connects. It applies the definition of GDP (entry 7) and the final/intermediate distinction (entry 6), and it depends on the treatment of inventories, transfers and imports (entries 10–12).

Nominal GDP

★ ⚑
Definition

GDP measured at the prices of the year in which the output was produced — current prices. Every GDP figure in this module is nominal GDP.

Not in this module. Your textbook introduces the word on page 112, in Module 11.

⚑ Learn the name from here. Your framework asks you to calculate nominal GDP in this topic, but the word nominal does not appear in Module 10. Everything this module calculates is nominal GDP — learn the name now.

To calculate nominal GDP, multiply each final good’s quantity by this year’s price and add them up. That is what Figures 10.3 and 10.4 do.

The problem nominal GDP has is that it rises when prices rise, even if nothing more is produced. Separating the two is real GDP, which is the next module.

Watch out. A rise in nominal GDP does not mean the economy produced more. It may only mean prices went up.

Textbook

The 2009 figure. $14,259 billion is nominal: 2009 output valued at 2009 prices.

Numeric

Two goods. 100 pizzas at $10 and 50 books at $20: nominal GDP = 1,000 + 1,000 = $2,000.

Illustrative

Prices double. Next year the same 100 pizzas and 50 books sell at $20 and $40. Nominal GDP is $4,000 — doubled — with exactly the same output.

How it connects. It is what GDP (entry 7) means until you are told otherwise, and it is the number all three approaches (entries 13, 14 and 8) produce.

Check yourself

17.1 An economy produces 20 laptops at $500 and 100 coffees at $3. What is nominal GDP?

Show answer

20 × $500 + 100 × $3 = $10,000 + $300 = $10,300.

17.2 Nominal GDP rose 5% last year. Can you tell whether output rose?

Show answer

No. Prices might have risen by 5% with no change in output. You need real GDP to tell.

Tackle the test — free response

Question 1 (6 points). Will each of the following transactions be included in GDP for the United States? Explain why or why not. (a) Coca-Cola builds a new bottling plant in the United States. (b) Delta sells one of its existing airplanes to Korean Air. (c) Ms. Moneybags buys an existing share of Disney stock. (d) A California winery produces a bottle of Chardonnay and sells it to a customer in Montreal, Canada. (e) An American buys a bottle of French perfume in Tulsa. (f) A book publisher produces too many copies of a new book; the books don’t sell this year, so the publisher adds the surplus books to inventories.

Show the answer and the rubric

One point each:

  1. Yes. New structures built in the United States are included in U.S. GDP.
  2. No. The airplane is used, and sales of used goods are not included.
  3. No. A transfer of ownership, not new production.
  4. Yes. An export.
  5. No. An import — it was not produced in the United States.
  6. Yes. Additions to inventories are counted as investment.

Each point needs the reason, not just yes or no. The reasons are the six rules of entry 16.

The six things to get right from Module 10

  1. Final output, counted once. Total sales are not GDP; value added is how you avoid double counting.
  2. Know the three approaches by name — expenditures, income, value added — even though your textbook never uses the names.
  3. GDP = C + I + G + (X − IM). Imports are subtracted because they were already counted in C, I and G.
  4. Investment means new physical capital and changes in inventories — not buying shares.
  5. Transfers are not G. Nothing is produced in exchange.
  6. Say “nominal”. Every GDP in this module is at current prices; real GDP is next.

One more thing, for later in the course. C, I, G and X − IM come back in the next unit as the four parts of aggregate demand, and the financial-markets box in the expanded circular flow becomes the market for loanable funds. Learn the equation properly here and you will use it in almost every unit that follows.
