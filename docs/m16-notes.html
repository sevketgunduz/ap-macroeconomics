The headline. Half of Topic 3.2 is here and taught well: the MPC, the MPS, MPC + MPS = 1, the chain of spending rounds and the multiplier 1/(1 − MPC), with a worked $100 billion example. The other half is missing: the tax multiplier — named in MOD-2.B.3 and occurring 0 times in all 927 pages, with no formula anywhere — the framework's own name expenditure multiplier (0 in the book), the multiplier as a shift in aggregate demand (0 times in the module), and the balanced change in spending and taxes that the CED's only sample question on this topic tests. The guide supplies all of it. The module's other half — consumption function, investment and inventories — is more than Topic 3.2 asks and is graded as Topic 3.1 content or background.

Krugman's five stated objectives (p. 158):

  1. The nature of the multiplier, which shows how initial changes in spending lead to further changes
  2. The meaning of the aggregate consumption function, which shows how current disposable income affects

consumer spending

  1. How expected future income and aggregate wealth affect consumer spending
  2. The determinants of investment spending
  3. Why investment spending is considered a leading indicator of the future state of the economy

Only objective 1 is Topic 3.2. Objectives 3 and 4 are the causes of the shifts in MOD-2.A.3; objectives 2 and 5 are not named anywhere in the framework (consumption function 0 times in the Macro CED, leading indicator 0).

The essential knowledge statements, verbatim (CED p. 61):

MOD-2.B.1 — A $1 change to autonomous expenditures leads to further changes in total expenditures and total output. MOD-2.B.2 — The expenditure multiplier quantifies the size of the change in aggregate demand as a result of a change in any of the components of aggregate demand. MOD-2.B.3 — The tax multiplier quantifies the size of the change in aggregate demand as a result of a change in taxes. MOD-2.B.4 — The expenditure multiplier and tax multiplier depend on the marginal propensity to consume. MOD-2.B.5 — The marginal propensity to consume is the change in consumer spending divided by the change in disposable income. The sum of the marginal propensity to consume and marginal propensity to save is equal to one.

Three features of that wording matter.

The learning objective says spending and taxes three times; the module never has a tax. Krugman's assumption 3 (p. 158) is "no government spending and no taxes", and the formula it derives, 1/(1 − MPC), is the only multiplier in the book. Tax multiplier occurs 10 times in the Macro CED (pp. 61, 67, and the Course at a Glance) and 0 times in all 927 pages. Module 21 (pp. 210–211) works a transfer through the rounds in Table 21.1 and says a tax cut works the same way, but never writes −MPC/(1 − MPC).

MOD-2.B.2 and MOD-2.B.3 measure the multiplier as a change in aggregate demand; the learning objective measures it as a change in real GDP. The two are equal only when the price level does not move — which is exactly Krugman's assumption 1 (p. 158, producers supply more at a fixed price). The CED's sample MCQ 5 reconciles them with the phrase "a maximum of". Teach the multiplier as the size of the AD shift, and the change in real GDP as its upper limit.

POL-1.A.4 — "The government spending multiplier is greater than the tax multiplier" (CED p. 67) is Topic 3.8, but it cannot be separated from MOD-2.B.3 and sample MCQ 5 needs it. It is taught here.

5. Corrections to make explicitly in class

The MPC is a ratio of changes, not of totals

Point A in Figure 16.1 spends $42,659 out of $46,936, a ratio of 0.91; the MPC the fitted line gives is 0.53. Students who divide total spending by total income get the first number and call it the MPC.

Divide by 1 − MPC, not by the MPC

With an MPC of 0.8 the multiplier is 1 ÷ 0.2 = 5, not 1 ÷ 0.8 = 1.25. The commonest single error on the topic, and test distractor families are built on it.

The multiplier gives the shift in AD — real GDP rises by that much at most

Krugman fixes the price level (assumption 1, p. 158) and says it will be relaxed. The framework measures the multiplier in aggregate demand (MOD-2.B.2) and the exam writes "a maximum of". Once SRAS slopes up, part of the AD shift is absorbed by prices.

A tax cut's first round is MPC × the cut

The commonest tax-multiplier error is to treat a $10bn tax cut as $10bn of spending. It is $10bn of disposable income, of which MPC × $10bn is spent. That is the whole reason the tax multiplier is smaller.

A balanced change is not neutral

Equal increases in spending and taxes raise real GDP by their own amount. Students expect zero, or the spending multiplier minus nothing. Sample MCQ 5's distractors (A, B, D, E) are exactly these errors.

Wealth shifts spending; the price level's effect on real wealth does not

Krugman's wealth (p. 166 — a stock boom, a fall in house prices) is an autonomous change and shifts AD. The real wealth effect of MOD-2.A.2 is a change in the price level and moves along AD. Module 17 will teach the second; head off the confusion here.

Teach as a pair, never singly.

  • MPC / MPS — two halves of the extra dollar
  • Autonomous / induced spending — the cause, and the rounds the multiplier already counts
  • Expenditure multiplier / tax multiplier — the whole change in round 1 against MPC × the change
  • Shift in AD / change in real GDP — equal only at a fixed price level
  • Wealth shifting spending / the real wealth effect — a shift against a movement along AD
  • Planned / actual investment — what firms choose against what happens to them
