Interpreting Real GDP

Fifteen terms from Module 11 of Krugman’s Economics for AP: what separates nominal GDP from real GDP, how to calculate one from the other, and what GDP cannot tell you. This is a calculation module — almost every exam question on it has a number in it — and five of its entries are things your textbook does not give you.

Five formulas carry this module. Real GDP = this year’s quantities × base-year prices. Deflator = nominal ÷ real × 100. Real = nominal ÷ deflator × 100. Growth = (new − old) ÷ old × 100. Per capita = real GDP ÷ population. The one-page summary at the end has them together.

The 15 terms

01

Aggregate output

★ Exam
Definition

The total quantity of final goods and services an economy produces.

Textbook page 113, margin.

This is the thing both kinds of GDP are trying to measure. It is a quantity — apples, haircuts, cars — not a sum of money. You cannot add apples to haircuts, so to get one number you have to value everything at some prices. Which prices you choose is the whole difference between nominal and real GDP.

Later in the course, aggregate output goes on the horizontal axis of the main diagram of Unit 3, and your textbook will use real GDP and aggregate output to mean the same thing.

Watch out. A rise in GDP is not automatically a rise in aggregate output. GDP is a value; aggregate output is a quantity. They move together only when prices stay still.

Textbook

Portugal, 1974–75. Output was falling, yet GDP measured in escudos rose 11%. The value went up while the quantity went down.

Numeric

The apples-and-oranges economy. Apple output rose from 2,000 to 2,200 billion (10%) and orange output from 1,000 to 1,200 billion (20%). Those are the changes in aggregate output. Everything else in the table is prices.

Illustrative

A bakery. It bakes 500 loaves this year and 500 next year, but charges more next year. Its output is unchanged; the money it takes in is not.

How it connects. Nominal GDP (entry 2) and real GDP (entry 3) are two ways of putting a single dollar figure on it.

Check yourself

1.1 An economy produces the same quantity of everything in two years, and every price doubles. What happens to aggregate output?

Show answer

Nothing. Aggregate output is a quantity, and no quantity changed. Only the value of the output doubled.

1.2 Why do you need prices at all to measure aggregate output?

Show answer

Because you cannot add different goods together as quantities. Valuing each one at a price turns them into dollars, which can be added.

02

Nominal GDP

★ Exam
Definition

The value of all final goods and services produced in a year, calculated at the prices current in that year. It measures how much is spent on output.

Textbook page 114, margin; also page 112.

Nominal GDP is the GDP you met in Module 10: each year’s quantities multiplied by that same year’s prices, then added up. Economists also call it GDP at current prices.

Learn the one-line version as well as the calculation: nominal GDP is how much is spent on output. That sentence is how your exam defines it, and it tells you immediately why nominal GDP can rise with no extra output — people can spend more on the same things.

Watch out. Nominal GDP moves with two things: quantities and prices. If one rises and the other falls, nominal GDP can go either way.

Textbook

The United States, 1994–2009. GDP roughly doubled, from $7,085 billion to $14,259 billion. Production did not double — part of the rise was prices.

Numeric

Table 11.1. Year 1: (2,000 × $0.25) + (1,000 × $0.50) = $1,000 billion. Year 2: (2,200 × $0.30) + (1,200 × $0.70) = $1,500 billion, a rise of 50%.

Illustrative

A café. It sells 100 coffees at $3 this year ($300 spent) and 100 at $4 next year ($400 spent). Nominal output is up a third; not one extra coffee was made.

How it connects. It values aggregate output (entry 1) at current prices. It is the starting point for real GDP (entry 3), and the numerator of the GDP deflator (entry 8).

Check yourself

2.1 In the café example, by what percentage did nominal GDP rise, and by how much did output rise?

Show answer

Nominal GDP rose from $300 to $400, which is 33.3%. Output did not rise at all — it was 100 coffees both years.

2.2 Complete the sentence your exam uses: nominal GDP is a measure of how much is …

Show answer

… spent on output.

03

Real GDP

★ Exam
Definition

The value of all final goods and services produced in a year, calculated using the prices of a base year. It measures how much is produced.

Textbook page 114, margin.

Real GDP asks one question: how much would GDP have changed if prices had not changed? To answer it you value every year’s output at the same set of prices — the prices of one chosen year — so that the only thing left to change is the quantities.

Your exam puts it in one line: real GDP is how much is produced. It is also described as GDP at constant prices, because the prices are held fixed. Holding them fixed is what removes the effect of changes in the overall price level.

Watch out. Real GDP is still measured in dollars — but in the dollars of the base year. A real GDP figure always comes with its base year attached: “in 2017 dollars”.

Textbook

Table 11.2. U.S. GDP in 2009 was $14,259 billion nominal but $12,989 billion in 2005 dollars. Prices were higher in 2009 than in 2005, so taking the price effect out makes the figure smaller.

Numeric

Table 11.1, Year 1 prices. Year 2 output at Year 1 prices: (2,200 × $0.25) + (1,200 × $0.50) = $1,150 billion, against $1,000 billion in Year 1.

Illustrative

The café again. At this year’s $3 price, next year’s 100 coffees are worth $300 — exactly this year’s figure. Real GDP did not change, which is the truth about the café.

How it connects. It is nominal GDP (entry 2) with the price effect removed, so it tracks aggregate output (entry 1) itself. Entry 4 is why it is needed; the base year (entry 5) is what it is measured in.

Check yourself

3.1 Real GDP rose from one year to the next. Does that mean aggregate output rose?

Show answer

Yes. Real GDP holds prices constant, so the only way it can rise is if quantities rose.

3.2 Give the exam’s one-line definitions of nominal and real GDP.

Show answer

Nominal GDP is a measure of how much is spent on output. Real GDP is a measure of how much is produced.

04

Why nominal GDP misleads over time

★ Exam
Definition

Nominal GDP can rise because output rises or because prices rise. Over time, only real GDP tells you which.

Textbook pages 112–113, and the Venezuela box on page 116.

For one year, nominal GDP is a good measure of the size of an economy. For comparing years, it is not, because prices change between them. With inflation, nominal GDP overstates growth. With falling prices, it understates it.

The rule to learn: nominal moves with output and prices; real moves with output only. Once you know which way prices went, you know which way the gap goes.

Watch out. Do not simply subtract the inflation rate from nominal growth. It is a rough shortcut that breaks down for large numbers. Convert properly (entry 9).

Textbook

Venezuela, 1997–2007. Nominal GDP grew about 28% a year — faster than China’s. Real GDP grew 2.9% a year. It was not a miracle; it was inflation.

Numeric

Portugal-style. Suppose nominal GDP rises 11% while prices rise 15%. Real GDP changes by 1.11 ÷ 1.15 = 0.965, a fall of about 3.5%.

Illustrative

Falling prices. If output rises 2% and prices fall 5%, nominal GDP changes by 1.02 × 0.95 = 0.969 — it falls about 3.1% while output rises.

How it connects. The fix is real GDP (entry 3), calculated in entry 6 or converted with the deflator in entry 9.

Check yourself

4.1 Nominal GDP rose 11% and the price level rose 15%. Did the economy produce more or less?

Show answer

Less. Spending rose more slowly than prices, so the quantity bought fell — by about 3.5%.

4.2 Can nominal GDP ever understate growth?

Show answer

Yes, when the price level falls. Then nominal GDP grows more slowly than output, or even falls while output rises.

05

The base year

★ Exam
Definition

The year whose prices are used to calculate real GDP. In the base year, real GDP equals nominal GDP.

Textbook page 114.

In the base year, “base-year prices” and “current prices” are the same prices, so the two calculations give the same number. That is the quickest check you have that you set a calculation up correctly.

It also fixes the GDP deflator (entry 8) at exactly 100 in the base year. Before the base year, when prices were usually lower, real GDP is above nominal. After it, below.

Watch out. Your textbook’s Table 11.2 prints 2005 nominal GDP as 12,683 and 2005 real GDP as 12,638 — but 2005 is the base year, and the text above the table says they are the same. Two digits have been swapped in one cell. Trust the rule, not the misprint.

Textbook

Table 11.2, 2005 dollars. Real GDP is above nominal in 2001 ($11,347 bn against $10,286 bn) and below it in 2009, because prices in 2001 were lower than in the base year and prices in 2009 higher.

Numeric

Table 11.1, Year 1 as base. Year 1 real GDP = Year 1 nominal GDP = $1,000 billion, and the deflator is 100.

Illustrative

Official statistics. U.S. real GDP is currently reported in “chained 2017 dollars”. The year in the label is the base.

How it connects. It is what real GDP (entry 3) is measured in. Choosing a different one changes measured growth — entry 10.

Check yourself

5.1 Real GDP for 2017 is reported in 2017 dollars. Nominal GDP in 2017 was $19.5 trillion. What was real GDP in 2017?

Show answer

$19.5 trillion. In the base year the two are equal.

5.2 Before the base year, is real GDP usually above or below nominal GDP? Why?

Show answer

Above. Prices were usually lower before the base year, so valuing that output at the higher base-year prices gives a bigger number.

06

Calculating real GDP

★ Exam
Definition

Multiply this year’s quantities by the base year’s prices, and add them up.

Textbook pages 113–114, Table 11.1.

Three steps, and the second is the only one people get wrong.

1. Take the quantities of the year you want. 2. Multiply each by its price in the base year — not the current year. 3. Add. If the year you want is the base year, you have just calculated nominal GDP, which is correct.

Watch out. Keep the quantities from the year you are measuring and change only the prices. Swapping which year the quantities come from gives a number that means nothing.

Textbook

Table 11.1. Year 2 at Year 1 prices: (2,200 × $0.25) + (1,200 × $0.50) = $550 + $600 = $1,150 billion.

Numeric

Britannica (your AP Review, question 1). 2010 output at 2008 prices: ($900 × 12) + ($10 × 110) + ($15 × 3) = $11,945.

Illustrative

A school canteen. Year 1: 200 sandwiches at $4, 100 juices at $2. Year 2: 220 sandwiches at $5, 90 juices at $2.50. Real GDP in Year 2 at Year 1 prices = (220 × $4) + (90 × $2) = $1,060, against $1,000 in Year 1.

How it connects. It is the fix for entry 4’s problem, and it produces the two figures you need for the growth rate (entry 7) and the deflator (entry 8).

Check yourself

6.1 Using the canteen numbers, what is nominal GDP in Year 2?

Show answer

(220 × $5) + (90 × $2.50) = $1,100 + $225 = $1,325.

6.2 In Table 11.1, what do you get if you value Year 1 quantities at Year 2 prices?

Show answer

(2,000 × $0.30) + (1,000 × $0.70) = $1,300 billion — Year 1’s real GDP with Year 2 as the base. It is a legitimate number, but a different base year.

07

The growth rate of real GDP

★ Exam
Definition

(new − old) ÷ old × 100, using real GDP figures.

Textbook page 114.

The growth rate of real GDP is how economists say how much more an economy produced. Use real figures, or you measure prices as well as output.

In Table 11.1 nominal GDP grew 50% and real GDP 15%. The difference, 35 points, is not output at all.

Watch out. Always divide by the old value. Dividing the change by the new value is the commonest arithmetic slip in this unit.

Textbook

Table 11.1. Real GDP: ($1,150 − $1,000) ÷ $1,000 × 100 = 15%. Nominal: ($1,500 − $1,000) ÷ $1,000 × 100 = 50%.

Numeric

The canteen. Real GDP rose from $1,000 to $1,060: growth of 6%. Nominal GDP rose to $1,325: 32.5%. Most of the nominal rise was prices.

Illustrative

Venezuela. Real growth of 2.9% a year over a decade in which nominal GDP grew 28% a year. Only the first number is growth.

How it connects. It needs two real GDP figures from entry 6. And it depends on the base year chosen — entry 10.

Check yourself

7.1 Real GDP rises from $400 to $480. What is the growth rate? What do you get if you wrongly divide by the new value?

Show answer

(480 − 400) ÷ 400 = 20%. Dividing by 480 gives 16.7%, which is wrong.

7.2 Nominal GDP grew 50% but real GDP grew 15%. What accounts for the difference?

Show answer

Higher prices. Real GDP removes the effect of the price level; nominal GDP does not.

08

The GDP deflator

★ Exam

⚑ Not in your textbook’s Module 11 — learn it from here. Your exam asks you to calculate it alongside real GDP. The textbook does not define it until Module 15 (page 146).

Definition

A price index for everything in GDP: nominal GDP ÷ real GDP × 100. It is 100 in the base year.

Not in Module 11. Defined in Module 15, page 146.

If nominal GDP is output at today’s prices and real GDP is the same output at base-year prices, their ratio tells you how much higher today’s prices are. Multiply by 100 and you have the GDP deflator.

A deflator of 130 means the prices of the economy’s final goods and services are, on average, 30% above their base-year level. And because it is a price index, the percentage change in the deflator from one year to the next is an inflation rate.

Watch out. Do not forget the ×100. Nominal ÷ real gives 1.304; the deflator is 130.4.

Textbook

Table 11.2. 2009: 14,259 ÷ 12,989 × 100 = 109.8. 2001: 10,286 ÷ 11,347 × 100 = 90.6. Above 100 after the base year, below it before.

Numeric

Table 11.1, Year 1 base. Year 2: 1,500 ÷ 1,150 × 100 = 130.4. Prices rose about 30.4% on average.

Illustrative

Fehran, from your exam board’s sample question. The deflator went from 100 to 125, so the inflation rate was (125 − 100) ÷ 100 × 100 = 25%.

How it connects. Its top line is nominal GDP (entry 2) and its bottom line real GDP from entry 6. Rearrange it and you can turn nominal GDP into real GDP — entry 9.

Check yourself

8.1 Nominal GDP is $2,400 billion and real GDP is $2,000 billion. What is the GDP deflator?

Show answer

2,400 ÷ 2,000 × 100 = 120.

8.2 The deflator rises from 110 to 121. What is the inflation rate?

Show answer

(121 − 110) ÷ 110 × 100 = 10%. Not 11% — the change is 11 points, but the starting value is 110, not 100.

09

Converting nominal GDP to real GDP

★ Exam

⚑ Not in your textbook — learn it from here. This exact calculation is the first point of a published sample exam question, and your textbook never writes it out.

Definition

Real GDP = nominal GDP ÷ GDP deflator × 100.

Not in the textbook as a procedure. It is entry 8 rearranged.

This is the deflator’s definition turned round. If you know how much was spent and how much higher prices are than in the base year, dividing takes the price effect out and leaves the quantity, in base-year dollars.

A quick sense-check: when the deflator is above 100, real GDP must come out smaller than nominal; below 100, larger. If your answer goes the wrong way, you multiplied instead of dividing.

Watch out. Divide by the deflator, then multiply by 100. Multiplying by the deflator adds inflation instead of removing it.

Textbook

Your AP Review, question 2, Country B. 2010 nominal GDP $6,000 with a price level of 200: $6,000 ÷ 200 × 100 = $3,000 in 2005 prices.

Numeric

Fehran, 2012. $225,000 ÷ 125 × 100 = $180,000. This is the scored answer to the published question.

Illustrative

Comparing two years. Nominal GDP rises from $11,000 bn (deflator 110) to $13,200 bn (deflator 120). Real GDP: $10,000 bn, then $11,000 bn — real growth of exactly 10%, while nominal growth was 20%.

How it connects. It is entry 8 rearranged, and the proper answer to entry 4. Real GDP divided by population gives real GDP per capita (entry 12), which is how living standards are compared (entry 13).

Check yourself

9.1 Nominal GDP is $450 billion and the deflator is 125. What is real GDP?

Show answer

$450 ÷ 125 × 100 = $360 billion.

9.2 A student converts $450 billion with a deflator of 125 and gets $562.5 billion. What went wrong, and how could they have spotted it?

Show answer

They multiplied by 1.25 instead of dividing. With a deflator above 100, real GDP must be below nominal, so an answer above $450 billion is impossible.

10

Why a fixed base year can overstate growth

★ Exam

⚑ Not in your textbook — learn it from here. Your textbook shows that two base years give two answers. Your exam also says which way the error usually runs.

Definition

Valuing output at old prices gives too much weight to goods whose prices have fallen — usually the goods whose output has grown fastest — and so overstates real GDP growth.

Textbook page 115 shows the two base years. The direction is not in the textbook.

Real GDP needs one set of prices, but the choice matters. Each good counts in proportion to its price, so the base year decides how much each good’s growth contributes.

In real economies the goods whose output grows fastest are usually the ones technology is making cheaper — computers, phones, solar panels. An early base year values each of them at its old, high price, so their growth counts for too much. That is the overstatement, and it is why statistical agencies do not use one fixed base year for ever.

Watch out. This is about relative prices, not inflation. Both calculations hold all prices constant; they differ only in which year’s prices they hold.

Numeric

Computers and bread (the chart above). Computers go from 10 at $2,000 to 20 at $1,000; bread stays at 1,000 loaves while its price rises from $5 to $10. At Year 1 prices real GDP grows 80%; at Year 2 prices, 50%.

Textbook

Apples and oranges, both ways. Year 1 prices give 15%, Year 2 prices 15.4%. Here the early base gives the lower figure, because oranges got dearer and also grew faster — the unusual case. Your textbook concludes that neither answer is more correct.

Illustrative

Computing power since 1990. Its price has fallen by more than 90% while its output has multiplied. Measured at 1990 prices, it would dominate growth figures far beyond its real importance.

How it connects. It is a problem with choosing a base year (entry 5), and it shows up in growth rates (entry 7). What statistical agencies do instead is chain-linking (entry 11).

Check yourself

10.1 An economy’s fastest-growing good is one whose price is falling. Will an early base year give a higher or a lower growth rate than a late one?

Show answer

Higher. The early base year values the fast-growing good at its old, higher price, giving its growth more weight.

10.2 Does this problem mean real GDP still contains inflation?

Show answer

No. Each calculation holds all prices constant. The problem is which year’s relative prices are used as weights.

11

Chain-linking

○ Background
Definition

Calculating real GDP growth as the average of the growth rate using an early base year and the growth rate using a late base year.

Textbook page 115, margin.

This is how your textbook describes the method U.S. statisticians use, and why U.S. real GDP is reported in “chained dollars”. It splits the difference between an early and a late base year.

Your exam does not ask you to chain-link anything. What it does expect you to know is the reason for it: a single fixed base year can overstate growth, so agencies use other methods (entry 10).

Watch out. The average is a method, not the “true” growth rate. Neither base year is right; chain-linking is a compromise between them.

Textbook

Apples and oranges. (15% + 15.4%) ÷ 2 ≈ 15.2%.

Numeric

Computers and bread. (80% + 50%) ÷ 2 = 65% — between the two, and a long way below the early base year’s figure.

Illustrative

The label on official figures. “Real GDP, billions of chained 2017 dollars” means a chain-linked series whose reference year is 2017.

How it connects. It is the agencies’ answer to entry 10.

Check yourself

11.1 Two base years give growth of 15% and 15.4%. What does chain-linking report?

Show answer

About 15.2%, the average of the two.

11.2 Do you need to perform chain-linking on the exam?

Show answer

No. You need to know why agencies do not use one fixed base year — that is entry 10.

12

GDP per capita and real GDP per capita

★ Exam
Definition

GDP divided by the population — the average GDP per person. Real GDP per capita is real GDP ÷ population.

Textbook page 115, margin.

A country with more people will usually have a bigger GDP simply because more people are working. To compare output per person, divide by the population. Use real GDP when you compare over time, for the same reasons as entry 4.

Keep the two questions apart. Which economy is bigger? Compare GDP. Where does the average person have more output? Compare GDP per capita. They often have different answers.

Watch out. Check the units. $840 billion ÷ 60 million is $14,000, not $14. Write the zeros out if you need to.

Textbook

2009 GDP. The United States $14,259 billion, Japan $5,049 billion, the European Union $16,191 billion. That tells you the EU’s economy is the largest — and nothing about who is better off, because it gives no populations.

Numeric

Your AP Review, question 2, Country A in 2010. Real GDP $4,000 and a population of 20: $4,000 ÷ 20 = $200 per person.

Illustrative

A big economy and a small one. Real GDP of $2,000 billion shared among 1,000 million people is $2,000 each; $400 billion among 8 million is $50,000 each. The first economy is five times bigger.

How it connects. It divides the real GDP of entry 9 by population, and it is what you use to compare living standards (entry 13).

Check yourself

12.1 Country Z has real GDP of $840 billion and 60 million people. What is its real GDP per capita?

Show answer

840,000,000,000 ÷ 60,000,000 = $14,000.

12.2 Country P has three times Country Q’s GDP. Does P have the higher GDP per capita?

Show answer

Not necessarily. It depends on the populations. P could have many more people and a lower figure per person.

13

Real GDP per capita and the standard of living

★ Exam
Definition

When a question asks whether the average person’s standard of living rose, fell or stayed the same, compare real GDP per capita.

Textbook page 115.

Your textbook calls real GDP per capita “a rough measure of the average real output per person” — of what a country can do. On the exam it is the measure you compute when you are asked about living standards from data.

The method is two steps you already have: convert nominal to real (entry 9), then divide by population (entry 12). Compare the two years. A rise in real GDP is not enough, because the population may have grown just as fast.

Watch out. Use real, not nominal, GDP per person. Nominal GDP per person rises with inflation even when nobody has more.

Textbook

Your AP Review, question 2. Country A’s real GDP doubled and Country B’s rose 50%, but real GDP per capita in 2010 is $200 in both, exactly where it started.

Numeric

Fehran. Real GDP per capita: $150,000 ÷ 100 = $1,500 in 2011; $180,000 ÷ 120 = $1,500 in 2012. The standard of living stayed the same, which is the scored answer.

Illustrative

A growing town. Output rises 5% in a year while the population rises 5%. Real GDP is up; the average resident has exactly as much as before.

How it connects. It is entry 12 applied to two years. What this measure cannot capture is entries 14 and 15.

Check yourself

13.1 Real GDP rose 20% and the population rose 20%. What happened to the average standard of living?

Show answer

It stayed the same. Real GDP per capita did not change.

13.2 Why is nominal GDP per capita the wrong measure?

Show answer

Because it rises with prices. Inflation alone would make it look as though living standards rose.

14

Nonmarket transactions

★ Exam

⚑ The word is not in your textbook — learn it from here. Your textbook describes the idea (housework, volunteering) without using the name. Your exam uses the name: it is the one limitation of GDP it spells out.

Definition

Production that is not bought and sold in a market — housework, caring for your own children, volunteering. GDP does not count it.

Textbook page 115, in substance.

GDP adds up the market value of output, so something that is never sold has no market value to add. The meal you cook at home is production; the same meal cooked in a restaurant is production and part of GDP.

The consequence cuts both ways. When work moves out of the market — a family stops paying a cleaner and cleans itself — measured GDP falls although nothing less is done. When it moves into the market, measured GDP rises although nothing more is done.

Watch out. “Not counted” does not mean “not produced”. Nonmarket work is real output; it is just invisible to GDP.

Textbook

Page 115. Real GDP does not include housework or volunteerism. Both are nonmarket transactions.

Numeric

A childminder. A family pays a childminder $12,000 a year, then one parent stays home and does the same care unpaid. Measured GDP falls by $12,000; the amount of childcare does not change.

Illustrative

A vegetable garden. Growing your own vegetables instead of buying them replaces market output with nonmarket output. The food is the same; measured GDP is lower.

How it connects. It is why real GDP per capita (entry 13) is only a rough guide to well-being. It is one of several limitations; entry 15 has the rest.

Check yourself

14.1 A retired engineer volunteers at a food bank every week. Is that work counted in GDP?

Show answer

No. It is a nonmarket transaction — nothing is sold.

14.2 Two neighbours start paying each other to mow each other’s lawns instead of mowing their own. What happens to measured GDP and to actual production?

Show answer

Measured GDP rises, because the mowing is now sold. Actual production is unchanged — two lawns, mowed.

15

The other limitations of GDP

★ Exam

⚑ Two of these are not in your textbook. The underground economy and changes in product quality appear in your exam board’s own sample question. Learn them from here.

Definition

GDP is a useful indicator, but it leaves out things that matter and counts things that do not make anyone better off.

Textbook page 115.

Your textbook’s list: real GDP does not include leisure time, volunteering, housework or natural beauty; it does rise with spending on things that make people unhappy — disease, divorce, crime, natural disasters; and it says nothing about how a country uses its output.

Add two more. The underground economy — work paid in cash and hidden from the authorities — is not recorded. And changes in product quality are not captured: a better phone at the same price adds nothing to GDP.

Watch out. A limitation is a reason GDP is an imperfect measure of well-being. It does not change how you answer a calculation question about living standards — for that, still compare real GDP per capita.

Textbook

Disasters. Rebuilding after a hurricane raises GDP, because new construction is counted. The destroyed homes are never subtracted. GDP rises; nobody is better off.

Numeric

Leisure. Two countries have the same real GDP per capita, but one works 1,600 hours a year and the other 2,000. The first produces 25% more per hour worked and has far more free time. GDP per capita shows them as equal.

Illustrative

The underground economy. A painter paid in cash who never reports the work produces real output that never appears in GDP.

How it connects. Each is a reason real GDP per capita (entry 13) falls short. The one limitation your exam names is nonmarket transactions (entry 14).

Check yourself

15.1 A hurricane destroys homes and the rebuilding raises real GDP. Is the country better off?

Show answer

No. GDP counts the rebuilding but never subtracts the destruction, so it rises while the country is only getting back to where it was.

15.2 Name two things GDP leaves out that your textbook does not mention.

Show answer

The underground economy, and changes in product quality.

The five questions from your textbook’s AP Review, with every option explained.

Question 1 (5 points). Britannica produces computers, DVDs and pizza. In 2008: computers $900 × 10, DVDs $10 × 100, pizza $15 × 2. In 2009: $1,000 × 10.5, $12 × 105, $16 × 2. In 2010: $1,050 × 12, $14 × 110, $17 × 3. (a) Percent change in computer production 2008–09? (b) Percent change in the price of pizza 2009–10? (c) Nominal GDP in 2008? (d) Real GDP in 2008, 2008 base? (e) Real GDP in 2010, 2008 base?

Show the answer and the rubric
  1. (a) (10.5 − 10) ÷ 10 × 100 = 5%
  2. (b) ($17 − $16) ÷ $16 × 100 = 6.25%
  3. (c) ($900 × 10) + ($10 × 100) + ($15 × 2) = $10,030
  4. (d) $10,030 again — in the base year, real GDP equals nominal GDP
  5. (e) ($900 × 12) + ($10 × 110) + ($15 × 3) = $11,945

One point each. Part (e) is entry 6: 2010 quantities, 2008 prices.

Question 2. Country A: nominal GDP $2,000, price level 100, population 10 in 2005; $4,000, 100 and 20 in 2010. Country B: $2,000, 100 and 10 in 2005; $6,000, 200 and 15 in 2010. (a) Percent increase in nominal GDP for each? (b) What happened to the price level? (c) Real GDP in 2010, 2005 base? (d) Percent increase in real GDP? (e) Compare real GDP per capita in 2010.

Show the answer

(a) A: 100%. B: 200%.

(b) A: unchanged. B: it doubled, from 100 to 200.

(c) A: $4,000 ÷ 100 × 100 = $4,000. B: $6,000 ÷ 200 × 100 = $3,000. That is entry 9.

(d) A: 100%. B: 50%.

(e) A: $4,000 ÷ 20 = $200. B: $3,000 ÷ 15 = $200. The same — and the same as in 2005. B’s nominal GDP tripled and its average resident has no more than before.

Question 3 — the one your exam board published. The table shows statistics for the country of Fehran: in 2011, nominal GDP $150,000, population 100, GDP deflator 100; in 2012, nominal GDP $225,000, population 120, GDP deflator 125. (a) Calculate (i) Fehran’s real GDP in 2012 and (ii) the inflation rate from 2011 to 2012, showing your work. (c) Did the standard of living of the average person in Fehran increase, decrease or stay the same between 2011 and 2012? Explain.

Show the scored answers

(a)(i) ($225,000 ÷ 125) × 100 = $180,000. One point.

(a)(ii) (125 − 100) ÷ 100 × 100 = 25%. One point.

(c) It stayed the same, because real GDP per capita did not change: $1,500 in 2011 ($150,000 ÷ 100) and $1,500 in 2012 ($180,000 ÷ 120). One point.

Nominal GDP is how much is spent; real GDP is how much is produced. GDP leaves out nonmarket transactions, leisure, the underground economy and quality changes, and it counts spending on disasters, crime and disease.

The five things to get right from Module 11

  1. Nominal is spent, real is produced. Nominal moves with output and prices; real moves with output only.
  2. Real GDP keeps this year’s quantities and changes only the prices — to the base year’s. In the base year, real equals nominal and the deflator is 100.
  3. Divide by the deflator, then multiply by 100. If the deflator is above 100, real GDP must come out smaller than nominal.
  4. Living standards means real GDP per capita. Convert first, then divide by population, then compare.
  5. Say “nonmarket transactions”. It is the one limitation your exam names, and the word is not in your textbook.

Everything in this module comes back. Real GDP goes on the horizontal axis of the aggregate demand and aggregate supply diagram in Unit 3, the GDP deflator goes on its vertical axis as the price level, and real GDP per capita becomes the definition of economic growth in Unit 5.
