1. Business cycle

Definition. "The business cycle is the short-run alternation between economic downturns, known as recessions, and economic upturns, known as expansions." (Krugman, p. 10)

CED anchor. MEA-2.A.1 — "Business cycles are fluctuations in aggregate output and employment because of changes in aggregate supply and/or aggregate demand." MEA-2.A.2 — "The phases of a business cycle are recession and expansion." (Topic 2.7, CED p. 53)

Where taught. p. 10.

Explanation

Two words carry the definition. Short-run locates it: months and years, not decades, and deliberately not the same time frame as economic growth. Alternation describes it: it goes back and forth between two states, and that is all "cycle" is claiming.

The cycle is the organising idea of the whole course. Units 3 and 5 are, between them, an account of why it happens and what policy can do about it, and both assume a student who already knows the vocabulary. That is what Module 2 is for.

Krugman gives the U.S. record since World War II: 11 recessions, an average recession of 10 months, an average expansion of 57 months, an average full cycle of 5 years 7 months, shortest 18 months, longest 10 years 8 months. The numbers are worth one minute, for the range rather than the averages.

The framework's causal clause — because of changes in aggregate supply and/or aggregate demand — is not in this module. Aggregate demand and aggregate supply occur 0 times in Module 2 and 357 and 275 times in the book, from Module 17. Name the gap in class or students conclude that cycles are uncaused.

Common error. Reading "cycle" as "regular". The phases are nothing like equal, nobody can date the next turn, and an average built from an 18-month cycle and a 128-month one predicts neither.

Examples

1. From the text. The Great Depression, beginning in 1929. Shantytowns called "Hoovervilles" grew across America, one of them in Central Park. Macroeconomics exists as a field because that downturn could not be explained by the economics of the time. (p. 10)

2. Numeric. 10 + 57 = 67 months, which is 5 years 7 months — the stated average cycle. The shortest was 18 months and the longest 128. The average is arithmetically correct and predictively useless, which is the point to make with it.

3. Illustrative. A large employer in one town cuts 400 jobs and the shops, restaurants and builders all slow within months. What makes it macroeconomic is the all at once — a single industry declining while others grow is a change in what people buy, not a cycle.

Figure. The cycle diagram in student_module_02.html: actual output waving around a rising potential-output trend, both recessions shaded, peaks and troughs marked. Every entry from 1 to 10 is a label on that one picture.

2. Recession

Definition. "Recessions are periods of economic downturns when output and employment are falling." (Krugman, p. 10)

CED anchor. MEA-2.A.2 — "The phases of a business cycle are recession and expansion." (Topic 2.7, CED p. 53)

Where taught. p. 10, with the FYI box on p. 11.

Explanation

The definition contains two variables and both are falling. Not growing slowly, not at a low level, and nothing about prices. That pairing is why a recession does damage: less produced, fewer people paid to produce it, so output and income fall together.

The FYI box on p. 11 is the most useful half-page in the module. Many countries use a rule of thumb — two consecutive quarters of falling aggregate output — and the United States does not. An independent panel at the National Bureau of Economic Research looks at several indicators, mainly employment and production, and makes a judgement. Krugman's phrasing is worth quoting to a class: "there is no exact definition."

The 2001 dispute in the same box is better than any invented example. The NBER dated the recession March to November 2001; critics argued it began earlier, when industrial production turned down, and others that it never ended in 2001 because employment fell for another eighteen months. All three positions are defensible, which is exactly what "judgement call" means.

Common error. Treating "recession" as "the economy is doing badly". Output must be falling. An economy with 9% unemployment and rising output is in an expansion, and students find that counter-intuitive enough to get it wrong under time pressure.

Examples

1. From the text. The downturn that began in December 2007 pushed the unemployment rate into double digits — the deepest since the 1930s. (pp. 10, 12)

2. Numeric. Three months of sharply falling output, three of slightly positive growth, three more of rapid decline. The two-quarter rule finds no recession; nine of twelve months were bad. This is Krugman's own argument for why the U.S. uses a panel rather than a rule. (p. 11)

3. Illustrative. A national construction slowdown: firms stop hiring, then stop replacing leavers, then make redundancies. Output and employment fall together and neither leads the other by much.

Figure. The shaded bands in Figure 2.1 (p. 10), and the shaded bands in the student guide's cycle diagram — each running from a peak to the following trough.

3. Expansion (recovery)

Definition. "Expansions, or recoveries, are periods of economic upturns when output and employment are rising." (Krugman, p. 10)

CED anchor. MEA-2.A.2 — "The phases of a business cycle are recession and expansion." (Topic 2.7, CED p. 53)

Where taught. p. 10.

Explanation

The same two variables as a recession, moving the other way. An expansion runs from a trough to the next peak, which means it includes the early stretch when unemployment is still high and has only just begun to fall — the part that does not feel like a recovery to anyone living through it.

Everything a student needs here is in one sentence: an expansion is defined by direction, not level. Most exam errors on this term come from testing the level.

The asymmetry is worth a line on the board. Average postwar recession 10 months, average expansion 57 — economies spend roughly five and a half times as long climbing as falling.

Common error. Equating expansion with prosperity. The recession that began in 2007 had technically ended while unemployment was still near its worst. "Recovery" is also a trap late in a long expansion: once output has passed its old peak, the word has stopped describing anything.

Examples

1. From the text. The U.S. economy staged a partial recovery beginning in 1933, and joblessness stayed high throughout the 1930s. Rising is not the same as recovered. (p. 10)

2. Numeric. 57 ÷ 10 = 5.7. The average expansion is 5.7 times the length of the average recession, which is why a randomly chosen month is far more likely to be an expansion month.

3. Illustrative. The late-1990s boom pushed the unemployment rate to 4% or lower (p. 12) — the late, comfortable stage of an expansion, and the only stage most people notice.

4. Depression

Definition. "A depression is a very deep and prolonged downturn." (Krugman, p. 10)

CED anchor. None. The word depression appears 0 times in the AP Macroeconomics Course and Exam Description. It is not a phase of the business cycle in the framework's terms — MEA-2.A.2 names two phases, recession and expansion.

Where taught. p. 10.

Explanation

A depression is not a third phase. It is a recession that is much deeper and much longer, with no numerical threshold separating the two, which is why the word is used so sparingly. The United States has not had one since the 1930s.

Its value in this module is historical rather than technical. The Great Depression is the reason macroeconomics exists: before it there was no serious body of theory about the economy as a whole and no policy toolkit for a slump. Krugman opens the module with it deliberately.

Common error. Writing "depression" on an exam answer for a severe recession. The framework's word for the contraction phase is recession, whatever its depth. Reserve "depression" for the 1930s unless a question supplies the word first.

Examples

1. From the text. The Hoovervilles — shantytowns named after President Hoover that sprang up across America after the 1929 slump, including one in New York's Central Park. (p. 10)

2. Illustrative — the rarity. 11 recessions since World War II and no depressions: roughly eighty years without one.

3. Illustrative — the word in use. In 2008 commentators asked whether the downturn was "a depression". The question was about depth and duration, not about a different kind of event, and no data could have settled it because no threshold exists.

Figure. None. There is nothing to plot that entry 2's figure does not already show.

5. Peak and trough ★

Definition. Not in Krugman. Written here from the framework: the peak is the turning point at which an expansion ends and a recession begins — the highest level of output before the downturn. The trough is the turning point at which a recession ends and an expansion begins — the lowest level of output before the recovery.

CED anchor. MEA-2.A.3 — "The turning points of a business cycle are peak and trough." (Topic 2.7, CED p. 53)

Where taught. Nowhere. Trough occurs 0 times in the whole of Krugman. Peak occurs 27 times, every one in ordinary language — "demand for gasoline peaks in the summer", "years of peak earnings", "fallen more than 20% from their peak" — and never once as the turning point of a business cycle.

Explanation

This is the largest single gap in the module, and it is cheap to fix: four minutes with Figure 2.1 on screen.

Keep the grammar explicit, because it is the whole of the idea. Recessions and expansions are periods — stretches of time, with a length. Peaks and troughs are moments — single points at which the direction reverses. On a diagram the phases are segments of the curve and the turning points are dots. Exam diagrams ask for all four labels.

The names feel inverted to students, and that is worth naming rather than smoothing over: the peak is the moment the good news ends, and the trough the moment the bad news does. Both are named for the level of output, not for how anything feels. Unemployment typically keeps rising for some months after the trough.

Dating is also what makes the cycle measurable. A full cycle is peak to peak, or trough to trough, which is how Krugman's own "average length of a business cycle" figure on p. 10 was computed — though he never says so, having no words for the endpoints.

Common error. Believing the peak is where the economy is healthiest. Near a peak actual output is usually above potential output — that is precisely the unsustainable state that produces the downturn. See entry 10.

Examples

1. From the framework. MEA-2.A.3 in full: "The turning points of a business cycle are peak and trough." One sentence, and a diagram-labelling question can be built on it.

2. Numeric. A peak in December 2007 and the next trough in June 2009 give an 18-month recession. If the following peak is February 2020, the expansion ran 128 months. Peak-to-peak, that cycle is 146 months — 12 years 2 months.

3. Illustrative. The summit of a hill walk. You are at the top for an instant; the climb and the descent are the periods. Nobody asks how long you spent at the summit.

Figure. The dots on the cycle diagram in student_module_02.html; the Peak and Trough buttons isolate them.

6. Employment, unemployment and the labor force

Definition. "Employment is the number of people currently employed in the economy." "Unemployment is the number of people who are actively looking for work but aren't currently employed." "The labor force is equal to the sum of employment and unemployment." (Krugman, p. 12)

CED anchor. MEA-1.C.1 — "The unemployment rate is the percentage of the labor force that is out of work." The three quantities are the inputs that statement is built from; the learning objective MEA-1.C(a) requires students to "define the labor force". (Topic 2.3, CED p. 48)

Where taught. p. 12.

Explanation

Three definitions, one idea. Every adult falls into exactly one of three boxes: working, actively looking for work, or neither. The first two boxes together are the labor force; the third box — retired people, full-time students, people caring for family, and anyone who has stopped searching — is not in the labor force at all, and that third box is where nearly every error originates.

"Actively looking" is doing real work in the definition. Wanting a job is not enough. Someone who would take a job but has given up searching is counted outside the labor force, not as unemployed — which is the mechanism behind the discouraged-worker problem (MEA-1.D.1, CED p. 48), a required element that Module 2 does not mention and Module 12 does.

The framework needs these three quantities for two different rates with two different denominators. Get the boxes right here and entries 7 and 8 are arithmetic; get them wrong and both rates are wrong.

Common error. "The labor force is everyone old enough to work." It is not, and the module's own MCQ 2 is built on exactly that error — option B is old enough to work, option E is employed or capable of working. The labor force is the employed plus the actively searching.

Examples

1. From the text. Krugman introduces the three together and immediately uses the labor force as the base for judging the job market: employment and unemployment only mean something relative to it. (p. 12)

2. Numeric. 150 million employed, 10 million actively looking, 90 million retired or studying. Labor force = 150 + 10 = 160 million. Adult population = 250 million. The 90 million are in neither of the first two boxes and belong in neither rate's numerator.

3. Illustrative. Four people: a part-time waiter (employed — part-time counts), a graduate sending out applications (unemployed), a retired teacher (not in the labor force), a 15-year-old (not in the labor force, below working age).

Figure. None needed; the three-box sort is better done on a board than drawn.

7. Unemployment rate

Definition. "The unemployment rate is the percentage of the labor force that is unemployed." (Krugman, p. 12)

CED anchor. MEA-1.C.1 — "The unemployment rate is the percentage of the labor force that is out of work." (Topic 2.3, CED p. 48). Learning objective MEA-1.C(c) requires students to calculate it.

Where taught. p. 12, with Figure 2.1 on p. 10.

Explanation

Krugman's wording and the framework's are the same statement; either earns the point. The formula is

unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labor force × 100

and the denominator is the labor force, not the population. This is the most expensive single error in Unit 2, because every later calculation inherits it.

As an indicator the rate reads the job market quickly: high means jobs are hard to find, low means they are easy. It rises in recessions and falls in expansions, which is the behaviour Figure 2.1 shows across twenty years. It is never zero — even a boom leaves people between jobs, which is the natural rate (MEA-1.E.2, CED p. 49, and Module 13).

The instructive property is that the rate can fall for a bad reason. If unemployed people stop searching they leave the labor force: the numerator falls and the denominator falls with it, and the rate drops without anybody finding work. That is why the framework pairs it with the participation rate, and why MEA-1.D.1 names discouraged workers as a limitation of the measure.

Common error. Dividing by the population. Give students a scenario with an adult population figure in it — that number belongs to entry 8 and is a distractor here.

Examples

1. From the text. Figure 2.1 (p. 10) plots the rate from 1989 to 2009 with recessions shaded: it rises sharply in every shaded band and falls between them. The late-1990s boom took it to 4% or lower; the recession beginning in 2007 pushed it into double digits. (pp. 10, 12)

2. Numeric. 10 million unemployed, 150 million employed. Labor force = 160 million. 10 ÷ 160 = 0.0625 = 6.25%. Dividing by a 250 million population instead gives 4% — a whole item lost.

3. Illustrative — the discouraged-worker trap. From a recession position of 20 million unemployed and 140 million employed the rate is 20 ÷ 160 = 12.5%. Five million then stop searching: 15 ÷ 155 = 9.7%. The rate falls nearly three points and not one person has found a job.

Figure. Figure 2.1 (p. 10); the labour-market calculator in student_module_02.html computes all three quantities live.

8. Labor force participation rate ★

Definition. Not in Module 2. From the framework: the labor force participation rate is the percentage of the adult population that is in the labor force.

CED anchor. MEA-1.C.2 — "The labor force participation rate is another measure of the labor market activity in an economy. The labor force participation rate is the percentage of the adult population that is in the labor force." (Topic 2.3, CED p. 48). Learning objective MEA-1.C(c) requires students to calculate it.

Where taught. Not in Module 2 — 0 occurrences. The phrase appears 12 times in the book, in Module 12. Teach it here anyway: the labor force is fresh, and the two rates only make sense as a pair.

Explanation

participation rate = labor force ÷ adult population × 100

Set it beside the unemployment rate and the difference is the question each one answers. The unemployment rate asks of the people in the job market, how many cannot find work? The participation rate asks of the adults who could be in the job market, how many are?

Together they make discouraged workers visible. When people give up searching, the unemployment rate falls and the participation rate falls. Seeing both move down at once is the signal that the apparent improvement is not one. Neither rate can show this alone, which is why the framework requires both and why teaching entry 7 without entry 8 leaves MEA-1.D.1 unreachable.

It also measures things the unemployment rate is blind to. Long-run social changes — women entering paid work through the twentieth century, longer education, an ageing population — show up in participation and not at all in unemployment.

Common error. Swapping the denominators. Labor force on the bottom is the unemployment rate; adult population on the bottom is the participation rate. Make students write the formula before the numbers.

Examples

1. From the framework. MEA-1.C.2 calls it "another measure of the labor market activity in an economy" — the CED's own signal that it is not a substitute for the unemployment rate but a companion to it.

2. Numeric. Labor force 160 million, adult population 250 million. 160 ÷ 250 = 0.64 = 64%. The remaining 36% are retired, studying, caring for family, or not searching.

3. Illustrative. In the discouraged-worker scenario from entry 7, participation falls from 160 ÷ 250 = 64% to 155 ÷ 250 = 62% in the same month the unemployment rate falls from 12.5% to 9.7%. Two falling rates, no new jobs.

9. Aggregate output

Definition. "Output is the quantity of goods and services produced." "Aggregate output is the economy's total production of goods and services for a given time period." (Krugman, p. 12)

CED anchor. MEA-2.A.1 — "Business cycles are fluctuations in aggregate output and employment…" (Topic 2.7, CED p. 53)

Where taught. p. 12.

Explanation

Aggregate means the same thing everywhere in this course: added up across the whole economy. One bakery's bread is output; everything every producer in the country makes in a year is aggregate output.

Two properties matter. It is a flow, not a stock — "for a given time period" is load-bearing, and "the economy produced $20 trillion" means nothing without "in 2024". And it moves opposite to the unemployment rate over the cycle, for a mechanical reason Krugman gives directly: producing more requires more workers.

It is also the quantity the rest of Unit 2 learns to measure properly. Module 10 turns it into GDP, Module 11 into real GDP. Here it is deliberately informal, and saying so prevents a student thinking they have already met GDP.

Common error. Reading aggregate output as an amount of money. It is a quantity of goods and services. If prices rise and the same number of cars leave the factory, aggregate output has not changed — which is the whole of the real-versus-nominal distinction in entry 14 and Topic 2.6.

Examples

1. From the text. Krugman's own statement of the link: at lower levels of output fewer workers are needed and the unemployment rate is relatively high; growth in output requires more workers, which lowers it. (p. 12)

2. Numeric. A small economy produces 400 loaves, 20 haircuts and 3 bicycles this year and less of each next year. Aggregate output has fallen, and some of those bakers, barbers and mechanics will lose work — the two halves of a recession, from one table.

3. Illustrative. Water from a tap versus water in the bath. Output is the flow; the stock of goods already produced is a different quantity, and the definition's time period is what keeps them apart.

Figure. The vertical axis of the cycle diagram in student_module_02.html — everything in entries 1–10 is read off it.

10. Potential output and the output gap ★

Definition. Not in Module 2. From the framework: potential output, also called full-employment output, is the level of GDP at which unemployment equals the natural rate. The output gap is the difference between actual output and potential output.

CED anchor. MEA-2.A.4 — "The difference between actual output and potential output is the output gap." MEA-2.A.5 — "Potential output is also called full-employment output. It is the level of GDP where unemployment is equal to the natural rate of unemployment." (Topic 2.7, CED p. 53)

Where taught. Not in Module 2 — 0 occurrences of either. Potential output appears 170 times in the book and output gap 71, from Module 18 onwards. Both are Topic 2.7 content, so they are examinable in this unit, months before the book gets to them.

Explanation

Module 2 gives students the wave and never the line it waves around. That is the omission to repair, and at this stage it can be repaired as a picture: the dashed trend is what the economy could produce with its people and equipment fully employed, the solid line is what it actually produced, and the vertical distance between them is the gap. The arithmetic belongs to Unit 3.

The sign is what students have to be able to read. Below the line, at a trough, capacity is being wasted — idle workers, quiet factories. Above the line, near a peak, the economy is running hotter than it can sustain, which is where inflationary pressure comes from and why a peak is not simply a good state.

MEA-2.A.5 also closes a loop from entry 7. Potential output is defined at the natural rate of unemployment, which is why full employment does not mean zero unemployment — the point students most often get wrong when the two ideas finally meet in Unit 3.

Common error. Reading potential output as a maximum the economy cannot exceed. Actual output can and does run above it; an unsustainable boom is exactly that state. "Potential" here means sustainable, not maximal.

Examples

1. From the framework. MEA-2.A.5 gives the definition through unemployment rather than through capacity — "the level of GDP where unemployment is equal to the natural rate of unemployment". Quote it as written; the link to the labour market is the examinable part.

2. Numeric. Potential output $20.0 trillion, actual output $19.2 trillion. Gap = −$0.8 trillion, or −0.8 ÷ 20.0 = −4% of potential: a recessionary gap. At an actual $20.4 trillion the gap is +$0.4 trillion, or +2%.

3. Illustrative. A plant built to make 1,000 cars a week makes 700 in a slump and 1,050 in a rush, on overtime it cannot keep up. 1,000 is potential; the other two are the gaps, one of each sign.

11. Inflation and deflation

Definition. "A rising overall price level is inflation." "A falling overall price level is deflation." (Krugman, p. 12)

CED anchor. Learning objective MEA-1.F(a) — "Define the consumer price index (CPI), inflation, deflation, disinflation, the inflation rate, and real variables." (Topic 2.4, CED p. 50). The costs of inflation anchor separately at MEA-1.H.1 (Topic 2.5, CED p. 51).

Where taught. pp. 12–13.

Explanation

Both definitions turn on overall. Krugman states the boundary explicitly: a change in the prices of a few goods changes the opportunity cost of buying those goods and is not inflation; the terms are reserved for general changes in prices throughout the economy. That sentence is the module's second free-response question.

His two costs are genuine and neither is the framework's. Inflation discourages holding cash, because cash loses value as the price level rises — in extreme cases people abandon money for barter. Deflation does the reverse: holding cash beats investing in new factories, and investment dries up, which can deepen a recession.

MEA-1.H.1 states a different cost: "Unexpected inflation arbitrarily redistributes wealth from one group of individuals to another group, such as lenders to borrowers" (CED p. 51). The phrase unexpected inflation occurs 3 times in the whole book and 0 times in Module 2. Teach Krugman's two, then add the framework's, and do not let "we covered the costs of inflation" stand.

Note also what is absent: no consumer price index (0 occurrences in the module, 35 in the book), no inflation rate calculation, no disinflation. Topic 2.4 is Module 15's job.

Common error. "Prices went up, so that's inflation." One price rising is a change in relative prices. Inflation is the general level. Students who cannot make this distinction lose the first point of the module's FRQ 2.

Examples

1. From the text. 1970 to October 2009: average hourly pay rose from $3.40 to $18.74, while a dozen eggs went from $0.58 to $1.60, a loaf of white bread from $0.20 to $1.39, and a gallon of gasoline from $0.33 to $2.61. The average worker's pay went about as far in 2009 as in 1970. (pp. 12–13)

2. Numeric. Check Krugman's claim: pay × 5.51, gasoline × 7.91, bread × 6.95, eggs × 2.76. Prices rose roughly as much as wages, which is what "the increase in the cost of living wiped out the wage gains" means — and a good five minutes of arithmetic on the way to real variables in Topic 2.4.

3. Illustrative. Streaming subscriptions get cheaper while everything else holds steady. That is not deflation; it is streaming becoming cheap relative to everything else.

Figure. None in the module. The purchasing-power widget in student_module_02.html shows what a chosen inflation rate does to $100 over a chosen number of years.

12. Price stability

Definition. "The economy has price stability when the aggregate price level is changing only slowly." (Krugman, p. 13)

CED anchor. POL-1.D.1 — "Central banks implement monetary policies to achieve macroeconomic goals, such as price stability." (Topic 4.6 Monetary Policy, CED p. 82). Preview: the term is defined here and assessed in Unit 4.

Where taught. p. 13.

Explanation

Read the definition precisely: stability is slow change, not no change. Economists do not target zero inflation, because aiming at zero risks overshooting into deflation, and a small positive rate keeps a safe distance from it.

The reason is planning. When the price level moves slowly and predictably, a wage agreed for next year, a thirty-year loan and a printed price list all still mean roughly what they meant when they were written. That is the content of Krugman's claim that price stability "helps keep the economy stable".

The framework's framing is different and worth flagging now. In Krugman price stability is a desirable state of the world; in POL-1.D.1 it is a goal a central bank pursues with monetary policy. A student meeting the term again in Unit 4 should recognise it as an objective, not a description.

Common error. Reading price stability as a general statement that the economy is healthy. An economy can have perfectly stable prices and high unemployment at the same time — the two goals are separate, and Unit 5's Phillips curve is about the tension between them.

Examples

1. From the text. Krugman gives the reason directly: economists regard price stability as desirable because it helps keep the economy stable. (p. 13)

2. Numeric. At 2% a year the price level doubles in about 35 years — a working lifetime. At 20% it doubles in under 4. The first is a background condition; the second reorganises everyone's behaviour.

3. Illustrative. A thirty-year fixed-rate mortgage. Both sides accept the contract only because neither expects the price level to do anything dramatic over its life.

Figure. None. The purchasing-power widget in the student guide makes the same point by setting the rate to 0.

13. Economic growth

Definition. "Economic growth is an increase in the maximum amount of goods and services an economy can produce." (Krugman, p. 13)

CED anchor. MEA-2.B.1 — "Economic growth can be measured as the growth rate in real GDP per capita over time." (Topic 5.6 Economic Growth, CED p. 98). Preview: defined here, assessed in Unit 5.

Where taught. p. 13.

Explanation

Every word of the definition is chosen to keep growth apart from an expansion. Growth is an increase in capacity — in what the economy could produce. An expansion is an increase in what it is producing, from a low starting point, and it lasts only until the next downturn. In the diagram, growth is the dashed potential-output line rising; the cycle is the wave around it.

What increasesActual output and employmentMaximum possible output — capacity

This is the module's central discrimination and its free-response question. The printed rubric awards the third of three points for stating the difference, not for the two definitions — say so before students write, because a script with both definitions and no contrast scores 2.

Krugman also flags a conflict students find surprising and the exam eventually tests: the goal of growth can work against the goal of a quick recovery, and what is good for long-run capacity is not always good for short-run stabilisation.

Common error. Calling a recovery "growth". Output rising 4% after falling 4% returns the economy to where it already was; capacity has not changed. Recovering lost ground is not growth.

Examples

1. From the text. In 1955 Americans were rightly proud of their prosperity, and only 33% of homes had a washing machine; hardly anyone had air conditioning. 1955 was not a slump — the economy was simply able to produce less. (p. 13)

2. Numeric. U.S. aggregate output per person in 2009 was almost nine times its 1900 level. Against a ninefold rise, Krugman notes, the recessions on Figure 2.2 are almost invisible — except the one that began the Great Depression. (p. 13)

3. Illustrative. Two ways for a bakery to make more bread: bring idle staff back to the ovens it already has (expansion), or buy a second oven (growth). Only the second raises what it could ever produce.

Figure. Figure 2.2 (p. 13); the dashed potential-output line in the student guide's cycle diagram is the same idea drawn against the cycle.

14. Real GDP per capita

Definition. Krugman defines it in running text rather than the margin: real gross domestic product per capita is "the value of final goods and services produced in the U.S. per person" (p. 13), plotted in constant 2005 dollars.

CED anchor. MEA-2.B.1 — "Economic growth can be measured as the growth rate in real GDP per capita over time." (Topic 5.6, CED p. 98). The real-versus-nominal machinery behind it is MEA-1.I and MEA-1.J (Topic 2.6, CED p. 52). Preview on both counts.

Where taught. p. 13, Figure 2.2.

Explanation

Three modifiers, each doing a job. GDP — total output. Real — measured at constant prices, so a rise means more goods, not higher prices. Per capita — divided by population, so a country does not look richer merely for being larger.

That combination is why it is the standard measure both of a country's material standard of living and of growth over time. Figure 2.2's point is its slope: across 1900–2009 the long-run rise dwarfs the business cycle, and apart from the Great Depression the recessions are almost invisible on the same axis.

Module 2 uses the term without building it. Real GDP per capita occurs once here and 128 times in the book; the GDP deflator (25 occurrences) and the consumer price index (35) are absent from this module entirely. Introduce it as a name to recognise, and leave the calculation to Modules 11 and 15.

Common error. Treating per capita output as a statement about how people live. It is an average and says nothing about distribution; two countries with identical real GDP per capita can differ sharply in what most of their people actually have.

Examples

1. From the text. Figure 2.2 plots U.S. real GDP per capita from 1900 to 2009 in 2005 dollars, so that a century can be compared on one axis. (p. 13)

2. Numeric. Output $20 trillion across 250 million people is $80,000 per person. If output rises 5% to $21 trillion while prices also rise 5%, real output is unchanged — and if population rose too, real GDP per capita has fallen.

3. Illustrative. A country's total output rises 3% while its population rises 4%. There is more produced in total and less to go round each person, which is exactly why growth is measured per capita.

Figure. Figure 2.2 (p. 13).

15. Model

Definition. "A model is a simplified representation used to better understand a real-life situation." (Krugman, p. 14)

CED anchor. None. The framework has no essential knowledge statement defining a model. Modelling is treated as a skill rather than content — Skill 1.A "Describe economic concepts, principles, or models" is the suggested skill for Topic 2.7 itself (CED p. 53). Context tier, and constantly used.

Where taught. pp. 14–15.

Explanation

The Wright brothers built a wind tunnel a year after their first glider flight, because testing a wing in a box of moving air is cheaper and safer than building an aircraft and hoping. Krugman's analogy is worth keeping: a model is deliberately not the real thing, and is useful for that reason.

He gives three ways economists model. A real but simplified economy — the classic case being the World War II prison camps where cigarettes became money even among prisoners who did not smoke. A computer simulation, as with the tax models officials run on proposed legislation. And most often a thought experiment, usually drawn as a graph. Nearly every diagram in this course is the third kind.

For a Macro class this entry earns its place as preparation. Units 3 to 6 are almost entirely models — AD–AS, the money market, loanable funds, the Phillips curve, the foreign exchange market — and a student who expects a model to be realistic will argue with every one of them.

Common error. Rejecting a model because an assumption is unrealistic. Every assumption is unrealistic; that is what makes it an assumption. The question is whether the simplification changes the answer to the question being asked.

Examples

1. From the text. Tax models — large mathematical computer programs governments use to assess how a proposed change in tax law would affect different groups. (p. 14)

2. Illustrative — the limit. "Garbage in, garbage out." A simulation is only as good as its data; feed a tax model the wrong income distribution and every figure it produces is wrong, however sophisticated the program. (p. 14)

3. Illustrative — the next module. Module 3 asks students to imagine an economy producing exactly two goods. No economy does, and that assumption is what makes the trade-off visible as a curve that can be measured.

Figure. None. The cycle diagram in the student guide is itself a model, which is the point worth making with it.

16. Other things equal (ceteris paribus)

Definition. "The other things equal assumption means that all other relevant factors remain unchanged. This is also known as the ceteris paribus assumption." (Krugman, p. 14)

CED anchor. None. Neither ceteris paribus nor other things equal appears anywhere in the AP Macroeconomics CED — 0 occurrences of each. The idea is present in substance, as "holding other factors constant" inside MEA-2.B.2 (CED p. 98), but no EK defines the assumption. Context tier, used in every graph question on the exam.

Where taught. p. 14.

Explanation

This is the assumption that lets a model answer anything at all. In the world a dozen things change at once and no causal claim is available. Holding everything else fixed isolates the effect of one change, which is the only way to reason about cause in a system this tangled.

Its practical importance in Macro is larger than its zero anchor suggests. Every shift question on the exam — "an increase in government spending shifts aggregate demand" — is an other-things-equal statement, and the mark scheme expects the student to move one curve, not two. A student who "helps" by shifting a second curve because it seemed realistic loses the point.

MEA-2.B.2 is the framework's own use of it: aggregate employment and aggregate output are directly related "holding other factors constant". Quote that when students ask why an assumption with no definition keeps appearing.

Common error. Reading it as "assume nothing changes". It means change exactly one thing on purpose and hold the rest still, so that any effect observed can be attributed to that one thing.

Examples

1. From the text. Krugman's reason for the assumption: models are important because their simplicity "allows economists to focus on the effects of only one change at a time". (p. 14)

2. Illustrative — two causes at once. Output rose 3% in a year when taxes fell and oil got cheaper. Without holding one fixed there is no way to attribute any part of the 3% to either.

3. Illustrative — the classroom version. A school science fair test: change one variable, keep the rest constant, or the result tells you nothing. The logic is identical and students already own it.
