This paper is built around one discrimination. Topic 1.4 has only two essential knowledge statements, and the boundary between them — change in quantity demanded against change in demand — is where almost every mark in this topic is won or lost. Eight of the twenty items test it directly. That is not padding; it is what content validity looks like when a topic has two requirements and one of them is a distinction.
Two entries are background, and they are flagged. Demand schedule and individual demand curve have no anchor in the Macro framework. Questions 19 and 20 test them in a separately scored section, because a student who misses those two has missed nothing the exam will ask for.

Section A — examinable content

1. Four markets are described. In which one would the supply and demand model be least reliable as a description of how the price is set?

  • (A) The world market for coffee beans, where even the largest retailer buys a small fraction of the crop.
  • (B) A national market for wheat with thousands of farms selling an identical grade.
  • (C) A market for cola beverages in which two firms account for most of total sales.
  • (D) A city market for haircuts with hundreds of salons and no dominant chain.
  • (E) An online market for second-hand textbooks with thousands of individual sellers.

2. A student is asked what happens to the price of coffee after a frost destroys part of the crop. They correctly draw a demand curve and stop.

Which element of the supply and demand model do they still need?

  • (A) The demand curve, which they have not finished.
  • (B) The set of factors that shift the demand curve.
  • (C) The supply curve and the market equilibrium.
  • (D) The law of demand.
  • (E) The definition of a competitive market.

3. A demand schedule for coffee beans shows that at $1.25 a pound consumers will buy 8.9 billion pounds a year, and at $1.00 a pound they will buy 10.0 billion pounds.

Which statement uses the terms correctly?

  • (A) Demand at $1.25 is 8.9 billion pounds.
  • (B) Demand is the difference between 10.0 and 8.9 billion pounds.
  • (C) Demand rises from 8.9 to 10.0 billion pounds as the price falls.
  • (D) The quantity demanded is 8.9 billion pounds.
  • (E) The quantity demanded at $1.25 is 8.9 billion pounds.

4. A student draws a downward-sloping curve that plots their table perfectly, labels it D, and draws a second curve labelled D₂ to its right. They do not label the axes.

On the printed six-point rubric, how many marks are at risk, and why?

  • (A) None — the economics is correct and the axes are obvious from context.
  • (B) One, for the axis convention, because price on the vertical axis is a separate scored point.
  • (C) Two, because both curves become unreadable without axes.
  • (D) Three, because every graphing point depends on the axes.
  • (E) Six, because an unlabelled graph scores nothing.

5. "A higher price for a good leads people to demand a smaller quantity of that good."

A classmate says this statement is false, because they have seen prices and sales rise together many times. What is the best response?

  • (A) They are right; the law of demand is only a rough tendency and has many exceptions.
  • (B) They are wrong; price and quantity can never move in the same direction in any market.
  • (C) The statement is incomplete. The law holds all other things being equal, and their examples are cases where other things changed.
  • (D) The statement applies only to competitive markets, and their examples are not competitive.
  • (E) The statement applies only to normal goods, and their examples are inferior goods.

6. Coffee cost considerably more in 2006 than in 2002, and the world drank considerably more of it. The demand schedules for the two years are both downward sloping.

Which account fits both facts?

  • (A) The law of demand failed for coffee in this period.
  • (B) Consumers moved up along a single demand curve, which raises both price and quantity.
  • (C) The demand curve for coffee slopes upward, which is rare but possible.
  • (D) The 2006 demand curve lies to the right of the 2002 curve, and the rightward shift was large enough to raise quantity even though the price rose.
  • (E) The quantity demanded rose because the price rose, which is what the schedules show.

7. A supermarket halves the price of its own-brand bread for one week and sells three times as much.

What has happened?

  • (A) Demand for the bread has increased; the curve shifted right.
  • (B) The quantity demanded has increased; there has been a movement down along the demand curve.
  • (C) Both demand and quantity demanded have increased.
  • (D) Demand has increased because the promotion changed consumer tastes.
  • (E) Nothing can be said without knowing whether bread is a normal good.

8. The price of tea rises sharply. Sales of coffee increase, although the price of coffee is unchanged.

On the diagram for the coffee market, what has happened?

  • (A) A movement down along the coffee demand curve, because a price changed.
  • (B) A movement up along the coffee demand curve, because the price of a beverage rose.
  • (C) The coffee demand curve has shifted right.
  • (D) The coffee demand curve has shifted left.
  • (E) Nothing, because the price of coffee did not change.

9. Two demand schedules for the same good are compared. At every price in the table, the second schedule shows a larger quantity than the first.

Which conclusion is justified?

  • (A) The good's price must have fallen between the two schedules.
  • (B) The second schedule must contain an error, because a demand schedule slopes downward.
  • (C) There has been a movement along the demand curve.
  • (D) The good has become an inferior good.
  • (E) There has been a change in demand, and the demand curve has shifted right.

10. A student writes: "Incomes rose, so the demand curve for restaurant meals shifted up."

What is the problem with that sentence, if any?

  • (A) No problem; up and right describe the same movement for a demand curve.
  • (B) The direction is wrong — an income rise shifts a normal good's curve down.
  • (C) Restaurant meals are an inferior good, so the curve shifts left.
  • (D) The word should be "movement", not "shift", because income is a determinant.
  • (E) "Up" is ambiguous for a downward-sloping curve; the unambiguous description is a shift to the right.

11. Margarine and butter are substitutes. The price of butter falls.

What happens in the market for margarine?

  • (A) Demand for margarine increases; the curve shifts right.
  • (B) Demand for margarine decreases; the curve shifts left.
  • (C) The quantity of margarine demanded increases along an unchanged curve.
  • (D) The quantity of margarine demanded decreases along an unchanged curve.
  • (E) Nothing, because the price of margarine has not changed.

12. A city doubles the tax on commercial parking garages in the city centre, raising the price of parking.

What is the most likely effect on the demand for car trips into the centre, and why?

  • (A) It decreases, because parking is a complement to a car trip and its price has risen.
  • (B) It increases, because drivers who have already paid for parking will make more use of it.
  • (C) It decreases, because the car trip's own price has risen.
  • (D) It is unchanged, because the tax falls on garages rather than on drivers.
  • (E) It increases, because parking and car trips are substitutes.

13. Printers and ink cartridges. Cartridge prices fall sharply.

Which sequence is correct?

  • (A) They are substitutes, so the demand for printers decreases.
  • (B) They are substitutes, so the demand for printers increases.
  • (C) They are complements, so the demand for printers decreases.
  • (D) They are complements, so the demand for printers increases.
  • (E) They are unrelated, so the demand for printers is unchanged.

14. A question asks a student to name a determinant of demand and explain its effect. The student may choose any determinant.

Which choice is safest against the AP Macroeconomics framework, and why?

  • (A) Tastes, because it is the broadest category and covers the most cases.
  • (B) Expectations, because it is the one students most often get wrong and so the most impressive.
  • (C) Consumer income, because it is the only determinant the required content actually names.
  • (D) The price of the good itself, because it is certain to affect demand.
  • (E) The number of consumers, because it is the only one that shifts the market curve.

15. A recession cuts household incomes across a country. Sales of own-brand instant noodles rise.

What does this tell you, and what has happened on the diagram?

  • (A) Noodles are a normal good, and their demand curve has shifted right.
  • (B) Noodles are a normal good, and there has been a movement along their demand curve.
  • (C) Noodles are an inferior good, and their demand curve has shifted left.
  • (D) Noodles are an inferior good, and their demand curve has shifted right.
  • (E) Nothing about the type of good; the price of noodles must have fallen.

16. A widely reported forecast says that laptop prices will fall substantially over the next three months.

What happens to the demand for laptops today?

  • (A) It increases, because consumers become more interested in laptops.
  • (B) It decreases, because consumers delay their purchases.
  • (C) It is unchanged, because the price has not actually fallen yet.
  • (D) The quantity demanded increases today, along an unchanged curve.
  • (E) The quantity demanded decreases today, along an unchanged curve.

17. Four events happen in the market for disposable nappies in the same month: a baby boom begins, cloth nappies become cheaper, a documentary raises concern about landfill, and the price of disposable nappies falls.

Which event is the only one that does not shift the demand curve for disposable nappies?

  • (A) The baby boom.
  • (B) The fall in the price of cloth nappies.
  • (C) The documentary about landfill.
  • (D) The fall in the price of disposable nappies.
  • (E) All four shift the curve.

18. Darla and Dino are the only two buyers in a market. At $2 a pound Darla buys 20 pounds and Dino buys 10. A third buyer then enters who would buy 5 pounds at $2. Neither Darla nor Dino changes their mind about anything.

What is the market quantity demanded at $2, and what has happened to the curves?

  • (A) 35 pounds. The market demand curve has shifted right; both individual curves are unchanged.
  • (B) 35 pounds. All three individual demand curves have shifted right.
  • (C) 30 pounds. A new buyer does not affect the market curve until prices adjust.
  • (D) 11.7 pounds, the average of the three buyers' quantities.
  • (E) 35 pounds. There has been a movement along the market demand curve.

Section B — background content

These two questions test entries with no anchor in the AP Macroeconomics framework. Score them separately. A student who answers Section A well and Section B poorly has lost nothing that the exam will ask for.

19. A table lists four prices and, next to each, the quantity buyers would purchase: $5 → 12 units, $4 → 18, $3 → 26, $2 → 40.

Which statement about this table is correct?

  • (A) It is a demand schedule, and plotting it would give a downward-sloping demand curve.
  • (B) It is a supply schedule, because quantity rises as you read down the table.
  • (C) It is a demand schedule, but it violates the law of demand.
  • (D) It shows four separate demands, one for each price.
  • (E) It cannot be plotted without knowing the number of consumers.

20. A Macro exam question refers simply to "the demand curve for a good" with no further qualification.

Which curve does it mean, and how do you know?

  • (A) The individual demand curve, because that is what the concept is built from.
  • (B) The market demand curve, because the framework's required content refers to the market demand curve and Macro never mentions individual demand.
  • (C) Either — the two are interchangeable, since both slope downward.
  • (D) The market demand curve, because individual demand curves do not obey the law of demand.
  • (E) It is ambiguous and the question cannot be answered as written.

Solutions

Question 1 — Competitive market · Analysis

Answer: (C)

A competitive market needs many buyers and many sellers, none of whom can influence the price. Two firms accounting for most of total sales is exactly the case Krugman gives as the counter-example: Coca-Cola and Pepsi can move the price, so the model's central prediction — that demand and supply settle the price between them — does not hold.

  • (A) ✗ The textbook's own example of a competitive market. Even Starbucks buys a tiny fraction.
  • (B) ✗ Thousands of sellers of an identical product is the textbook case of competition.
  • (C) ✓ Correct. Concentration is what breaks the condition, and it is the number of *sellers

with power*, not the number of firms overall, that matters.

  • (D) ✗ Hundreds of salons with no dominant chain satisfies the condition.
  • (E) ✗ Thousands of individual sellers, none of whom sets the price.

Question 2 — The supply and demand model · Analysis

Answer: (C)

A question about what happens to the price cannot be answered from demand alone. The model has five elements, and price is determined by element 4, the market equilibrium — which requires element 2, the supply curve. A frost is a supply-side event.

  • (A) ✗ The demand curve is finished and is not the problem. Nothing in the scenario changes it.
  • (B) ✗ A frost does not shift demand. It is not a determinant of demand.
  • (C) ✓ Correct. Supply curve, then equilibrium. Those are elements 2 and 4, and they are

Module 6 rather than this one.

  • (D) ✗ They have the law of demand; it is embodied in the curve they drew.
  • (E) ✗ The definition is a precondition for using the model, not a missing piece of the answer.

Question 3 — Quantity demanded · Application

Answer: (E)

Quantity demanded is a number attached to one specific price. Demand is the whole schedule. Both halves of the key are right: the term is quantity demanded, and it is tied to $1.25.

  • (A) ✗ Uses "demand" for a single number at a single price. Demand is the entire relationship.
  • (B) ✗ Demand is not a difference between two quantities.
  • (C) ✗ Demand does not rise when the price falls — the quantity demanded does. This is the error

the whole module is about.

  • (D) ✗ The term is right but the price is missing, and without the price the number means

nothing. Quantity demanded is only defined at a stated price.

  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 4 — Demand curve · Application

Answer: (B)

The printed rubric awards its six marks separately, and one of them is: graph with "Price" on the vertical axis and "Quantity" on the horizontal axis. The other five — the labelled table, the negative relationship, the labelled curve, the plotted points, the second labelled curve — are all earned by this student.

  • (A) ✗ The convention is a scored point in its own right. Markers do not infer it.
  • (B) ✓ Correct. One mark, and only one.
  • (C) ✗ Plausible-sounding, but the curve-labelling marks are separate and were earned.
  • (D) ✗ Over-counts. The rubric does not make other points conditional on the axes.
  • (E) ✗ Far too severe. Five of six marks survive.

Question 5 — Law of demand · Analysis

Answer: (C)

The law of demand is stated all other things being equal. Almost every real-world counter-example is a case where other things were not equal — incomes, population, tastes or the price of something related moved, so the curve shifted while the price changed.

  • (A) ✗ Treats real exceptions as exceptions to the law. Krugman notes that genuine exceptions

are so rare they can be ignored; these are not exceptions at all.

  • (B) ✗ Overstated in the other direction. Price and quantity can rise together — when the

curve shifts, which is precisely the classmate's observation.

  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Competitiveness is a condition for the model, not for the law of demand.
  • (E) ✗ Inferior goods are about income, not about price, and their demand curves slope downward

like everything else.

Question 6 — Law of demand · Synthesis

Answer: (D)

This is Krugman's own worked puzzle, pp. 50–51. Two downward-sloping curves, the 2006 one to the right of the 2002 one. Population grew and lattes became popular, so more was demanded at every price. Move from a point on D₁ to a point on D₂ that is both higher and further right, and both facts hold at once.

  • (A) ✗ Nothing failed. The stem even tells you both schedules slope downward.
  • (B) ✗ Movement along one curve cannot raise price and quantity together — that is the law of

demand.

  • (C) ✗ Contradicts the stem, which says both schedules are downward sloping.
  • (D) ✓ Correct. The shift did the work; the movement along partly offset it.
  • (E) ✗ Reaches a true-sounding phrase by inverting cause and effect. Quantity did not rise

because price rose.

Question 7 — Movement along the curve · Application

Answer: (B)

The bread's own price fell. That is the one cause of a movement along the curve. Sales tripling is dramatic but irrelevant to the classification.

  • (A) ✗ The curve does not shift. Nothing about buyers changed.
  • (B) ✓ Correct. Down and to the right along an unchanged curve.
  • (C) ✗ Demand did not change. Only one of the two did.
  • (D) ✗ Reaches a wrong answer by a plausible route: a price cut is not a change in tastes, even

though both raise sales.

  • (E) ✗ Normal and inferior describe responses to income. No income changed here.

Question 8 — Movement along the curve · Analysis

Answer: (C)

This is the related-good trap. A price moved, but it was tea's price, not coffee's. For the coffee curve, the price of a substitute is one of the shift factors, so the coffee curve moves right. Coffee's own price is unchanged, so there is no movement along it.

  • (A) ✗ A price changed, but not this good's. Always ask whose price moved.
  • (B) ✗ Same error, and the direction is wrong too.
  • (C) ✓ Correct. Tea dearer, some tea drinkers switch, coffee demand up at every price.
  • (D) ✗ Right mechanism, wrong direction. Left would follow from tea getting cheaper.
  • (E) ✗ The stem states that sales rose at an unchanged price, which is the definition of a

shift. "Nothing happened" contradicts the data given.

Question 9 — Change in demand · Analysis

Answer: (E)

The four-word test is at any given price. If more is demanded at every price, the curve has moved right. That is what a change in demand is.

  • (A) ✗ A price change moves you along one schedule; it does not produce a second one.
  • (B) ✗ Both schedules can slope downward and still differ. That is Figure 5.2 exactly.
  • (C) ✗ A movement happens within a single schedule. Two schedules is the signature of a shift.
  • (D) ✗ Inferior describes a response to income. Nothing here says which determinant moved — only

that one did.

  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 10 — Change in demand · Analysis

Answer: (E)

For a downward-sloping curve, "up" and "left" are visually similar and mean opposite things. The unambiguous vocabulary is right for an increase in demand and left for a decrease. The student's economics is correct; the description is not safe.

  • (A) ✗ They do not describe the same movement, which is why the convention exists.
  • (B) ✗ The direction the student implies is right; the problem is the word, not the economics.
  • (C) ✗ Restaurant meals are the textbook's own example of a normal good.
  • (D) ✗ "Shift" is the correct word. A determinant shifts; only the own price moves you along.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 11 — Substitutes · Application

Answer: (B)

Butter and margarine are substitutes. Butter gets cheaper, so some margarine buyers switch to butter, so less margarine is wanted at every price. The margarine curve shifts left.

  • (A) ✗ Right mechanism, wrong direction. The substitute became cheaper, which pulls buyers

away from margarine.

  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Margarine's own price did not change, so there is no movement along its curve.
  • (D) ✗ Same error as (C), and it arrives at a true-sounding fall in quantity by the wrong route.
  • (E) ✗ A substitute's price is a determinant of this good's demand. Something does happen.

Question 12 — Complements · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Parking and a car trip are consumed together, so they are complements. The price of the complement rose, so the demand for car trips falls — the curve shifts left. This is the real policy Krugman describes: cities tax parking precisely to get this shift.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ A sunk-cost argument, and it is about people who have already paid. The question is about

demand at every price.

  • (C) ✗ Right conclusion, wrong reasoning — and the reasoning is what is being tested. The car

trip's own price did not rise; its complement's did. This would be a movement, not a shift.

  • (D) ✗ A tax on garages raises the price drivers pay. Who writes the cheque does not determine

who bears the cost.

  • (E) ✗ Substitutes replace each other. Nobody parks instead of driving there.

Question 13 — Substitutes and complements · Synthesis

Answer: (D)

Two steps, and both have to be right. First classify: printers and cartridges are consumed together, so they are complements. Then apply the direction: for complements, a fall in the price of one increases demand for the other. Cheaper cartridges, more demand for printers, curve shifts right.

  • (A) ✗ Wrong classification and wrong direction.
  • (B) ✗ Wrong classification. It reaches the right conclusion by the wrong route, which is why it

is the most dangerous option here — a student who guesses "increases" and back-fills a reason lands on it.

  • (C) ✗ Right classification, wrong direction. Cartridges got cheaper, not dearer.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ They are as closely related as any pair in the topic.

Question 14 — Normal goods and the framework · Application

Answer: (C)

MKT-2.B.1 reads: factors that influence consumer demand, such as changes in consumer income, cause the market demand curve to shift. Income is the only determinant the required content names, which makes it the one guaranteed to be recognised. The framework's own suggested classroom activity also uses income.

  • (A) ✗ Legitimate, and breadth is not the criterion. The framework never mentions tastes.
  • (B) ✗ Legitimate but not safer, and "impressive" is not a marking criterion.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ The good's own price is not a determinant of demand at all. It moves you along the

curve. Choosing it would lose the mark outright.

  • (E) ✗ Every determinant shifts the market curve; that is what MKT-2.B.1 says. Number of

consumers is not special in that respect.

Question 15 — Inferior goods · Analysis

Answer: (D)

Income fell and demand rose. That is the definition of an inferior good, run in the direction students rehearse least. A fall in income shifts an inferior good's curve right.

  • (A) ✗ For a normal good, falling income shifts the curve left, and sales would drop.
  • (B) ✗ Both halves wrong, and income changes never produce movements along the curve.
  • (C) ✗ Right classification, wrong direction. Left would follow from income rising.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ The scenario gives you an income change and a quantity response, which is exactly what

identifies the type of good. Assuming an unstated price change ignores the data.

Question 16 — Expectations · Analysis

Answer: (B)

Expectations run against intuition. If it will be cheaper later, wait — so less is bought today at every price, and today's curve shifts left. Krugman's mirror case is the Starbucks price rise of October 2006, where an expected rise made shoppers buy early.

  • (A) ✗ Confuses a price forecast with a change in tastes.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ True that no price has changed, but that is why this is a shift: behaviour changed

without a price change. "Unchanged" is the answer students give when they look for a price and cannot find one.

  • (D) ✗ Quantity demanded changes only when the own price changes, which has not happened.
  • (E) ✗ Same error as (D), and the quantity demanded at today's price does fall — but because

the curve moved, not along it.

Question 17 — Which factor · Analysis

Answer: (D)

Three of the four events are determinants: a baby boom is the number of consumers, cheaper cloth nappies is the price of a substitute, and the landfill documentary is tastes. The fourth — a fall in the price of disposable nappies — is the good's own price, and it moves you along the curve.

  • (A) ✗ Number of consumers. Shifts right.
  • (B) ✗ Price of a substitute. Shifts left.
  • (C) ✗ Tastes. Shifts left.
  • (D) ✓ Correct. The only own-price event in the list.
  • (E) ✗ Exactly one of the four does not shift the curve, and identifying it is the question.

Question 18 — Market demand curve · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

Horizontal summation: hold the price fixed and add the quantities. 20 + 10 + 5 = 35 pounds at $2. And the mechanism matters — no existing buyer changed their mind, so no individual curve moved. The market curve shifted right because there are now more curves in the sum. That is how population growth raises demand.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Right arithmetic, wrong mechanism. The stem says explicitly that Darla and Dino did not

change their minds, so their curves are where they were.

  • (C) ✗ The market curve is the sum of whoever is in the market. A new buyer changes the sum

immediately.

  • (D) ✗ You add quantities; you never average them. This is the most common summation error.
  • (E) ✗ A movement requires the own price to change, and the price is fixed at $2 throughout.

Question 19 — Demand schedule · Application · background

Answer: (A)

A demand schedule pairs each price with the quantity buyers would purchase at it. Reading down the table the price falls and the quantity rises, which is the law of demand, so plotting it gives a downward-sloping demand curve.

  • (A) ✓ Correct. Check the direction before the label: as price falls, quantity rises.
  • (B) ✗ Quantity does rise as you read down, but the price is falling down the table. For a

supply schedule, quantity would rise as price rises.

  • (C) ✗ It obeys the law of demand. Nothing about it is a violation.
  • (D) ✗ It shows one demand, observed at four prices. Four quantities demanded, one demand.
  • (E) ✗ The schedule is already a market schedule if it describes market behaviour, and it can be

plotted as given.

Question 20 — Individual and market demand · Synthesis · background

Answer: (B)

Two reasons, and a complete answer gives both. The required content says that determinants cause the market demand curve to shift, and the phrase individual demand has zero occurrences in the AP Macroeconomics framework. Krugman says the same thing about ordinary usage: most of the time when economists say "the demand curve" they mean the market one.

  • (A) ✗ True that the concept is built from individual curves, but that is not what an

unqualified reference means.

  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ They are not interchangeable — one is the horizontal sum of many of the other — and

sloping the same way does not make them the same curve.

  • (D) ✗ Right conclusion, false reason. Individual demand curves obey the law of demand; that is

exactly why the market curve does.

  • (E) ✗ It is not ambiguous, and saying so would forfeit the question.

Notes for the teacher

Content validity. All 15 concept entries appear across the 20 items. Weighting follows the framework rather than the textbook: Topic 1.4 has two essential knowledge statements, and the distinction that runs between them — quantity demanded against demand — carries eight items (6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 16, 17 and, in part, 3). Krugman spends four of his eleven pages on the five shift factors; this paper spends seven items on them, which is proportionate to a framework that names one.

The two-question drill is what is being tested. Did the good's own price change? Then it is a movement. Did anything else change? Then it is a shift. Questions 7, 8 and 17 put the own-price case and the related-good case next to each other deliberately, because a student who can pass one and fail the other has a procedure rather than an understanding.

Bloom levels. Application 6 · Analysis 10 · Synthesis 4, a ratio of 1 : 1.67 : 0.67 against the standard's 1 : 3 : 1.75. The shortfall in analysis is real and is worth stating plainly rather than papering over: this topic offers almost nothing to calculate, so items that would be arithmetic-application elsewhere become classification-analysis here, and the counts compress towards the middle. Compare the Module 4 paper, where two of four learning-objective verbs were computational and application ran to six items out of twenty on genuine arithmetic. Here, only the summation in question 18 involves a sum at all.

Distractor design. Every wrong option encodes a documented error. The recurring ones:

  • The own-price trap — treating a price cut as an increase in demand (7A, 17D as the key, and the

textbook's own MCQ 1A and 5E)

  • The related-good trap — treating another good's price as an own price (8A, 8B, 12C)
  • Right conclusion, wrong reasoning — 12C and 13B both arrive somewhere defensible by a route

that would fail on a free-response question, and both are called out as such in the solutions

  • Direction reversal — 11A, 13C, 15C: correct classification, inverted sign
  • Category error — 3A and 3C use "demand" where "quantity demanded" belongs; 13A and 13B

misclassify the pair before choosing any direction

Answer distribution. A 3 · B 5 · C 5 · D 4 · E 3. The first draft was badly skewed — B took eight of the twenty keys and E took none — because the natural phrasing of a shift-or-movement item puts "the curve shifts right" early in the option list, where it gets written as B. Six items were rebalanced by permuting their options, never by rewriting them: questions 3, 9 and 10 moved their key to E, questions 6 and 15 to D, and question 12 to A. No stem, no explanation and no piece of economics changed — only the order in which five already-written options appear. There is no positional pattern left: no letter takes more than five of the twenty keys, and the longest run is E at questions 9 and 10, the two items that deliberately share the change-in-demand theme.

Timing. 35 minutes rather than the usual 40, because there is no arithmetic. If a class needs longer than that, the problem is the shift/movement distinction, not the reading load — and the diagnostic table will show it, because the slow students will also be the ones missing 7, 8 and 17.
