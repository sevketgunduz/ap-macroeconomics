Measuring Inflation

Thirteen terms from Module 15 of Krugman’s Economics for AP: how a price index is built from a market basket, how the consumer price index and the GDP deflator measure inflation, how to compare dollars from different years — and why the CPI overstates inflation, which your textbook explains without the name your exam uses.

The 13 terms

01

Aggregate price level

★ Exam
Definition

A single measure of the overall level of prices in the economy.

Textbook page 142, margin.

In any year some prices rise, some fall and some stay put. Gasoline jumped from $3 to $4 a gallon in 2008 while electronics got cheaper. The aggregate price level sums all of that up in one number.

You never see it directly. It is measured by a price index — entries 4, 5 and 11.

Watch out. One price rising is not inflation. Inflation is the overall level rising.

Textbook

Summer 2008. Gasoline rose from about $3 to over $4 a gallon, while egg and electronics prices fell. People still felt the cost of living rising.

Numeric

The diagram above. Three fruit prices rose by 100%, 66.7% and 80%; for your textbook’s basket the overall rise is 84.2%.

Illustrative

A month at the shops. Bread up, phones down, rent up. Whether prices overall rose depends on how much of each you buy.

How it connects. It is measured by a price index (entry 4) built on a market basket (entry 2); the best known is the CPI (entry 5).

Check yourself

1.1 Can the aggregate price level rise while some prices fall?

Show answer

Yes. It is an average across goods.

1.2 Why is one price not enough to measure inflation?

Show answer

Because prices move differently, and what matters is the cost of what people actually buy.

02

Market basket

★ Exam
Definition

A fixed set of goods and services — what a typical consumer buys — whose cost is tracked over time.

Textbook page 142, margin.

To measure prices overall, pick a basket and keep it the same. Then any change in its cost is a change in prices, not in what people buy.

That is both the strength and the weakness of the method. Keeping the basket fixed isolates price changes; but real people do change what they buy — entries 8 and 10.

Watch out. The quantities in the basket stay the same in both years. Only the prices change.

Textbook

Table 15.1. 200 oranges, 50 grapefruit and 100 lemons, priced before and after a frost.

Numeric

Pricing it. (200 × $0.20) + (50 × $0.60) + (100 × $0.25) = $95.

Illustrative

Your own basket. A month of lunches, bus fares, phone data and streaming. Price the same list every year and you have your own price index.

How it connects. It is how the price level (entry 1) is measured, priced against a base year (entry 3) to make a price index (entry 4). What goes in it matters — entry 10.

Check yourself

2.1 Why must the basket stay fixed?

Show answer

So that a change in its cost reflects prices, not a change in what is bought.

2.2 A basket has 10 sandwiches at $4 and 5 coffees at $2. What does it cost?

Show answer

40 + 10 = $50.

03

Base year

★ Exam
Definition

The year the others are compared with. A price index always equals 100 in its base year.

Textbook page 143.

The base year is the reference point. Dividing the basket’s cost in the base year by itself and multiplying by 100 always gives 100, so you never need to remember what the basket cost then.

The U.S. CPI uses a base period, 1982–84, rather than a single year.

Watch out. The base year’s index is 100, not the basket’s cost in that year.

Textbook

Your AP Review, question 4. The value of a price index in the base year is 100.

Numeric

The diagram above. The basket cost $95 in the base year; the index for that year is 100, not 95.

Illustrative

Moving the base. Choose a different base year and every index number changes, but the percentage changes between years do not.

How it connects. It is the denominator of a price index (entry 4), and it prices the same basket (entry 2).

Check yourself

3.1 What is the CPI’s value in its base period?

Show answer

100.

3.2 An index is 125 this year. What does that mean?

Show answer

Prices are 25% higher than in the base year.

04

Calculating a price index

★ Exam
Definition

Price index = cost of the basket in a given year ÷ cost of the basket in the base year × 100.

Textbook page 143, equation 15-1 and Table 15.1.

Two steps. First price the basket in each year: multiply each price by its quantity and add. Then divide this year’s cost by the base year’s and multiply by 100.

The result tells you how much the basket’s cost has risen since the base year: an index of 184.2 means 84.2% more.

Watch out. Price the same quantities in both years. A common slip is to use different quantities after prices change.

Textbook

Table 15.1. $95 before the frost, $175 after: 175 ÷ 95 × 100 = 184.2.

Numeric

Your AP Review, question 1. A basket costing $100 in the base year and $108 now has an index of 108.

Illustrative

The diagram above. Press More lemons, fewer oranges: $100 → $180, an index of 180.

How it connects. It measures the price level (entry 1) from a basket (entry 2) and a base year (entry 3). The CPI (entry 5) is one; the inflation rate is its change (entry 6); the GDP deflator (entry 11) is another.

Check yourself

4.1 A basket cost $60 in the base year and costs $72 now. What is the index?

Show answer

72 ÷ 60 × 100 = 120.

4.2 The same basket costs $57 a year later still. What is the index?

Show answer

57 ÷ 60 × 100 = 95 — prices fell below the base year.

05

Consumer price index

★ Exam
Definition

The price index for the market basket of a typical urban family. It measures how much more income that family needs to keep the same standard of living as prices change.

Textbook page 144, margin.

The government prices about 80,000 items a month in some 23,000 shops in 87 cities. The basket is weighted by what families actually spend: in 2008, housing was 43% of it and motor fuel 3%.

It is the number behind “the inflation rate” in the news, and it is used to adjust pensions and tax brackets (entry 13). But a fixed basket only approximates the cost of living — entries 8 and 9.

Watch out. The CPI prices what consumers buy. It is not the price of everything produced — that is closer to the GDP deflator.

Textbook

Figure 15.1. Housing 43%, food and beverages 16%, transportation 12%, motor fuel 3% of the CPI basket in December 2008.

Numeric

Two families. $20,000 in 1985 and $40,000 in 2010, when the CPI had doubled: about the same standard of living.

Illustrative

Other countries. Poor countries weight food heavily; Japan weights raw fish more and beef less; France weights wine more.

How it connects. It is a price index (entry 4) for consumers, with entry 1 as its purpose. Its weights are entry 10; its biases are entry 8 and entry 9; payments tied to it are entry 13.

Check yourself

5.1 What does the CPI try to measure?

Show answer

How much more income a typical family needs to keep the same standard of living as prices change.

5.2 Why does housing matter more than motor fuel in the CPI?

Show answer

Families spend far more on it, so it has a bigger weight in the basket.

06

The inflation rate from a price index

★ Exam
Definition

Inflation rate = (index in year 2 − index in year 1) ÷ index in year 1 × 100.

Textbook page 143, equation 15-2.

The inflation rate is the percentage change in a price index from one year to the next. Usually that index is the CPI, but the GDP deflator works the same way.

The difference between two index numbers is in points. The inflation rate is a percentage. They are equal only when the first index is exactly 100.

Watch out. From 80 to 120 is 40 points but 50%.

Textbook

Your AP Review, question 3. The CPI was 201.6 in 2006 and 207.3 in 2007: (207.3 − 201.6) ÷ 201.6 × 100 = 2.8%.

Numeric

Your AP Review, multiple choice 2. From 80 to 120: 40 ÷ 80 × 100 = 50%.

Illustrative

Two indexes, two answers. The GDP deflator went from 96.8 to 100.0 in 2004–05 (3.3%) while the CPI went from 188.9 to 195.3 (3.4%).

How it connects. It is the change in a price index (entry 4), and it can be measured with the GDP deflator (entry 11). Comparing dollar amounts uses the same index numbers — entry 7.

Check yourself

6.1 The CPI rises from 250 to 260. What is the inflation rate?

Show answer

10 ÷ 250 × 100 = 4%.

6.2 A price index falls from 120 to 114. What is the inflation rate?

Show answer

−6 ÷ 120 × 100 = −5% — deflation.

07

Comparing dollar amounts across years

★ Exam
Definition

To express an amount in another year’s dollars, multiply by the ratio of the two years’ index values: value in year B = value in year A × (index in B ÷ index in A).

Textbook page 144, in words. The formula is not written out.

This is how you compare a 1985 salary with a 2010 salary, or a price from your grandparents’ day with today’s. Scale the old amount up by however much prices have risen since.

If the converted old amount is bigger than the new one, the new one buys less — even if it is more dollars.

Watch out. Put the year you are converting to on top of the ratio.

Textbook

The 1985 and 2010 families. The CPI doubled, so $20,000 in 1985 is worth about $40,000 in 2010 dollars — the same as the 2010 family’s income.

Numeric

A price then and now. $50 when the CPI was 120, in dollars of a year when the CPI is 180: 50 × 180 ÷ 120 = $75.

Illustrative

A starting salary. $30,000 when the CPI was 150; the CPI is now 240. In today’s dollars that is 30,000 × 1.6 = $48,000. A $45,000 offer today buys less.

How it connects. It uses the index numbers of entry 6 and the indexing idea of entry 13: payments scaled by the CPI.

Check yourself

7.1 $200 when the CPI was 100. What is it in dollars of a year when the CPI is 150?

Show answer

200 × 150 ÷ 100 = $300.

7.2 Your grandfather earned $10,000 when the CPI was 40. The CPI is now 300. What is that in today’s dollars?

Show answer

10,000 × 300 ÷ 40 = $75,000.

08

Substitution bias

★ Exam

⚑ The name is not in your textbook — learn it from here. Your textbook describes this with hamburgers on page 145 but never calls it substitution bias. Your exam uses exactly that name.

Definition

When a price rises, people switch to cheaper substitutes. A fixed basket keeps buying the expensive good, so the CPI overstates the rise in the cost of living.

Textbook page 145 describes it, without this name.

The CPI prices the same basket every year. Real families do not: if hamburgers double in price, many people eat something else. Their cost of living rises by less than the fixed basket says.

This is the shortcoming your exam names. It makes the CPI a little too high as a measure of inflation.

Watch out. The direction is overstate. The CPI shows more inflation than people actually face.

Textbook

Hamburgers. If their price suddenly doubled, many Americans would switch to cheaper food; an index heavy in hamburgers would overstate the rise in the cost of living.

Numeric

The diagram above. Your textbook’s basket shows 84.2%. A household that switched towards lemons, which rose less than oranges, faces 80%.

Illustrative

Beef and chicken. Beef prices jump; families buy more chicken. The CPI, still pricing last year’s beef, reports more inflation than they feel.

How it connects. It is a flaw of the CPI’s fixed basket (entry 5): the weights (entry 10) go out of date. Entry 9 gives two more reasons for the same overstatement.

Check yourself

8.1 Does substitution bias make the CPI overstate or understate inflation?

Show answer

Overstate.

8.2 Why does a fixed basket cause it?

Show answer

Because it keeps pricing goods that people have stopped buying because they became expensive.

09

New goods and quality changes

★ Exam

⚑ Partly not in your textbook — learn it from here. Your textbook explains new goods. It never mentions quality changes, which are the other half of this idea.

Definition

New goods and better quality at the same price make a dollar worth more. A fixed basket misses both, so the CPI overstates inflation.

Textbook page 145 for new goods. Quality is not in the textbook.

New goods: in 1985 there was no internet and no smartphone. Innovation widens what money can buy, which is like a fall in prices — but a basket set before the good existed cannot see it.

Quality: a laptop that costs the same as five years ago but is far faster is really cheaper. If the index records only the price tag, it counts no improvement.

Watch out. A higher price is not always inflation. If the product is better, part of the price rise is paying for quality.

Textbook

Innovation. By widening the range of consumer choice, innovation makes a given amount of money worth more.

Numeric

A better phone. A phone rises from $600 to $630 (5%) but has twice the storage and a better camera. Counting the whole $30 as inflation overstates it.

Illustrative

Broadband. Almost no household had broadband a decade before your textbook; an old basket misses both the new service and its falling price.

How it connects. It adds to substitution bias (entry 8), and is another flaw of the CPI (entry 5).

Check yourself

9.1 Name three reasons the CPI overstates inflation.

Show answer

Substitution bias, new goods, and quality improvements.

9.2 A car costs 3% more but is safer and uses less fuel. Is the full 3% inflation?

Show answer

No. Part of it pays for better quality.

10

The basket's weights decide the answer

★ Exam
Definition

The same price changes give a different index for a different basket. The weights — how much of each good — matter as much as the prices.

Textbook pages 144–145, Check Your Understanding 1 and Figure 15.1.

Oranges doubled in price, lemons rose 80% and grapefruit two-thirds. A basket heavy in oranges shows more inflation; one heavy in lemons shows less.

So different households face different inflation rates: a retiree who spends mostly on medical care and a student who spends on tuition can both differ a lot from the CPI.

Watch out. An out-of-date basket can bias either way for a particular good. Overall, though, the CPI’s net bias is upward.

Textbook

Check Your Understanding 1. With 100 oranges and 200 lemons the index is 180, not 184.2: the index is very sensitive to the basket.

Numeric

The diagram above. Oranges only: 100%. Grapefruit only: 66.7%. Lemons only: 80%.

Illustrative

An old basket and cars. A ten-year-old basket with too few cars, whose prices rose faster, understates inflation — your textbook’s own example.

How it connects. It is about the basket (entry 2) behind the CPI (entry 5), and it is why substitution bias (entry 8) happens.

Check yourself

10.1 Which basket shows more inflation after the frost: mostly oranges or mostly grapefruit?

Show answer

Mostly oranges — their price rose the most.

10.2 Why might a retiree’s inflation rate differ from the CPI?

Show answer

Their basket has different weights, for example much more medical care.

11

GDP deflator

★ Exam
Definition

Nominal GDP ÷ real GDP × 100. A price index for everything produced in the economy.

Textbook page 146, margin.

It is not built from a basket, but it does the same job: it equals 100 in the base year and rises with prices. Its percentage change is an inflation rate, just like the CPI’s.

It covers investment and government purchases as well as consumer goods, and leaves out imports. So it can differ a little from the CPI, though the two usually move together.

Watch out. If nominal GDP doubles and real GDP is unchanged, the deflator has doubled: all of the rise was prices.

Textbook

Figure 15.3. The CPI, the PPI and the GDP deflator all show deflation in the early Depression, inflation in World War II and the 1970s, and stability in the 1990s.

Numeric

Nominal against real. Nominal GDP $150 billion, real GDP $120 billion: deflator = 125.

Illustrative

Your AP Review, question 2. The deflator went from 100.0 to 103.3 in 2005–06: 3.3% inflation; the CPI gave 3.2%.

How it connects. It is another price index (entry 4), whose change is an inflation rate (entry 6). A third index is the PPI (entry 12).

Check yourself

11.1 Nominal GDP is $500 billion and real GDP $400 billion. What is the deflator?

Show answer

500 ÷ 400 × 100 = 125.

11.2 Name one difference between what the CPI and the GDP deflator cover.

Show answer

The deflator includes investment and government goods and excludes imports; the CPI prices a consumer basket, imports included.

12

Producer price index

○ Background
Definition

A price index for goods and services bought by producers — raw materials such as steel, electricity and coal.

Textbook pages 145–146, margin.

Producers of raw materials raise prices quickly when demand changes, so the PPI often moves before the CPI. That makes it an early warning of inflation.

You will not be asked to calculate it. Know what it is and why people watch it.

Watch out. Background only. Your exam does not ask you to calculate the PPI.

Textbook

The late 2000s. Energy and food prices surged and then dropped; the PPI showed the swing far more than the CPI or the GDP deflator.

Numeric

Figure 15.3. The PPI’s annual changes range far wider than the other two indexes.

Illustrative

A steel shortage. Steel prices jump this month. Car prices, and the CPI, follow months later.

How it connects. It is a third index alongside the GDP deflator (entry 11).

Check yourself

12.1 Why is the PPI called an early warning signal?

Show answer

Producer prices respond quickly to demand, so they move before consumer prices.

12.2 Will you need to calculate the PPI?

Show answer

No.

13

Indexing to the CPI

○ Background
Definition

Payments that rise automatically with the CPI — Social Security, tax brackets and wages with a cost-of-living adjustment (COLA).

Textbook page 147, the box on indexing.

Tying a payment to the CPI protects it from inflation: if prices rise 3%, the payment rises 3%. That is why people on indexed pensions do not lose from unexpected inflation.

It also makes the CPI’s biases expensive. If the CPI overstates inflation by 1 point, every indexed payment rises 1 point more than the cost of living.

Watch out. Background here. It connects to who loses from unexpected inflation in Module 14.

Textbook

1780. Massachusetts tied its soldiers’ pay to a basket of corn, beef, wool and leather.

Numeric

Social Security. 48 million people; each extra point of measured inflation adds 1% to every check.

Illustrative

A COLA clause. A union contract raises wages each year by the CPI’s percentage change.

How it connects. It uses the CPI (entry 5) to do what entry 7 does by hand: scale a dollar amount by the change in prices.

Check yourself

13.1 Why does an indexed pension protect against inflation?

Show answer

It rises by the same percentage as the CPI.

13.2 If the CPI overstates inflation, who gains from indexing?

Show answer

People receiving indexed payments: they get slightly more than the true rise in their cost of living.

The five things to get right from Module 15

  1. A price index prices the same basket in two years and divides by the base year’s cost. The base year is 100.
  2. Index points are not percentages. Divide the change by the earlier index.
  3. To compare dollars across years, scale by the ratio of the two index values.
  4. The CPI overstates inflation: substitution bias, new goods and quality changes. Know the name substitution bias.
  5. The GDP deflator is a second index — everything produced, not a consumer basket.
