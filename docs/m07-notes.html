The headline, and it is unusual. This is the shortest module in Unit 1 — six pages, one essential knowledge statement — and it is the only one whose learning objective the College Board illustrates with a published sample exam question. That question (CED p. 134) is the hardest case in the module: a simultaneous shift with an indeterminate price. The answer key (CED p. 140) tags it MKT-2.G, Unit 1. Teach the double shift; the CED has told you it counts.

Krugman's two stated objectives for the module (p. 71):

  1. How equilibrium price and quantity are affected when there is a change in either supply or

demand

  1. How equilibrium price and quantity are affected when there is a simultaneous change in both

The first is four results; the second is four more. Eight outcomes, one page of arithmetic-free reasoning, and a module that is genuinely finishable in a period.

The essential knowledge statement, verbatim (CED p. 39):

MKT-2.G.1 — Changes in the determinants of supply and/or demand result in a new equilibrium price and quantity.

Two words carry the whole module: and/or. "Or" is the single-shift case, which is easy. "And" is the simultaneous case, which is not, and which the CED's sample question goes straight to.

The eight results, and the pattern that generates them

Do not ask students to memorise eight rows. Give them the mechanism and the rows fall out.

Single shift. The curve that moves determines the quantity direction; the other curve is the one you slide along.

ShiftPriceQuantityWhy
Demand increasesrisesrisesshortage at the old price → price up → movement up along supply
Demand decreasesfallsfallssurplus at the old price → price down → movement down along supply
Supply increasesfallsrisessurplus at the old price → price down → movement down along demand
Supply decreasesrisesfallsshortage at the old price → price up → movement up along demand

Notice the structure: when demand moves, price and quantity go the same way; when supply moves, they go opposite ways. That is the whole of the single-shift case in one sentence.

Simultaneous shift. One rule, and it is worth more than any table:

Shifts in opposite directions → the price is determinate and the quantity is ambiguous. Shifts in the same direction → the quantity is determinate and the price is ambiguous.
Both curvesPriceQuantity
Demand up, supply downrisesambiguous
Demand down, supply upfallsambiguous
Demand up, supply upambiguousrises
Demand down, supply downambiguousfalls

The reason is visible on a diagram and worth showing rather than asserting. When the curves move in opposite directions, both shifts push the price the same way, so the price must move — and they push quantity in opposite ways, so quantity depends on which shift is bigger. When they move the same way, the roles swap.

With straight-line curves of equal and opposite slope the arithmetic is exact and pretty: the determinate variable moves by an amount proportional to the sum of the two shifts, and the ambiguous one by an amount proportional to their difference. The student guide's diagram is built on D: P = 14 − ½*Q* and S: P = 2 + ½*Q*, equilibrium (12, 8), and shows two panels of each simultaneous case that reach the same price but different quantities.

The CED's own sample question — read it before teaching this

CED p. 134, sample multiple-choice question 2:

Which of the following changes would result in an indeterminate change in the equilibrium price in a perfectly competitive market? (A) An increase in demand and a decrease in supply (B) An increase in demand and an increase in supply (C) A decrease in demand and an increase in supply (D) A decrease in demand with no change in supply (E) A decrease in supply with no change in demand

The published answer key (CED p. 140) gives B, Skill 2.B, Learning Objective MKT-2.G, Unit 1. Three things follow.

  1. The double shift is examinable. The only sample item the CED publishes for this learning

objective is the hardest case in the module, and it requires all four simultaneous results to eliminate the distractors.

  1. The College Board's word is indeterminate. Krugman says ambiguous. Teach both, and prefer

the CED's word in written answers. Indeterminate appears twice in the whole Macro CED — both times in the sample exam, never in the required course content — so this question is the only place a student will meet it before the real paper.

  1. The phrase perfectly competitive appears exactly once in the Macro CED, and it is in this

question. Students who have only heard "competitive market" from Module 5 should be told the two mean the same thing here.

Everything after Unit 1. MKT-2.G is the goods-market rehearsal for the single most repeated task in AP Macroeconomics: something changes, show it on a diagram, say what happens to both variables.

And the indeterminate case recurs. The CED's sample question 10 (p. 137) asks what contractionary monetary policy plus contractionary fiscal policy do to interest rates and real GDP, and its published answer is: "Real GDP will decrease, and the change in interest rates will be indeterminate." Same reasoning, different market — two forces pushing one variable in opposite directions. A student who understands why quantity is ambiguous here has already understood that Unit 5 question.

Say this in class. It is the strongest available answer to "why are we still doing coffee?"

FRQ 1's rubric is the Skill 4.C checklist, and it is worth copying onto the wall next to Module 5's:

  1. Vertical axis labelled Price (or P), horizontal axis labelled Quantity (or Q)
  2. A downward-sloping demand curve and an upward-sloping supply curve, both labelled
  3. Equilibrium price and quantity found at the intersection and labelled on the axes
  4. A new, labelled curve drawn on the correct side of the original
  5. The new equilibrium price and quantity found at the new intersection and labelled on the axes

Marks 1, 2 and 3 are the Module 5 and 6 conventions. Marks 4 and 5 are what 4.C adds. Students who draw a beautiful shift and never label the new price and quantity lose two of five.

5. Corrections to make explicitly in class

The disturbance is a curve, not a price — this is Module 6 reversed

Module 6 asked: the price is wrong, what happens? Module 7 asks: a curve moved, what happens? Taught in the same week, they merge into "something about surpluses" unless the difference is stated.

Module 6Module 7
What is wrong initiallythe price, away from equilibriumnothing — the market was in equilibrium
What movesthe price, back to the unchanged intersectiona curve, to a new intersection
The surplus or shortageis the phenomenon being studiedis a transient, on the way to the new equilibrium
Ends atthe same equilibrium as beforea different equilibrium

The surplus or shortage in Module 7 is worth naming out loud anyway, because it is the mechanism: demand shifts right → at the old price there is now a shortage → that shortage pushes the price up → which pulls the quantity supplied up along the supply curve. Students who can narrate that chain do not have to memorise the four single-shift results.

Only one curve moves unless the question moves two

The commonest wrong answer on MCQ 1 is "supply will decrease" for a salmonella outbreak, because "something bad happened to tomatoes" feels like a supply event. It is not: the tomatoes are still there and still growable. What changed is that buyers do not want them. Demand shifts left.

Give students the diagnostic question: did the event change what buyers want at a given price, or what sellers will offer at a given price? Nothing else decides it.

"Ambiguous" does not mean "no change" and it does not mean "we cannot tell anything"

Two errors in one word.

  • It does not mean the variable stays the same. In Krugman's Figure 7.3 the quantity changes in

both panels — it rises in one and falls in the other. Zero change is only the knife-edge case.

  • It does not mean the question is unanswerable. The other variable is completely determined,

and a full answer says so: "the equilibrium price rises; the change in equilibrium quantity is indeterminate without knowing the relative sizes of the shifts."

That sentence is the model answer. Drill it verbatim.

Say which variable is indeterminate, not just "it depends"

A simultaneous-shift question always has one determinate variable and one indeterminate one. An answer that says only "it depends on the size of the shifts" has thrown away the half of the question that could be answered. Structure every answer the same way: name the determinate variable and its direction first, then name the indeterminate one.

Draw the second panel

The single most effective five minutes in this module: have students draw the same two shift directions twice, once with a big demand shift and once with a big supply shift, and see the quantity go the other way. Assertion does not produce belief here; two diagrams do.
