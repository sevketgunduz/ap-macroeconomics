The headline. The module teaches the model well — the downward-sloping short-run curve, the vertical long-run curve, supply shocks and expectations shifting the short-run curve — but it argues in its own vocabulary, NAIRU (18 times in the module, 0 in the CED) and accelerating inflation, and never uses the four words the framework's statements turn on: demand shock, long-run equilibrium, inflationary gap and recessionary gap are 0 times each in pp. 331–342. One statement is absent from the whole book: the long-run curve never shifts (MOD-3.B.3; long-run Phillips curve occurs 10 times, all in this module, none moving it). Supply the labels and the shift; the second half of the module — deflation, the zero bound, the liquidity trap — is background.

Krugman's five stated objectives (p. 331):

  1. What the Phillips curve is and the nature of the short-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment
  2. Why there is no long-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment
  3. Why expansionary policies are limited due to the effects of expected inflation
  4. Why even moderate levels of inflation can be hard to end
  5. Why deflation is a problem for economic policy and leads policy makers to prefer a low but positive inflation rate

Objectives 1–3 are MOD-3.A and MOD-3.B. Objective 4 is disinflation, defined in Topic 2.4 and already taught in Module 14. Objective 5 has no anchor in the framework.

The essential knowledge statements, verbatim (CED pp. 93–94):

MOD-3.A.1 — The short-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment can be illustrated by the downward-sloping short-run Phillips curve (SRPC). MOD-3.A.2 — An economy is always operating somewhere along the SRPC. MOD-3.A.3 — The long-run relationship between inflation and unemployment can be illustrated by the long-run Phillips curve (LRPC), which is vertical at the natural rate of unemployment. MOD-3.A.4 — Long-run equilibrium corresponds to the intersection of the SRPC and the LRPC. MOD-3.A.5 — Points to the left of long-run equilibrium represent inflationary gaps, while points to the right of long-run equilibrium represent recessionary gaps. MOD-3.B.1 — Demand shocks correspond to movement along the SRPC. MOD-3.B.2 — Supply shocks correspond to shifts of the SRPC. MOD-3.B.3 — Factors that cause the natural rate of unemployment to change will cause the LRPC to shift.

Three features of that wording matter.

The framework's Phillips curve is the AD–AS model redrawn. MOD-3.A.5 borrows the AD–AS vocabulary of gaps; MOD-3.B.1 and MOD-3.B.2 sort shocks exactly as AD and SRAS shifts are sorted; and the Unit 5 opener's Sample Instructional Activity 1 (CED p. 91) is to "draw the connection between shifts of AD with movement along the short-run Phillips curve" and "between shifts in the short-run aggregate supply (SRAS) curve and shifts of the short-run Phillips curve." MOD-2.D.1 (p. 62) states the bridge from the other side: moving along SRAS, "there is a short-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment. [See EK MOD-3.A.1]". Krugman builds the curve from data and wage-setting instead, and the AD–AS link appears only in the back-of-book solution to Check Your Understanding 1 (p. S-20).

Expected inflation, the book's central mechanism, is not in the eight statements. Expected inflation occurs 38 times in the module and 0 times on CED pp. 93–94; inflationary expectations occurs on p. 93 only in the title of a listed classroom resource. The framework reaches it two ways: MOD-2.C.3 (p. 62) — "a change in inflationary expectations, will cause the SRAS curve to shift" — which by Activity 1's correspondence shifts the SRPC; and the CED's own Graph and Switch activity for this graph (Instructional Approaches, p. 118), which changes the situation by "an increase in expected inflation." Teach it as examinable.

The natural rate, not the NAIRU. MOD-3.A.3 puts the long-run curve at "the natural rate of unemployment" (21 times in the CED). Krugman puts it at the NAIRU and only on p. 337 says the two are the same thing. NAIRU and nonaccelerating are 0 times each in the CED.

5. Corrections to make explicitly in class

Say natural rate, not NAIRU

The book writes NAIRU 18 times; the exam writes natural rate of unemployment and never NAIRU. Accept NAIRU as a synonym in discussion and require natural rate on a graph label.

The FRQ 1 rubric says SRAS′; it means SRPC′

The seventh printed point (p. 342) reads "New SRPC is labeled, for example as 'SRAS''". The graph beside it is labeled SRPC′. A student who labels a Phillips curve SRAS loses the point on the exam.

The gaps sit on opposite sides of the two graphs

On the AD–AS graph an inflationary gap is to the right of potential output; on the Phillips curve it is to the left of the natural rate, because more output means less unemployment. Students who carry "inflationary is right" across lose MOD-3.A.5 items.

A supply shock can give a recessionary gap with higher inflation

A negative supply shock moves the economy up and to the right: inflation rises and unemployment rises — stagflation. The point is to the right of long-run equilibrium, so it is a recessionary gap, whatever inflation does.

Movement along against shift

The Unit 5 opener (CED p. 89) names it as the commonest Phillips-curve error: "the difference between the movement along the curve versus shifts of the curve." Demand — spending, fiscal and monetary policy — moves the economy along the SRPC. Supply shocks and expectations shift it. The natural rate shifts the LRPC.

The axes are rates, not levels

Inflation rate against unemployment rate. MCQ 2's distractors are price level and output — the AD–AS axes. A Phillips curve drawn with price level on the vertical axis earns nothing.
