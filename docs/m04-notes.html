Krugman's three stated objectives for the module (p. 23):

  1. How trade leads to gains for an individual or an economy
  2. The difference between absolute advantage and comparative advantage
  3. How comparative advantage leads to gains from trade in the global marketplace

This is the best-aligned module in Section 1. All three objectives map onto MKT-1, every knowledge element is covered, and both required data formats — PPCs and tables — appear. The gaps are two missing vocabulary items and one claim the text shows but never states. See sections 3 and 5.

The enduring understanding is worth reading twice, because it is not the Micro one. Quoted verbatim (CED p. 36):

MKT-1 — Production and consumption increase by engaging in trade.

Micro's MKT-2 says instead that "the consequences of scarcity can be mitigated through specialization in production and by exchange". Micro frames trade as a response to scarcity; Macro frames it as a source of more output and more consumption. Same model, same four essential knowledge statements, different reason for teaching it — and the Macro framing is the one that points forward, to economic growth in Unit 5 and to net exports in Unit 6.

Note the skill shift. Topic 1.2 was graph-drawing (Skill 4.A); Topic 1.3 is quantitative — MKT-1.A.b says determine using data from PPCs or tables, and MKT-1.B.b says calculate mutually beneficial terms of trade. Plan for arithmetic, not just diagrams.

CED-listed resources for this topic (p. 36): Davidson Next AP Macroeconomics Course—Comparative Advantage & Trade; Classroom Resources International Economics and the AP Macroeconomics Course. The Macro CED lists two where the Micro CED lists three — it drops The Basics of Absolute and Comparative Advantage.

The knowledge elements, in the CED's own words

Learning objective MKT-1.A (CED p. 37):

a. Define absolute advantage and comparative advantage. b. Determine (using data from PPCs or tables as appropriate) absolute and comparative advantage.
  • MKT-1.A.1 — Absolute advantage describes a situation in which an individual, business, or

country can produce more of a good or service than any other producer with the same quantity of resources.

  • MKT-1.A.2 — Comparative advantage describes a situation in which an individual, business, or

country can produce a good or service at a lower opportunity cost than another producer.

Learning objective MKT-1.B (CED p. 37):

a. Explain (using data from PPCs or tables as appropriate) how specialization according to comparative advantage with appropriate terms of trade can lead to gains from trade. b. Calculate (using data from PPCs or tables as appropriate) mutually beneficial terms of trade.
  • MKT-1.B.1 — Production specialization according to comparative advantage results in

exchange opportunities that lead to consumption opportunities beyond the PPC. (Two words of the Micro version are missing here: the 2022 Micro MKT-2.B.1 inserts "not absolute advantage" after "comparative advantage", and says "consumption possibilities". Quote the Macro wording; teach the Micro clause anyway — section 5.)

  • MKT-1.B.2 — Comparative advantage and opportunity costs determine the terms of trade for

exchange under which mutually beneficial trade can occur.

This is the one place where the Micro and Macro editions of Module 4 genuinely diverge, and it is worth being precise about it.

In AP Microeconomics, the country-level pages at the end of this module are a preview. Topic 2.9 International Trade and Public Policy (POL-1.B.1, Micro CED p. 56) returns to trade with a world price, tariffs, quotas and a full surplus analysis, so the Micro teaching notes tell you to flag Figure 4.3 as unfinished business.

AP Macroeconomics has no such topic. Counted in the Macro CED:

TermOccurrences in the Macro CED

Both tariff and quota occur in a single essential-knowledge statement — MKT-5.E.1, Topic 6.4, Effect of Changes in Policies and Economic Conditions on the Foreign Exchange Market — where they are named only as examples of things that shift the supply of a currency. There is no welfare analysis of trade anywhere in AP Macroeconomics.

Comparative advantage itself appears 16 times in the Macro CED: 6 of them on the Topic 1.3 page, the rest in the course-at-a-glance, the labeling-system illustration, the back-matter restatement of the unit, and one Instructional Approaches example (p. 121 — see section 7). No other topic's required course content mentions it.

Macro's trade unit — Unit 6, Open Economy: International Trade and Finance, 10–13% of the exam and ~5–7 class periods — is about the balance of payments, exchange rates, the foreign exchange market and net exports. It never asks who holds the comparative advantage.

So teach Figure 4.3 as the end of this thread rather than the start of one. The pay-off is real but it is conceptual: a student who understands why a country with an absolute disadvantage in everything still exports something will find Unit 6's net-export logic easier. Nothing on the exam will ask them to combine the two.

3. Two required terms this module never names

Unlike Modules 1 and 3, nothing here is missing conceptually. Both gaps are vocabulary — Krugman teaches the idea thoroughly and simply does not attach the CED's word to it.

"Terms of trade"

The phrase does not appear anywhere in the book — zero occurrences across all 927 pages. Yet MKT-1.B.2 names it, MKT-1.B.a requires explaining specialization "with appropriate terms of trade," and MKT-1.B.b requires students to calculate mutually beneficial terms of trade.

Krugman teaches exactly this on pp. 26–27, at length and well: Hank will not give up more than 2 coconuts per fish, Tom will not give up more than 4/3 fish per coconut, so the deal must sit between the two opportunity costs. He just never labels it.

Supply the term and make the range explicit. Using Table 4.1:

Tom's opportunity costHank's opportunity costMutually beneficial terms of trade
1 fish costs3/4 coconut2 coconutsbetween 3/4 and 2 coconuts per fish
1 coconut costs4/3 fish1/2 fishbetween 1/2 and 4/3 fish per coconut

The deal in Table 4.2 — 10 fish for 10 coconuts — is 1 coconut per fish, inside the range. So is the 1 fish for 1½ coconuts in Check Your Understanding 2. The 15-coconuts-for-5-fish deal Krugman rules out is 3 coconuts per fish, outside it. Teach students to write the range first, then test any proposed deal against it. That is precisely what MKT-1.B.b asks for.

"Consumption opportunities beyond the PPC"

MKT-1.B.1 states that specialization leads to consumption opportunities beyond the PPC. (Micro's MKT-2.B.1 says possibilities; the Macro wording is opportunities. Nothing turns on the difference except that a quotation has to be right.) Krugman's Figure 4.2 shows this, but the text and caption say only that each castaway "can consume more of both goods than either could without trade" — a weaker claim. A student can pass the module believing trade means more than before rather than outside your own production possibilities.

Prove it numerically from Figure 4.2:

PPCConsumption with tradeMax on own PPC at that fish quantityBeyond the PPC?
Tom40 fish or 30 coconuts, slope −3/430 fish, 10 coconuts30 − ¾(30) = 7.5 coconutsYes — 10 > 7.5
Hank10 fish or 20 coconuts, slope −210 fish, 10 coconuts20 − 2(10) = 0 coconutsYes — 10 > 0

Have students plot both consumption points on the PPCs and see them sit outside. It takes five minutes and it converts a vague claim into the exact statement the CED requires.

FRQ 1 is the most valuable single item in Section 1. Its 9-point rubric walks the full chain: compute opportunity cost from intercepts (Country A ¼ computer per bushel, Country B 1¼), identify absolute advantage and justify it by output, identify comparative advantage and justify it by opportunity cost, name the import, then state the minimum acceptable export price and explain why. That last part is MKT-1.B.b, and the rubric's reasoning — "Country A's opportunity cost of producing corn is ¼ computers, so that is the lowest price they will accept" — is exactly the boundary logic to drill.

Between FRQ 1 (graph) and FRQ 2 (table), this module covers both data formats the CED names. That fills the gap left by Module 3, which offers no table-based work at all.

5. Corrections and teaching points

The input/output trap — the biggest failure point in this topic

The two review items present data in opposite directions, and students who memorize a procedure rather than the definition will get one of them backwards:

ItemData formatReading
Check Your Understanding 1Italy: an automobile takes 8 workers/day; a washing machine takes 3 workers/dayInput per unit — lower is better
FRQ 2Country A: 15 bushels of wheat per worker per week; 60 units of textilesOutput per worker — higher is better

Absolute advantage flips direction between the two. Teach students to ask, before any arithmetic: "Is this table output-per-input, or input-per-output?" Then compute opportunity cost as a ratio within one producer's column and compare across producers — never compare raw numbers across producers to get comparative advantage.

Worth stating explicitly: the two are reciprocals. If Country A makes 15 bushels per worker, that is 1/15 of a worker per bushel.

Absolute advantage is not comparative advantage — and here the Macro CED does not say so

This is the classic student error in the topic, and the two frameworks treat it differently.

The 2022 Micro MKT-2.B.1 writes the correction into the required knowledge itself: "according to comparative advantage, not absolute advantage". The 2020 Macro MKT-1.B.1 does not — the clause is simply absent, and the sentence otherwise matches word for word. Since the Micro CED is the later revision of shared text, read the omission as an artefact of vintage, not as a decision that Macro students may confuse the two.

The practical consequence for you is small but real: you cannot cite the framework for the correction in this course, so cite the textbook instead. Krugman makes the point five times, and here he is unambiguously stronger than the CED text:

  • p. 27 — "comparative, not absolute, advantage is the basis for mutual gain"
  • p. 27 — "as long as people have different opportunity costs, everyone has a comparative advantage

in something, and everyone has a comparative disadvantage in something"

  • Figure 4.2 caption, p. 26 — Hank "has an absolute disadvantage in both goods but a comparative

advantage in coconuts"

  • The margin definition itself ends: "Having an absolute advantage is not the same thing as having a

comparative advantage"

  • The FYI on p. 27 makes it concrete: Bangladesh is at an absolute disadvantage in almost everything

and still has a comparative advantage in clothing

Use all five. This is the module's strongest pedagogy and it is exactly what the exam targets.

Read comparative advantage off the slope

Figure 4.3 makes the point in one line: the flatter PPC belongs to the country with the comparative advantage in the good on the horizontal axis. Tie this back to Module 3, p. 19 — the slope of a straight-line PPC is the opportunity cost of the horizontal-axis good. Students who learned the slope there get comparative advantage from a graph almost for free.

One caution on the Adam Smith pin factory

The pin-factory passage (p. 23) illustrates division of labor within a firm — gains from specialization through practice and task-splitting. Comparative advantage is a different mechanism: gains from differing opportunity costs, which exist even with no learning-by-doing at all. Both are real, but only the second is what MKT-1 tests. Use the pin factory as motivation, then move to Tom and Hank for the actual model.

Teaching order — what depends on what

Comparative advantage is the most tightly ordered topic in Unit 1: nearly everything here is a prerequisite for something else, and skipping a step leaves students computing ratios they cannot interpret.

Teachbeforebecause
Opportunity cost as a ratioComparative advantagecomparative advantage is the lower opportunity cost, so the ratio has to be fluent first
Output per worker / input per unitAbsolute advantagewhich direction the table runs decides whether a bigger number is better
Absolute advantageComparative advantagethe contrast is the whole lesson, and it only works if the weaker idea arrives first
Comparative advantageSpecialization and gains from tradewho makes what is decided by the ratio
Comparative advantageTerms of tradethe two opportunity costs are the bounds of the range
Specialization and gains from tradeConsumption opportunities beyond the PPCthe bundle consumed comes out of the trade, so the trade has to exist first

Teach as a pair, never singly. Each of these is defined against the other:

  • Absolute advantage / comparative advantage — more output against lower opportunity cost. The

Macro EK omits the "not absolute advantage" clause, which makes teaching the pair together more important here, not less

  • Comparative advantage / comparative disadvantage — two sides of one ratio; everyone has both
  • Self-sufficiency / gains from trade — the baseline and the improvement on it
  • Production / consumption — the distinction that makes "beyond the PPC" coherent rather than

self-contradictory; this is what test question 18 turns on

  • Output per worker / input per unit — reciprocals, and the largest arithmetic trap in the

topic
