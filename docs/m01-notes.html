This is a revision of the Microeconomics notes, not a copy

A Microeconomics version of these notes exists at AP Microeconomics/Module1/. Do not use it for a Macro class. Module 1 is shared reading, but the two frameworks treat it very differently, and several of the Micro findings reverse in Macro. The differences are set out in section 5; the short version is that most of this module is background in a Macro course.

Krugman's five stated objectives for the module (p. 2):

  1. How scarcity and choice are central to the study of economics
  2. The importance of opportunity cost in individual choice and decision making
  3. The difference between positive economics and normative economics
  4. When economists agree and why they sometimes disagree
  5. What makes macroeconomics different from microeconomics

Objectives 1 and 2 are examinable. Objectives 3 and 4 are not. Objective 5 has no essential knowledge statement either — but in this course it is the boundary of the subject, so it is worth the ten minutes it was not worth in Micro. See section 3.

Verdict on alignment

Topic 1.1 is one sentence long. MOD-1.A.1 — "Individuals and societies are forced to make choices because most resources are scarce" — is the entire required content of the topic, and Module 1 delivers it comfortably, along with the opportunity-cost half of Topic 1.2.

Everything else in the module is background. Of the seventeen concepts in student_module_01.html, five are examinable in Macro and eleven have no anchor anywhere in the Macro framework — including the entire economic-systems sequence and all of marginal analysis. In the Microeconomics course those were core content.

So: teach it in half a period, do not test most of it, and move to Module 2. The danger here is the opposite of Module 2's. Module 2 under-delivers what the unit needs; Module 1 over-delivers what this course does not ask for, and a teacher coming from AP Micro will spend two periods on it out of habit.

The requirements, in the CED's own words

MOD-1.A.1 — "Individuals and societies are forced to make choices because most resources are scarce." (CED p. 34)
Learning objective MOD-1.A — "Define scarcity and economic resources." (CED p. 34)
MOD-1.B.2 — "The PPC can be used to illustrate the concepts of scarcity, opportunity cost, efficiency, underutilized resources, and economic growth or contraction." (CED p. 35)
Learning objective MOD-1.B(c) — "Calculate (using data from PPCs or tables as appropriate) opportunity cost." (CED p. 35)

Two things to notice in that wording.

"Most resources are scarce" — not all. The same qualification the Micro framework makes explicit (MKT-1.A.2 names established knowledge as non-rival) is compressed into the single word most here. Krugman never makes the point at all. Say it: some resources are not scarce, because using them does not use them up.

Macro never lists the factors of production. MOD-1.A requires students to define economic resources; no Macro essential knowledge statement names land, labour and capital, and entrepreneurship appears 0 times in the Macro CED. Teach the standard four-way list — it is correct economics and every prep book uses it — but do not build a marking scheme on the fourth item.

Opportunity cost is examinable here as a calculation. MOD-1.B(c) requires it, and Module 3 is where students meet the graph. Module 1 supplies the idea; do not skip it because the PPC has not arrived yet.

That is a long way from Krugman's pollution example. No Macro statement defines incentives or asks a student to reason about them in general. Teach the idea in a sentence, flag that its only examinable appearance is in supply-side policy in Unit 5, and move on.

Two of these deserve more than a line, because "no anchor" understates their importance in a Macro course.

Micro versus macro is the course boundary. In the Microeconomics notes this was context only and was worth ten minutes for honesty. Here it tells students what the next thirty modules are about. Table 1.1 on p. 5 and MCQ 5 in the AP Review both do this well, and MCQ 5's wrong answers — government employment policy, the economy's employment level, the overall price level — are a preview of Unit 2.

Models are not examinable content and they are the whole method. Big Idea 3 is Macroeconomic Models: "simplified representations that depict basic economic relationships and can be used to predict and explain how those relationships are affected by economic shocks" (CED p. 17). Units 3 to 6 are almost entirely models — AD–AS, the money market, loanable funds, the Phillips curve, the foreign exchange market. A student who expects a model to be realistic will argue with every one of them. Ten minutes here saves an argument later, even though no question will ever ask what a model is.

5. Corrections to make explicitly in class

These are the points where the Macro framework and the Micro habit diverge. Three of the five reverse a correction the Microeconomics notes ask you to make.

1. "Mixed economy" is no longer the star finding — it is not in this course at all. In AP Microeconomics, MKT-1.B.2 names command, market and mixed economies, the phrase appears nowhere in Krugman, and the Micro notes tell you to supply the definition because it is examinable. In AP Macroeconomics, mixed economy, market economy and command economy each occur 0 times in the framework. Teach the vocabulary if you like; do not spend a period on it and do not write it into a test. If you teach both courses, this is the correction most likely to catch you out.

2. "Marginal" means something else in this course. Marginal benefit, marginal cost and marginal analysis have no Macro anchor. The word marginal appears 12 times in the Macro CED and every one is the marginal propensity to consume or save (MOD-2.B.2, Unit 3), against 186 occurrences in the Micro CED. A student who has learned "marginal means one more" will meet marginal propensity to consume in Unit 3 and needs to be told it is the same word doing a narrower job. Teach MB and MC as general reasoning tools — they are — and say plainly that they are not assessed here.

3. Entrepreneurship, again, but harder. Krugman teaches four factors of production and its own FRQ rubric awards a point for entrepreneurship. The Micro framework names three. The Macro framework names none of them explicitly and never uses the word entrepreneurship. Teach the four-way list; do not let a student believe a fourth item earns a mark.

4. Say "most resources are scarce", not "all". MOD-1.A.1 says most. Krugman does not make the point and it is the half-sentence students need in order to answer a question about non-rival resources such as established knowledge.

5. Micro versus macro is now worth teaching. The reversal in the other direction. Ten minutes, Table 1.1, and MCQ 5.

Teaching order — what depends on what

Seven of the seventeen glossary terms cannot be taught in any order you like. Each row below means the left term has to be in place before the right one makes sense.

Teachbeforebecause
ScarcityOpportunity costwith nothing scarce there is no cost to speak of
Individual choiceOpportunity costthe cost is a forgone alternative, so an alternative has to have been chosen against
Opportunity costMarginal analysisthe rule compares costs, and this is what a cost is
Marginal benefit / marginal costMarginal analysismarginal analysis is nothing but the rule for comparing the two
Property rightsIncentivesownership is what makes a reward stick to the person who earned it
Microeconomics and macroeconomicsEconomic aggregatesan aggregate is precisely what makes macro macro
Economic modelPositive and normative economicsmodels are the tool for answering the positive half

Only the first two rows fall inside the twenty-five minutes above. Rows 6 and 7 begin inside it — you introduce micro/macro and models — and finish in the background reading. Rows 3, 4 and 5 are entirely background here.

Teach as a pair, never singly. Each of these terms is defined by its contrast, and a student who meets one without the other will blur them in Unit 2:

  • Economics / economy — the study against the thing being studied
  • Opportunity cost / marginal cost — the whole sacrifice against the cost of one more
  • Market / command / mixed economy — a triad, and the outer two are limiting cases of the middle

one. Worth remembering that mixed economy appears 0 times in the Macro CED, so this triad is background reading in this course even though the Micro course requires it.
