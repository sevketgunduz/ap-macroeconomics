1. Over a year, gasoline prices rise 20%, electronics prices fall 10%, rents rise 4% and food prices do not change.

What can you conclude about the aggregate price level?

  • (A) It rose 14%.
  • (B) It rose 3.5%.
  • (C) Nothing yet: it depends on how much of each is bought.
  • (D) It fell, because electronics got cheaper.
  • (E) It rose 20%, the largest change.

What does the basket cost?

  • (A) $150
  • (B) $33
  • (C) $16
  • (D) $528
  • (E) $120

3. This year a country's consumer price index is 125.

Which statement is correct?

  • (A) The market basket cost $125 this year.
  • (B) Prices have risen 125% since the base year.
  • (C) Prices rose 25% this year.
  • (D) The market basket costs 25% more than it did in the base year.
  • (E) The market basket cost $100 in the base year.

What is the price index for 2020?

  • (A) 36
  • (B) 115

  • (E) 15

What is the price index for the next year, using the base-year basket?

  • (B) 116.1

  • (D) 120
  • (E) 115

Section A — solutions

Question 1 — Aggregate price level · Analysis

Answer: (C)

The aggregate price level is measured by the cost of a market basket, which weights each good by how much is bought. Four percentage changes alone cannot be combined. Krugman's opening (p. 142): some prices rose and some fell in 2008, and a price index was needed to say how fast prices overall were rising.

  • (A) ✗ Adds the four changes, as if each were the whole basket.
  • (B) ✗ A simple average gives every good the same weight, whatever is spent on it.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ One price falling does not settle the direction of the overall level.
  • (E) ✗ The largest change is not the average.

Question 2 — Market basket · Application

Answer: (A)

(4 × $10) + (10 × $8) + (2 × $15) = 40 + 80 + 30 = $150.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Adds the three prices and ignores the quantities.
  • (C) ✗ Adds the quantities and ignores the prices.
  • (D) ✗ Multiplies the total quantity by the total of the prices.
  • (E) ✗ Leaves out the books.

Question 3 — Base year · Analysis

Answer: (D)

An index of 125 means the basket costs 125 ÷ 100 = 1.25 times its base-year cost: 25% more than in the base year.

  • (A) ✗ The index is not a dollar cost.
  • (B) ✗ 125 is the level; the rise since the base year is 25%.
  • (C) ✗ The index compares with the base year, not with last year.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ The base year's index is 100 whatever the basket cost then.

Question 4 — Calculating a price index · Application

Answer: (B)

276 ÷ 240 × 100 = 115.

  • (A) ✗ The change in dollars, not an index.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Divides the base-year cost by this year's: 240 ÷ 276.
  • (D) ✗ The dollar cost, not the index.
  • (E) ✗ That is the percentage rise since the base year.

Question 5 — Calculating a price index · Synthesis

Answer: (E)

The index prices the base-year basket in both years. Base year: (10 × 20) + (20 × 10) = $400. Next year: (10 × 22) + (20 × 12) = $460. Index = 460 ÷ 400 × 100 = 115. The new quantities belong to a new basket; the fixed-basket index ignores them — which is exactly why substitution bias arises.

  • (A) ✗ Prices the new quantities in the new year — (8 × 22) + (25 × 12) = $476 — against the old basket's $400.
  • (B) ✗ Prices the new basket in both years: 476 ÷ 410. A different index, not the fixed-basket one.
  • (C) ✗ The shirt price change alone.
  • (D) ✗ The meal price change alone.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 6 — Consumer price index · Analysis

Answer: (A)

The CPI prices "the market basket of a typical urban American family" (p. 144) and so tracks the income that family needs to keep its standard of living as prices change.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ That is closer to the GDP deflator.
  • (C) ✗ That is the producer price index.
  • (D) ✗ The CPI prices a whole basket, weighted by spending.
  • (E) ✗ Spending changes with quantities; the CPI holds quantities fixed.

Question 7 — The inflation rate from a price index · Application

Answer: (C)

(168 − 160) ÷ 160 × 100 = 8 ÷ 160 × 100 = 5%.

  • (A) ✗ The change in index points.
  • (B) ✗ Divides by the later index: 8 ÷ 168.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ The index itself.
  • (E) ✗ 168 ÷ 160 × 100, the ratio.

Question 8 — The inflation rate from a price index · Analysis

Answer: (E)

X: 15 ÷ 150 × 100 = 10%. Y: 12 ÷ 100 × 100 = 12%. More points is not more inflation when the starting levels differ.

  • (A) ✗ Points, not percentages.
  • (B) ✗ The level of an index says nothing about its rate of change.
  • (C) ✗ 10% and 12% differ.
  • (D) ✗ Percentage changes are comparable whatever the base year.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 9 — Index, then inflation · Synthesis

Answer: (B)

Indexes: year 1 = 88 ÷ 80 × 100 = 110; year 2 = 99 ÷ 80 × 100 = 123.75. Inflation = (123.75 − 110) ÷ 110 × 100 = 12.5% — the same as (99 − 88) ÷ 88.

  • (A) ✗ The change in index points.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Year 2's rise since the base year, not since year 1.
  • (D) ✗ Divides by the later cost: 11 ÷ 99.
  • (E) ✗ The inflation rate from the base year to year 1.

Question 10 — Comparing dollar amounts across years · Application

Answer: (D)

$40 × 175 ÷ 125 = $56. The target year's index goes on top.

  • (A) ✗ The ratio upside down: 40 × 125 ÷ 175.
  • (B) ✗ Divides by 100 instead of by the original year's index.
  • (C) ✗ Adds the change in index points to the price.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Ignores the rise in prices.

Question 11 — Comparing dollar amounts across years · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

$35,000 × 210 ÷ 140 = $52,500 in today's dollars. The $50,000 offer buys less than the parent's salary did.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Compares nominal dollars from different years.
  • (C) ✗ The ratio upside down: 35,000 × 140 ÷ 210.
  • (D) ✗ The CPI rose by half, but $50,000 is only about 43% more than $35,000.
  • (E) ✗ The ratio of the two index values is all the conversion needs.

Question 12 — Substitution bias · Analysis

Answer: (C)

Families switch from the good whose price rose to a cheaper substitute; a fixed basket keeps pricing the beef. Krugman's hamburger case (p. 145): such an index "would overstate the true rise in the cost of living." The framework's name is substitution bias.

  • (A) ✗ Backwards: the switch lowers the true rise, so the CPI is too high.
  • (B) ✗ The basket's beef weight is now out of date.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Nothing about beef or chicken has changed in quality.
  • (E) ✗ Prices are collected monthly; the basket is the fixed part.

Question 13 — Substitution bias · Synthesis

Answer: (B)

Fixed basket: base cost (10 × 2) + (10 × 2) = $40; new cost (10 × 4) + (10 × 2.20) = $62; inflation = 22 ÷ 40 × 100 = 55%. The household's new bundle — 5 coffees and 15 teas — cost (5 × 2) + (15 × 2) = $40 at old prices and (5 × 4) + (15 × 2.20) = $53 at new ones: 32.5%. By switching towards tea, whose price rose least, the household faces less inflation than the fixed basket reports.

  • (A) ✗ 32.5% is the household's bundle, not the fixed basket; and the bias runs the other way.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ The household changed its basket precisely to avoid the full rise.
  • (D) ✗ Adds the two goods' percentage changes, 100% and 10%.
  • (E) ✗ The fixed basket shows 55%, not 32.5%.

Question 14 — New goods and quality changes · Analysis

Answer: (E)

The same price tag buys a much better laptop. Recording the whole 4% as inflation counts nothing for the improvement, so the CPI is too high.

  • (A) ✗ The price is measured accurately; its meaning is not.
  • (B) ✗ A higher price that buys more quality is not all inflation.
  • (C) ✗ Nobody switched goods.
  • (D) ✗ Better quality lowers the true price rise; it does not add to it.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 15 — New goods and quality changes · Analysis

Answer: (D)

Krugman (p. 145): innovation "makes a given amount of money worth more. That is, innovation is like a fall in consumer prices." An old basket sees neither the new product nor its falling price.

  • (A) ✗ Leaving out a cheapening product makes measured inflation too high, not too low.
  • (B) ✗ Its absence is exactly the problem.
  • (C) ✗ Substitution is switching between goods already in the basket.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Its price is falling.

Question 16 — The direction of all three biases · Analysis

Answer: (A)

The framework names substitution bias as "causing it to overstate"; new goods and quality improvements work the same way. Krugman: "many economists believe that the CPI somewhat overstates inflation" (p. 145).

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ The direction is reversed.
  • (C) ✗ Better quality at the same price is a hidden price fall; missing it overstates inflation.
  • (D) ✗ They point the same way and do not cancel.
  • (E) ✗ They are about the CPI's consumer basket.

Question 17 — The basket's weights decide the answer · Analysis

Answer: (B)

Her basket gives medical care, whose prices rose faster than average, a much bigger weight than the CPI's does, so her personal inflation rate is above 3%.

  • (A) ✗ The CPI's basket is not hers.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Her heaviest item rose faster than the CPI.
  • (D) ✗ Only 40% of her spending is medical; the rest rose by other amounts.
  • (E) ✗ Adds the two rates.

Question 18 — The basket's weights decide the answer · Synthesis

Answer: (C)

Basket A: (30 × 1) + (10 × 3) = $60 → (30 × 1.50) + (10 × 3.30) = $78, a rise of 30%. Basket B: (10 × 1) + (30 × 3) = $100 → (10 × 1.50) + (30 × 3.30) = $114, a rise of 14%.

  • (A) ✗ Same prices do not mean the same index when the weights differ.
  • (B) ✗ Those are the two goods' own price changes.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ The two answers swapped.
  • (E) ✗ A simple average ignores both baskets' weights.

Question 19 — GDP deflator · Application

Answer: (E)

600 ÷ 480 × 100 = 125.

  • (A) ✗ Real over nominal: 480 ÷ 600.
  • (B) ✗ Not a figure the data give.
  • (C) ✗ Forgets the × 100.
  • (D) ✗ The percentage by which prices exceed the base year, not the deflator.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 20 — GDP deflator · Analysis

Answer: (D)

Krugman (p. 146): "If nominal GDP doubles but real GDP does not change, the GDP deflator indicates that the aggregate price level doubled."

  • (A) ✗ The ratio of nominal to real has doubled.
  • (B) ✗ Output is real GDP, which is unchanged.
  • (C) ✗ The ratio upside down.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Nothing is wrong: all the rise was prices.

Question 21 — Inflation from two indexes · Synthesis

Answer: (B)

Deflator: (126 − 120) ÷ 120 × 100 = 5%. CPI: (249.6 − 240) ÷ 240 × 100 = 4%. The CPI rose more points but less in percentage terms, because it started higher.

  • (A) ✗ Both are changes in index points.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ The two answers swapped.
  • (D) ✗ The deflator is right; the CPI figure is points.
  • (E) ✗ The CPI is right; the deflator figure is points.

Question 22 — CPI against GDP deflator · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Imported cars are in consumers' basket, so the CPI rises. The GDP deflator covers goods produced in the country, so the price of an import does not enter it directly.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ The two indexes cover different goods.
  • (C) ✗ The reverse of the truth.
  • (D) ✗ The CPI basket includes cars, imported ones too.
  • (E) ✗ Cars bought by households are consumer goods, and imports are not in GDP.

Section B — solutions

Question 23 — Producer price index · Analysis

Answer: (E)

The PPI prices "raw commodities such as steel, electricity, coal" bought by producers, and "is often regarded as an 'early warning signal' of changes in the inflation rate" (pp. 145–146).

  • (A) ✗ Consumers buy goods made from steel, not steel itself; their prices follow later.
  • (B) ✗ The deflator responds only as the higher costs reach final goods.
  • (C) ✗ Raw materials are exactly what the PPI prices.
  • (D) ✗ Producer prices respond first.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 24 — Indexing to the CPI · Analysis

Answer: (C)

The pension rises with the CPI each year. If the CPI rises 1 point more than the cost of living, the pension rises 1 point more than the pensioner needs: its real value grows about 1% a year.

  • (A) ✗ The direction is reversed.
  • (B) ✗ The overstatement feeds straight into the payment.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ It keeps rising with the CPI.
  • (E) ✗ Indexing is what protects it from unexpected inflation.

Notes for the teacher

Content validity. All 13 concept entries appear across the 24 items. The two background entries — the producer price index and indexing — are tested once each in Section B; the PPI item asks only what it measures, since the CED excludes calculating it. Questions 10–14 and 16 test material the textbook does not contain or does not name: the conversion of dollars between years (done once in words, p. 144), the name substitution bias (0 times in the book) and quality changes (0 times in the module).

No published item is reproduced. None of the CED's sample multiple-choice questions is tagged with this module's learning objectives, and sample FRQ 2(a)(ii) is used in Module 11.

Bloom levels. Application 5 · Analysis 13 · Synthesis 6, a ratio of 1 : 2.6 : 1.2 against the standard's 1 : 3 : 1.75. The suggested skill for Topic 2.4 is 2.C — interpret an outcome using quantitative data — so twelve items calculate, and the synthesis items chain two procedures: an index then an inflation rate (9), a conversion then a comparison (11), a fixed basket against a changed one (5, 13), two baskets (18), two indexes (21).

Distractor design. Every wrong option encodes a documented error. The recurring families:
