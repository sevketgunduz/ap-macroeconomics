The headline. This module is well aligned. Both learning objectives it serves — MEA-1.C (the two rates) and MEA-1.D (the limitations) — are taught, with the formulas printed and a worked review. The one thing missing is the middle clause of MEA-1.C: "Explain how changes in employment and the labor market affect the unemployment rate and the labor force participation rate." The textbook defines both rates and never moves a single person between categories to see what happens to them. That is where the counter-intuitive exam items live — a discouraged worker lowers the unemployment rate — so section 3 supplies it. And the CED's own sample question 4 (p. 135) is a Module 12 calculation; the test reproduces it.

Krugman's four stated objectives (p. 118):

  1. How unemployment is measured
  2. How the unemployment rate is calculated
  3. The significance of the unemployment rate for the economy
  4. The relationship between the unemployment rate and economic growth

The first three are MEA-1.C and MEA-1.D. The fourth is Topic 2.7's business cycle, arriving early — see section 2.

The essential knowledge statements, verbatim (CED p. 48):

MEA-1.C.1 — The unemployment rate is the percentage of the labor force that is out of work. MEA-1.C.2 — The labor force participation rate is another measure of the labor market activity in an economy. The labor force participation rate is the percentage of the adult population that is in the labor force. MEA-1.D.1 — The measured unemployment rate is often criticized for understating the level of joblessness because it excludes groups such as discouraged workers and part-time workers.

Two features of that wording matter more than they look.

The framework never defines who is unemployed. Unemployed occurs 0 times in the Macro CED; MEA-1.C.1 says "out of work", which a retiree also is. The operational test — no job, actively looking in the past four weeks, available — is Krugman's (p. 119). Yet the suggested skill for the topic is 1.B, identifying a concept from an example, which on this topic means classifying people. Teach Krugman's test as the definition and the CED's phrase as the shorthand.

"Part-time workers" are not excluded from the unemployment rate. They are counted as employed — Krugman p. 119, "either full time or part time". What MEA-1.D.1 means is that the rate cannot see the part-timers who want full-time work, because it scores them as employed. Section 5 says how to put that to students.

The calculation, and the CED's own item

Two formulas, and the review's FRQ 1 is the model answer (p. 125, 6 printed points):

GivenLabor forceParticipation rateUnemployment rate
Adult population 12 m, employed 5 m, unemployed 1 m5 + 1 = 6 m6 ÷ 12 × 100 = 50%1 ÷ 6 × 100 = 16.7%

The printed rubric rounds the last to 17%. Accept either; tell students to show the fraction.

CED sample multiple-choice question 4 (Exam Information p. 135):

The population of Country X is 250,000, and the labor force is 200,000 people. If 175,000 people are employed, what is the unemployment rate? (A) 10% (B) 12.5% (C) 20% (D) 80% (E) 87.5%

The published key (p. 140) gives B, Skill 1.C, Learning Objective MEA-1.C, Unit 2. Unemployed = 200,000 − 175,000 = 25,000; 25,000 ÷ 200,000 = 12.5%. Every distractor is a real error: 10% divides the unemployed by the population; 20% is the share of the population not in the labor force; 80% is the participation rate; 87.5% is the employed share of the labor force. The item's whole difficulty is choosing the denominator. It is question 7 of the test.

The four rows students get wrong are the last four. A discouraged worker lowers the unemployment rate, which is the whole of MEA-1.D.1 in one line. An improving economy can raise it, as discouraged workers come back — Krugman's CYU 1. A retirement raises it by shrinking the denominator, with nobody losing a job. And involuntary part-time work changes neither rate, which is what the CED means by "excludes … part-time workers".

Say plainly what is not missing. Every definition, both formulas and every limitation the CED names are in the module, with a printed rubric for the calculation.

5. Corrections to make explicitly in class

Part-time workers are employed

MEA-1.D.1 says the rate "excludes groups such as discouraged workers and part-time workers". Read literally, that is wrong about part-timers: they are in the labor force and counted as employed (p. 119; MCQ 3 depends on it). The criticism is that someone working ten hours who wants forty is scored exactly like someone working forty. Put it to students as: the unemployment rate excludes the underemployment of part-time workers, not the workers.

"Out of work" is not the test

The CED's shorthand, "the percentage of the labor force that is out of work", is fine inside the labor force. Applied to a person it misleads: a retiree is out of work and not unemployed. The test is three conditions — no job, looked in the past four weeks, available — and the four weeks is what separates an unemployed person from a discouraged one.

Two of the book's own solutions do not follow its definitions

  • FRQ 2(a), Julie — an engineer mowing lawns full time. The back-of-book answer is *"Employed

(underemployed)". But Krugman's definition of the underemployed (p. 120) is "people who work part time because they cannot find full-time jobs"*. Julie is employed; in everyday speech she is overqualified, but by the textbook's definition and the official measures she is not underemployed. Mark "employed" correct, and use the item to fix the definition.

  • CYU 2(e), Natasha — a graduate student who went back to school because jobs were scarce. The

solution calls her "marginally attached". That definition requires someone who would like a job and has looked in the recent past; the stem says neither. Not in the labor force is the defensible answer. Marginally attached is background (entry 11), so this costs nothing on the exam — but students who learn the solution learn the wrong definition.

Fewer people unemployed is not always good news

A falling rate can mean people found jobs or people gave up. A rising rate can mean people lost jobs or people started looking. Always ask which category they moved between. The participation rate tells you which: it falls when people give up and rises when they start looking.

The denominator is the labor force

The CED's sample question is built on this, and so is most of the test. The unemployment rate divides by the labor force; the participation rate divides by the adult population. Mixing them produces exactly the distractors of sample question 4.

Do not carry the Micro correction across

In AP Microeconomics, Krugman's unemployment had to be retrained to the CED's underutilized resources. Here it is the framework's own topic. Students taking both courses should be told so explicitly.
