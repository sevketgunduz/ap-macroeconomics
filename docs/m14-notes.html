The headline. This module's weight sits in the wrong place for the exam. Topic 2.5, Costs of Inflation, has one essential knowledge statement — unexpected inflation "arbitrarily redistributes wealth … such as lenders to borrowers" (MEA-1.H.1) — while Krugman spends two and a half of his eight pages on shoe-leather, menu and unit-of-account costs, which occur 0 times in the Macro CED. Teach the winners and losers (pp. 138–139) and the definitions Topic 2.4 needs — the inflation rate, deflation, disinflation, real variables — as the core, and the three named costs as background. Two things the framework asks for are missing: who else is hurt besides lenders (the CED's own activity names individuals living on fixed incomes), and any calculation of a change in a real variable.

Krugman's five stated objectives (p. 134):

  1. The economic costs of inflation
  2. How inflation creates winners and losers
  3. Why policy makers try to maintain a stable rate of inflation
  4. The difference between real and nominal values of income, wages, and interest rates
  5. The problems of deflation and disinflation

Objective 2 is MEA-1.H. Objective 4 is MEA-1.F.4 and, for interest rates, Topic 4.2. Objective 5 is the definitions of MEA-1.F a. Objectives 1 and 3 — the named costs and the case for stable inflation — are background: the framework's costs of inflation are the redistribution in objective 2.

The essential knowledge statements, verbatim.

MEA-1.H.1 (CED p. 51) — Unexpected inflation arbitrarily redistributes wealth from one group of individuals to another group, such as lenders to borrowers. MEA-1.F.3 (CED p. 50) — The inflation rate is determined by calculating the percentage change in a price index, such as CPI or the GDP deflator. MEA-1.F.4 (CED p. 50) — Real variables, such as real wages, are the nominal variables deflated by the price level. MEA-3.B.1 (CED p. 77) — A nominal interest rate is the rate of interest paid for a loan, unadjusted for inflation. MEA-3.B.3 (CED p. 77) — A real interest rate can be calculated in hindsight by subtracting the actual inflation rate from the nominal interest rate.

Three features of that wording matter.

"Unexpected" is the operative word. Topic 2.5's only statement is about unexpected inflation, and the learning objective adds (deflation). Krugman's winners-and-losers passage is built the same way — "If the actual inflation rate is higher than expected, borrowers gain" (p. 138) — but he never uses the framework's verb: redistribut- occurs 0 times in the module.

"Such as lenders to borrowers" is an example, not the list. The CED's own Instructional Approaches page (p. 117) turns this topic into an Ask the Expert activity with stations for "lenders, borrowers, individuals living on fixed incomes, etc." Fixed income occurs 0 times in the module (once in the whole book, p. 629, in another context); saver 0 in the module. Krugman's only groups are borrowers and lenders, with mortgages as the example.

The named costs are not there. Shoe-leather, menu cost and unit-of-account each occur 0 times in the Macro CED; unit of account occurs 3 times, all as a function of money (MEA-3.C.2, p. 78). Hyperinflation is 0. And inflation itself is 0 in the Micro CED — this is a Macro-only module.

5. Corrections to make explicitly in class

Spend the period on redistribution, not on menu costs

The book's section headings make shoe-leather, menu and unit-of-account costs look like the module's content, and its review tests them three times. The framework never names them. When an AP question says costs of inflation, it means what MEA-1.H.1 says: unexpected inflation moves wealth from one group to another. Teach the three costs in five minutes as background.

Inflation does not make everyone poorer

Krugman's own opening (pp. 134–135): if all prices and all incomes rise together, real incomes do not change. The losses come from inflation that was not expected and from contracts fixed in dollars.

Only the surprise redistributes

Inflation that everyone expected is built into the nominal rate and the wage contract, and moves nothing — FRQ 2's lender would have charged more had he expected 5%. The same 5% inflation is harmless if expected and a transfer if not. Students who say "inflation hurts lenders" without unexpected have the rubric point wrong.

Disinflation is not deflation

Disinflation is the inflation rate falling — from 8% to 3%, prices still rising. Deflation is the price level falling — an inflation rate below zero. The CED defines both under MEA-1.F a, and the words are one prefix apart.

The real rate "in hindsight" uses actual inflation

MEA-3.B.3 says "in hindsight … the actual inflation rate". When a loan is signed, the parties work with expected inflation; when it is repaid, the real rate it actually earned uses actual inflation. Every redistribution question is the gap between the two.

FRQ 1(b): the printed answer is not the only defensible one

Lanwei's company reimburses her late, in dollars that have lost value. The rubric calls this a unit-of-account cost and a net cost to the economy, because she travels less. It is also a transfer from Lanwei to her company — a fixed nominal payment eroded by inflation, which is entry 15. Accept either argument if it is explained.

Do not carry the Micro silence across

Inflation occurs 0 times in the Micro CED. Students taking both courses have met real versus nominal only as relative prices; here the price level itself is the subject.

Teach as a pair, never singly.
